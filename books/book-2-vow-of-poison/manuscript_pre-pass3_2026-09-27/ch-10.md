The account's register is in the bottom drawer of a different cabinet, under a stack of annual filings signed by a partner who's been dead since before the war.

It's a ledger, heavy, bound in leather gone black and cracked at the spine. I open it on the desk.

Columns. Dates in calendars I can't all read. Transfers in and out: a shipping company, a holding firm, a dead woman's estate. And along the left side of every page, a name.

A different name, every generation. One name runs for decades, and then the handwriting changes and the name changes with it, and a new one runs for a while, and then another.

Some of them are in French. Some are in a script I don't recognize at all. They go back to the very front of the book, to pages so brown and brittle I'm afraid to turn them.

Every hand is different. The patience in them isn't.

I read people for a living. I read what they leave on the ground: how fast they were going, what they were carrying, whether they were afraid.

I read these names the same way, and every one of them was written by somebody who had never been in a hurry in their life. Every one of them sat down and signed the account as if they had all the time there was.
