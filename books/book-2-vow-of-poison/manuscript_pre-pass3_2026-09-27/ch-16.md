Delphine Thibodeaux brings me a glass of water before I leave.

She comes out of the kitchen door at the back of Fontenot's with it, a tall plastic tumbler full of ice, and hands it to me without being asked. She's Rémy's sister, sharp-faced and dark-eyed, and she smells of fryer oil and the lemon she's been cutting.

"You're driving out to the old Arceneaux place," she says.

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to. Everybody in there watched you ask." She tips her head back and sniffs the air, once, long, her nostrils flaring. "It's going to rain. About an hour. Maybe less."

The sky over the bayou is clear. Stars everywhere.

The old Arceneaux place is at the end of a shell road even worse than Fontenot's.

It's a burned-out house on blocks, black timbers and a collapsed tin roof, cypress knees standing up out of the water all around it like a crowd at a wake. There's a tin shed behind it that didn't burn. There's a stand of old live oaks behind the shed, and behind those, the woods.

I get out of the truck and read the ground.

Somebody comes here.

It isn't often, and it's never many. A single person, alone. The shell drive has a single track down the middle of it that isn't mine, a small tire, a scooter or a light bike, pushed in by hand from the road. The same track comes and goes. It goes up to the shed and stops. Then boots.

Small boots. A careful stride. Whoever wears them doesn't hurry, and doesn't stop to look around, and knows exactly where they're going.

I follow them around the shed.

There's a bucket behind it, half-buried in the weeds, tipped against the wall. I crouch down and look in.

Bulbs.

Dozens of them, papery and brown, the kind you plant in fall. Wild iris. And beside the bucket, lying in the dirt, a trowel with fresh black mud dried on the blade.

The boots go past the bucket. They go into the trees.

I stand at the edge of the woods and look after them. There's a path in there, narrow, one person wide, worn down to bare earth through the palmettos. It goes back into the dark under the oaks.

Somebody is planting iris in the Arceneaux woods. Somebody comes out here, alone, and puts bulbs in the ground, for a wolf who doesn't have a grave.

For whom, and what the fuck is back there?

I step onto the path.

The first drop of rain hits the back of my neck.

Then the sky opens up, all at once, a warm hard downpour out of a sky that was clear an hour ago, and the path in front of me turns to running mud in seconds, and every print on it washes away.

Fucking Delphine. Fucking weather-sniffing, lemon-cutting Delphine.

When the rain slows to a drizzle, I get out and walk back around the shed, one more time. I tell myself I'm checking the path. The path is gone, a sheet of black water under the oaks.

But there's something new on the shed.

It's nailed to the door at eye level. A cream envelope, heavy stock, the rain beading on it and running off without soaking in. Nailed through the top edge with two good nails, driven by somebody who wanted it to hold in weather.

It wasn't there before the rain. I walked right past this door. He waited until I was busy.

My name is on the front of it. *Mr. Roche.* In a patient hand, with a familiar slope on every capital.

I stand in the drizzle and look at it.

He was here. While I sat in the truck with my eyes shut and her voice in my ear, telling her what I'd do to her, he walked up this shell drive in his thin shoes and nailed this to a dead wolf's door, and I didn't hear him.

I pull the nails with my fingers. I take it down.

I don't open it.

I put it in my coat, in the inside pocket, against the card from the firm.

Then I drive home to the canal with it riding against my chest the whole way.
