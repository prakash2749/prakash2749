# I-1 · Cray

*Placement: after Ch. 5.*

I did not come early for her.

The witch was never in doubt. The witch has been promised since before she had teeth, and a promised thing keeps. I could have left the envelope under her mat and gone home to my tea.

The moon would have done the rest, and the paper would have been perfectly content.

I came early for him.

I have stood at the end of that canal road on more nights than I would like to confess to anyone with a sense of proportion. There is a live oak on the corner with a root that heaves the sidewalk, and if you stand behind it in a dark coat, the streetlamp does not find you. I found it the first week. I have been fond of it ever since.

From behind that oak, I have watched Silas Roche take the turn off the canal.

Every night. Every single night. In rain and in the wet heat and in the week of the crowning, when the whole court was drunk and he was the only sober man in the city.

He comes along the canal at the pace of a man with somewhere to be, and at the turn he slows, and he takes it, and he stops under the lamp at the witch's gate and he stands there.

He does not go in, most nights. He stands. He looks at her kitchen window. Then he goes on.

I have introduced a great many people to a great many things.

I know what a man looks like when he is standing at the door of the thing he wants and has not been introduced.

---

The instruction I was given names the witch.

Only the witch. It is a very short instruction, as they always are, written on good paper in a hand that has never once hurried. The Marchand name and every name her house holds, delivered at the new moon. Nothing more is asked of me. Nothing more is ever asked.

I could deliver exactly that, and be thanked, and go home.

I find I would rather not.

It is a great deal to know that a principal exists, and to serve, and to be permitted into the correspondence, and never once to be told the intention behind the letters.

One does not complain. I have never complained. But one notices, over a very long career, that the people who are only ever useful are the first to be set down when they stop.

Is that disloyal? I prefer to call it thrift. A man should have something in his pocket that belongs to nobody else.

A King's enforcer, freely given, would be a lovely thing to have in one's pocket.

---

He poured my wine into her gutter.

I watched him do it from the oak. He pulled the cork with his teeth and upended the bottle into the drain behind her kitchen, and the witch stood in the doorway and said something that made his jaw move.

Was I offended? Not in the least. A refused gift is simply a gift owed. It sits on the ledger between people like a door left ajar, and sooner or later one of them has to decide whether to close it.

So I sent for another bottle of the same, and I carried it up her walk myself while he was across the city reading my step on the firm's doorstone. I stood on the board that gives, until I was quite sure she was asleep and would not come to the door. I set the bottle where the first had stood, squared to the edge.

I did not knock. Knocking is for people who expect an answer.

---

Tonight I sat in my front room with the lamp low and chose the evening.

That is the whole of my art, really. Anyone can make an offer. The skill is in choosing the evening on which the offer will be heard as a kindness.

It will not be tomorrow. Tomorrow he is angry about the bottle, and an angry man hears a threat in everything.

It will not be the night he beds her, either. A man is very generous that night, and very stupid, and he would give me his name for the asking and hate me for it by morning. I have no use for a gift that curdles.

No. I will wait for the evening he finds out what he is, on paper, in a clerk's brown ink. The evening he learns who stood at the edge of the pen.

That evening, a man is very lonely. That evening, he will want to be told he was worth something once.

I will tell him.

I will set out another glass before he knocks, and open something good, and tell him the truth, which is always the most persuasive thing I own.

Then I will ask him for his name.

And he will think about it.
