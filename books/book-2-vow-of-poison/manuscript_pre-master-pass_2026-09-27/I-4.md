# I-4 · Cray

*Placement: after Ch. 23.*

The letter came under my door before dawn, and I knew the hand before I had it out of the envelope.

It is a very beautiful hand, and it has never hurried. I have kept every letter in it for longer than this house has stood.

I have never read one without my fingers going cold.

I read it standing, in the front hall, in my stockings.

It was short.

They always are.

*The jars were not asked for.*

That was all.

No signature.

There never is. Just the line, on good paper, folded once, with the mark pressed into the wax.

---

I made my tea. I sat in my chair by the window with the letter on my knee, and I read it again, and I considered it.

It was true.

The jars were not asked for.

The instruction names the witch and her house. Her name, and every name the house holds, delivered at the new moon.

Nothing was said about breaking anything before then. Nothing was said about the Aldric glass, which is now a scatter of green on a kitchen floor on the far side of the city, with a great many old promises loose in the air above it.

The Aldric house will write to the King. The King will write back. There will be a certain amount of noise.

The principal dislikes noise.

I chose the jars myself.

I sent my men in when the lamp went out, and I gave them a list, and I told them which glass to take and which to leave. I told them to fold the cloth before they touched the working. I wanted her to see that it was done by someone with manners.

And I wanted him to see it. Him most of all.

---

Was it a mistake?

I have sat with the question all morning, and I find I do not think so. A man does not give up his name because he is asked nicely. He gives it up because he has stood in the ruin of something he loves and understood that he cannot mend it by killing anyone.

He stood in that kitchen this morning. I am certain of it.

He read every shard. He read the order they broke in and the shelves they skipped, and he understood exactly what it meant, and he did not come to my door.

That is a man who is almost ready.

The principal does not see it that way. The principal sees noise, and a broker who chose without being asked.

I am not in the habit of being rebuked.

I find, sitting here with my tea going cold, that my fingers are not quite steady on the cup.

---

I have always been careful with the principal. I have been useful and prompt.

I have delivered what was asked, on the night it was asked, and I have never once asked to be told why. I have brought a great many names to that account, and been thanked often enough to know the difference between thanks and this.

This is not thanks.

This is the tone one uses to a dog that has brought home the wrong bird.

I read the line again. *The jars were not asked for.*

What happens to a broker who makes too much noise? I have never had to find out.

I have seen the column of names in the account's register, and I know that none of them is still signing. They ran for a while, each in turn, and then the handwriting changed.

I have never wanted to be among the names that change.

---

So I will be very courteous.

I will write back this afternoon on my best paper and apologize for the noise, in terms so correct there can be nothing in them to object to. I will say the Aldric matter will resolve itself. I will say the witch will be delivered at the new moon, exactly as instructed, and that nothing more will be broken before then.

And I will choose where.

Not her kitchen. Her kitchen is hers, and she knows every board of it.

I have seen what she does with salt on her own ground. I have no wish to stand in it again.

Mine, then. My front room.

My chairs, my table, my good cups on the tray. Neutral ground has manners, and so do I, and my house has more manners than most.

I will invite her. I will write it on a pleasant card and have it left under her mat. *Miss Marchand is expected at the new moon. Tea at dusk.*

And I will invite him.

The principal did not ask for him. The principal asked only for the witch. But I have been rebuked this morning, and I find I would like very much to bring something home that nobody asked for and that nobody can call noise.

A King's enforcer, freely given, would be very quiet indeed.

I will set out a chair for each of them.

I will set out the good cups.

And I will be gracious to the very last. I have always been gracious.

What else would I be, this morning, with my fingers cold on the cup?

Grace is the only coat I own that fits over fear.
