# I-5 · Cray

*Placement: after Ch. 28.*

The reply to my apology came at dusk, on the eve of the new moon.

I had written very carefully. I had used my best paper and my best pen, and I had said all the correct things in all the correct order, and I had never said *I was afraid*, because one does not say that to the principal.

One simply lets it be understood.

The reply was shorter than my letter.

They always are.

*The witch is required. The enforcer is a courtesy.*

That was all. No reproach this time, and no forgiveness either. Only the terms, restated, in that beautiful unhurried hand, as though I might have forgotten them.

I sat with it in my chair by the window until the light went.

---

A courtesy.

I have always been fond of courtesies. They are the smallest things one can offer and the ones people remember longest. A glass of the right wine.

A name remembered. A chair set for a man before he knocks.

The principal does not want him, and never has. The principal wants the witch, and her name, and the whole city's debts on her shelves, and the enforcer is simply something I may bring if I like, as one brings flowers to a dinner.

I would like to bring him.

I have been rebuked this season. I have been reminded, in a hand that has never hurried, that I am useful and not necessary. I have looked at the column of names in the register and counted the ones that stopped signing, and I have not slept well since.

A man in my position ought to have something in his pocket.

A King's enforcer, freely given, sitting at the King's door and bringing me what he notices. That is not a courtesy. That is a door of my own.

---

He will not be given freely.

I know that now. I knew it the moment I told him about her lock, and watched his face on my step. She holds his name.

She will not let him give it. He did not know until I told him, and I watched him walk away down my street toward the river not knowing what to do with his own feet.

I chose to tell him.

Was it cruel, or only honest? I prefer to think it was honest. A man ought to know what he's signing, even if it's too late to read it.

And now he knows, and she knows he knows, and they will both walk into my front room tomorrow at dusk with their faces set, each of them meaning to save the other.

It will be very moving.

Why do I find that I'm looking forward to it? I have always liked a signing with feeling in it.

---

The King's hand is coming.

I know this too. My men at her ward line have been counting the Roche brothers, and there are more of them each night, standing at corners, pretending to wait for streetcars that do not run. There are more of them at the end of my own street.

Big men, patient men. They stand very still and they do not blink, and I recognize the posture. I taught it to my own.

And behind the King's hand, the King.

He will come to watch. He will stand in my doorway with her at his shoulder, the translator, and he will say *witnessed*, and whatever is done in my front room tomorrow will be lawful under his crown.

I find I don't mind. I have never done anything in my life that would not bear witnessing. I have only ever introduced people to what they already wanted.

---

So tonight I set the room.

I move the small table to the center of the rug, the new red rug, the one without any stains. I polish it. I set out the good cups on the silver tray, the thin ones with the gold rim that I have not used since before the war, touching each rim with my fingertip.

A cup for me. A cup for her. A cup for him.

I bring out a bottle of wine. A very good year. The same year I left on her step, and he poured out, and I replaced.

I draw the cork now, so it will breathe.

Then the chairs.

I set my own at the head of the table, where I always sit. I set one on either side of it, facing each other, for the witch and the enforcer, so they'll have to look at one another across the paper.

A chair for each of us. No more.

I stand back and look at it in the lamplight.

It's a lovely room. It has always been a lovely room. I have made a great many introductions in it, and every one was gracious, and every one was correct.

Tomorrow will be the most correct of all.

I put out the lamp.

On the table, the cork lies beside the bottle, and the wine breathes in the dark, and somewhere in the city a very old patience is waiting for the moon to go out.

I will be gracious to the last. I have always been gracious.

What else is there to be?
