# Son of Wolves · Chapter One · Isabeau

The iris are a half-step flat this year, and I have been planting them long enough to hear it.

---

I come out to the Arceneaux woods at dawn on Thursdays, as I have every Thursday since before anyone in this bayou thought to count. I leave my bicycle at the shed, by the door with the pair of new nail holes in it, and I take the trowel and the bucket and I walk the path.

I know every root on it.

I stop at each one.

My father taught me that.

Not about roots.

About stopping. *A Vale doesn't hurry, Isabeau. A Vale arrives exactly when she meant to.* He said it as he said everything, in perfect tune, and I have spent my whole life a quarter-tone under him, trying to find the note.

He's dead now. I have found that the note is easier to hear without him standing in the room.

The rain came through on Tuesday. The ground is soft. I can smell the wild iris before I see them, green and sweet, a smell that sits in the air like a held chord.

I planted the first of them when this path was a deer track.

I have planted a few more every autumn since.

Nobody has ever asked me why.

Nobody knows to ask.

The clearing opens ahead of me, small and round, with the light coming down gray through the oaks. The mound.

The grass I cut last week. The cypress marker, silvered, with his name cut deep.

*HIPPOLYTE ARCENEAUX.*

I kneel in the wet grass in front of it, as I always do, and set down my bucket.

Then I see the ink.

---

It sits below his name.

A signature. Written, not carved, in brown ink so fresh the Tuesday rain beaded on it and never touched it.

I know that hand.

I have held it in my own. I have read it by lamplight in my father's study, in letters he kept in a locked drawer and thought I'd never find. I have seen it at the foot of every letter the broker ever sent him, and above those, in the notations on the margins, and once, only once, in a letter older than all the others, one I kept back from the witch and buttoned into my coat.

That hand signs for the account.

It has never once signed for anything that was not about to be paid.

I stay on my knees in the wet. I am very still.

I have been told I go still like a clock stopping, and I suppose it's true. I can hear my own quiet, and it has a pitch to it, high and thin, the note a glass makes just before it breaks.

Who came here?

Who walked my path, on my roots, past my iris, and knelt where I kneel, and wrote on him?

And what are they going to collect?

---

"Yeah, no."

I don't turn around. I know the voice.

Everyone in Barataria knows the voice. It comes from the treeline behind me, low and rough and bayou-flat, and it is exactly a whole step under every other sound in the clearing.

"Mr. Thibodeaux," I say to the marker.

"You're the one doing this." He's closer now. I hear the palmettos move. "The flowers. The grass. Every week. I've been out here watching the prints for a month trying to figure out whose feet were that small."

"How diligent of you."

"You're a Vale."

"I'm aware."

"You're a Vale," he says again, as though repeating it might make it make sense, "planting flowers on a wolf's grave. On *this* wolf's grave."

I stand up.

I do it slowly, as my father taught me. I brush the wet grass from my knees.

I pull my gloves straight at the wrists. Then I turn around, and there he is.

He's larger than I remember. He's been larger than I remember every time I've seen him, as though my memory keeps trying to make him a more reasonable size. Sunburned across the nose.

Wet to the knees from the path. His left arm held a fraction stiff against his side, where the old King broke it and it never healed right. He's standing at the edge of my clearing with rain in his hair, looking at me as if I am the most out-of-tune thing he has ever heard.

He killed two people once, because my father told him to.

He has never pretended otherwise. It's the only thing about him that has ever been in tune.

"Silas Roche was here Tuesday," he says. "He saw the ink. He went white as a sheet, and Roche doesn't go white."

"I imagine not."

"He's gone to tell the King."

"Then the King will know by noon." I take a step toward the path. "If you'd be so kind as to let me pass, Mr. Thibodeaux, I have somewhere to be."

He doesn't move.

He's standing on the path. On my path. Square in the middle of it, where it narrows between two palmettos, and there's no way past him that doesn't touch him.

"Yeah, no," he says softly. "Not this time."

---

I stop an arm's length from him.

I can smell him. Wolf and rain and river mud and something under it, warm and animal and alive, that makes the back of my throat ache.

It's been a very long time since I stood this close to a wolf. The last time, I was a girl and it was a matter of politics, and I didn't breathe.

I breathe now. I can't seem to help it.

"Move," I say.

"Tell me why."

"Why what?"

"Why a Vale's been tending Hippolyte Arceneaux's grave." His eyes are a pale bayou green, and they're on my face, and they don't leave it. "Why the account signed it. Why you looked at that ink like you'd seen a ghost. And why every wolf in this bayou's been lying about him having a grave for as long as I've been alive."

"You're asking a great many questions for a man standing in my way."

"I'm asking one. You just don't want to hear it."

He reaches out.

I think he's going to take my arm. I think he's going to put that big sunburned palm on me and move me, and I brace for it, and I am not at all certain I'd stop him.

He doesn't take my arm.

He takes the trowel. Out of my gloved fingers, gently, as though I might have forgotten I was holding it. He turns it over and looks at the mud on the blade, black and fresh, and then he looks at me.

His fingers brushed my wrist when he took it. Just the edge of them, through the glove. I can feel exactly where.

"Somebody's collecting, Miss Vale," he says. "You know it. I know it. And whatever's on that marker, it isn't paid yet."

The iris are a half-step flat. The rain is coming again.

I can hear it in the oaks. And the wolf who killed Selene Ward's parents is standing on my path with my trowel in his fist and his eyes on my mouth.

"No," I hear myself say. "It isn't."

"Then we need to talk."
