# 32 · Ondine

I always prepare the room.

I ran nearly the whole way across the city to get to this one.

Down my street in the blue dark, along the canal, through the Marigny with my bag banging against my hip and my lungs on fire. The whole time I could still hear him in my head, shouting through my grandmother's door.

It's amazing what you can outrun when you're scared enough. His voice isn't on that list. I can hear it right now, standing in this man's hallway, clear as the kettle.

Christ, what have I done to him?

Cray lets me prepare it, and that's the part I keep turning over while I work. He opened his door before I knocked, in shirtsleeves and a dark waistcoat, and took in my bag and my laced boots and my face, still blotchy from crying on my own porch.

Then he stepped back and said, "Miss Marchand. How lovely. You'll want the front room, I expect. The light's better."

The light is better. I'll give the bastard that.

---

His house smells of clove and cold ash and beeswax, with old paper under all of it, like an archive nobody has aired in a century. Under the rug by the table there's one more smell, faint, scrubbed at and not quite gone: brandy, and blood.

I know that blood. I opened my grandmother's ledger with it.

So this is where Silas broke the bottle. I stand on the stain a little longer than I need to, and I let myself be furious about it, because furious is easier to carry than scared.

Then I get to work.

Salt first, always. I lay a line across the threshold of the front room, fine and unbroken, poured from a paper twist with my wrist loose and never stopping, as I was taught.

Then a ring around the table, wide enough to take every chair. Then a short line under the window, because windows are just doors for people with better manners.

Cray watches from the hallway with his fingers laced in front of his waistcoat.

"Marchand work," he says. "I've always admired it. Your grandmother laid a line like that across my feet once. I was quite moved."

"She threw a jar at them, is what I heard."

"That too." He smiles. "The line came after. She was a very thorough woman."

"She was a lot of things."

"Yes." He says it gently, as if we're at her wake. "She was."

He watches me pour for a while. I can feel him deciding whether to say more.

"You have her mouth," he says at last. "Did anyone ever tell you? When you set it like that. I noticed it on your step, the first night."

"Don't."

"It was meant kindly."

"Everything you say is meant kindly. That's what's so fucking unbearable about you."

He inclines his head, as if I've paid him a compliment he'll treasure.

"Would you like tea while you work? I have a very good Assam."

"I'd like you to stay on your side of the salt."

"Of course." He takes one step back into the hallway, precisely one, and folds his fingers again. "Your house, your rules. Even here."

I don't answer him. I lay the bone.

It's a knucklebone from the Marchand ossuary jar, yellow and light as a dried bean, and I set it at the head of the table where the paper will lie.

You can use salt for bone if you have to. I'm not going to have to. I brought the real thing.

---

He's set three chairs.

One at the head, for himself. One on each side, for me and for Silas, facing each other across the table. Three good cups on a silver tray. A bottle of wine, the cork already drawn so it can breathe.

He set a place for Silas. He was so sure, the smug son of a bitch.

I drag a fourth chair in from against the wall and put it at the foot of the table, facing his, and I straighten it until all four legs sit square inside the salt.

He tilts his head.

"Who's that for?"

"The principal." I don't look up. "Every party at the table. You can't change the terms of a debt behind the creditor's back, or the creditor comes to collect the change. You taught my grandmother the forms. You know that."

For the first time since he opened the door, he looks at me as if I've said something he hadn't already guessed.

Then his whole face warms.

"How correct," he says softly. "How very correct. Nobody has thought to set that place in a long time."

The room shifts when he says it. The lamp flame leans a fraction toward the empty chair and then straightens again.

I pretend I didn't see that. Jesus Christ, I saw that.

What exactly did I just invite to sit down? And why does it feel so pleased to be asked?

---

He brings out the paper in a leather folder gone soft with age and lays it open on the table in front of the knucklebone.

There it is. My grandmother's obligation.

I've seen it copied in her ledger, and I've read it described in her dying script, but I've never seen the original. Honorine's signature sits at the foot, surname only, careful and upright. Above it, the consideration, delivered. And above that, the collateral line, one name, in writing that was never hers.

*Ondine.*

He wrote me down.

Before I was ever born, a man sat at a table and wrote my name like a note for the grocer. Eggs, butter, shit, a granddaughter.

"Whenever you're ready," Cray says. He takes his chair at the head, crosses one knee over the other, and folds his fingers on his knee. "There's no hurry at all. Mr. Roche will be along, I'm sure."

"He won't."

"No?"

"I locked him in my house."

Cray laughs, delighted, a warm real laugh I didn't expect him to have in him. "Oh, well done. With what?"

"The writ."

"The King's writ." He shakes his head, admiring. "You spent the crown on a lock. Your grandmother would have kissed you on both cheeks."

"My grandmother sold me to you."

"Introduced," he says. "She introduced you. I've never sold anyone anything, Miss Marchand. Mr. Roche will tell you the same."

"Mr. Roche will tell me a lot of things, after he's done yelling."

"I look forward to it."

I sit down in my chair, and I take out Isabeau's letters.

---

There are four of them, tied with black ribbon, and every one of them is in his hand.

Cassian's correspondence with his broker. Isabeau Vale brought them to my door in a box, with her face shut tight as a piano lid, and when I asked her what it had cost her to carry them across the city, she said, "If you'd be so kind as to never tell me how it ends."

I untie the ribbon. I lay the top letter flat beside the collateral line, so that his signature sits right next to my name.

Same serifs. Same slope on every capital. Same patience.

Cray's eyes go to the letter, and then to me. His fingers stay folded on his knee.

"What are you cooking, Miss Marchand?"

"Salt for bone," I tell him.

I take out the knife that has never cut food, and I cut my thumb with it.

---

Blood on the salt. That's how every working starts in my house. The client pays in, and the house holds the payment.

I press my bleeding thumb to the salt ring, then to the knucklebone, then to the paper just under the collateral line. Then I say the terms out loud, because you have to. Plain words, every one. A witch who hides the terms from herself pays what she didn't intend.

"This line was written by the broker's hand. The broker's hand is here." I touch his signature on Isabeau's letter. "A name the broker wrote, the broker answers. His hand, his name."

The ink moves.

It's small at first. The first letter of my name softens at its edges, as if the paper under it has gone damp. Then the whole word loosens and lifts, just barely, a thread of old brown ink standing up off the page like a hair in a draft.

It's working. It's actually fucking working.

Across the table, Cray has gone very still.

And then the fourth chair pushes back.

---

The chair itself doesn't move. Whatever is sitting in it leans forward, and every flame in the room leans with it. The air goes heavy and cold, and suddenly it smells of river silt and wet stone and a candle burned down to nothing in a room without a window.

It doesn't speak. It has never needed to.

It looks at the paper, and at me, and at the thread of my name standing up off the page.

And it doesn't agree.

I feel the refusal as pressure, the weight under a pot lid a second before the pot boils over.

It's patient. God help me, it's so patient. It's older than my house and older than Cray's, and it has never once been in a hurry, and it has all night to wait me out.

I have until sundown, a cut thumb, and a knucklebone.

The thread of my name starts to settle back onto the paper.

"No," I say. "No, you don't. Not in my working."

I press down harder with my thumb. The cut opens wider, and I bleed into the salt, and I say the terms again, louder.

"His hand, his name. You took a line in the broker's writing. You don't get to keep it in mine."

The pressure doesn't lift. It changes. It stops pushing, and it starts asking.

Every working costs the client something enforceable. I know that better than anyone alive. I've charged a year, a name, a memory, a favor with no ceiling on it. I've never once sat on the paying side of my own table.

So what does it want? What would something that old even bother to take from me?

"Fine," I say, and my voice cracks right down the middle. "Fine. Take it. Take whatever you want and get the fuck off my name."

---

It takes her face.

I feel it happen. I'm thinking of her, because of course I'm thinking of her, at a table with salt and bone and her name at the foot of the page. I'm a little girl in her kitchen and she's teaching me salt for bone, her fingers over mine on the paper twist, her voice saying *wrist loose, chère, never stop pouring.*

I can see her fingers. I can see the salt. I can see the high lace collar from the portrait in my hall, the one she glared out of this morning while I cried against her wall.

Where her face should be, there's nothing at all.

No darkness, no blur. Her face is simply gone, lifted out of me clean, as neatly as I'd fish a bay leaf out of a pot before I serve it.

I make a sound I've never made in my life.

On the paper, the thread of my name comes clean off the page, a curl of brown ink hanging in the air above the table, and then it dissolves.

For one breath the collateral line is blank.

Then new ink rises into it from underneath, from inside the paper itself, in a patient hand with a familiar slope on every capital.

*Absalom Cray.*

---

He understands before I've finished reading it.

I watch it land in him.

For a second his face does nothing, that pleasant nothing he wears for everyone.

Then it breaks.

He moves.

I don't see him do it. Nobody sees an Elder move.

I'm in my chair, and then I'm not, and his fingers are around my throat and my back hits the wall so hard the plaster cracks behind my skull.

My boots are off the floor. I kick him, and it's like kicking a doorframe.

"Take it off."

His voice is still perfectly polite, and that's the worst part of all. His grip is crushing my windpipe, and his voice is the same one that offered me wine on my own step.

"Take it off, Miss Marchand. Please. You don't understand what you've put in that line."

I can't breathe, and I can't answer. I claw at his wrist and it doesn't give, not even a little.

A witch in a room with a hostile Elder is a dead woman. I've always known that. Every witch in this city learns it before she learns to pour salt.

I prepared the room anyway. I prepared it for the working, not for this, and I'm going to die of that distinction on a nice man's plaster.

Silas is going to be so goddamn angry with me.

"It's done," I croak, with the last air I have. "It was always your hand."

His grip tightens.

Behind him, the fourth chair sits perfectly still. Whatever is in it is watching the two of us now, a buyer at an auction with no preference at all about who wins.

Is this how she felt at the end, with the medicine rattling in her chest? Did she know what she'd signed me into, or did he tell her it was nothing?

Did I really have her mouth? I'll never know now. Oh, fuck.

My vision starts going gray at the edges.

And the front door comes off its hinges.

---

It doesn't open. It comes off. It slams into the hallway wall in two pieces, and the salt line I laid across the front-room threshold goes up in a spit of white sparks as a body crosses it without slowing down.

Silas.

His right palm is raw and open, blistered to the wrist, the old burns split and bleeding down his fingers onto Cray's floor. His coat is gone. His face is doing what I've only ever seen it do once, in my kitchen, standing in the glass.

He looks at Cray's fingers on my throat.

"Witch," he says, very low. "Look at me."

I look at him.

I try to think of her, so I'll have one thing to hold on to while the gray closes in. The kitchen. The salt. The lace collar. The voice saying *never stop pouring.*

I can't remember my grandmother's face.
