# 13 · Silas

She asks me to read it to her, and I do, and I would give the rest of my years to unread it.

---

The ledger is open on the table where my blood cracked the binding last night. She slammed it shut before either of us read a word, then put her fingers in my hair and pulled me down, and I knelt on her kitchen floor and put my mouth on her, and the ledger stayed closed until morning.

The kitchen smells like both of us. Her skin and rosemary burned at dawn and old paper and the roux that caught on the stove while I was on my knees and she forgot it existed.

She's at the sink. Hair still down from where I pulled the pencil out of it. Below her ear, the mark from my mouth she hasn't touched yet.

She won't sit.

I know what standing means. I have read a thousand rooms by how the people in them hold their weight, and standing means a person who wants the option of leaving.

Ondine Marchand has never once left her own kitchen. So why is she standing as if she might? What has she already guessed?

"Read it out loud," she says. "I can't look at her writing right now."

I sit. I open the ledger.

---

Honorine Marchand kept her records like every witch does. Her own pen, her own script, every term of a working set down plainly. A witch who hides the terms from herself is a witch who pays what she didn't intend.

One page, and the ink changes from the first entry to the last. Good iron gall at the top. Brown and thin at the bottom, where the grip weakened and the nib wandered.

The paper went brittle where it folded against itself inside a brick wall, for longer than Ondine has been alive.

The first entry is a date in old Marchand calendar. I know the letters but I can't convert the year. Under the date, a name.

"*Jean-Pierre Marchand.*"

Her hands go flat on the counter.

"And beside the name," I say, "in the margin. A cause."

"By a wolf." Her voice is polished smooth from a lifetime of telling it. The words come out worn, the same way a handle comes out smooth from being gripped in the same place every day. "On the Tchoupitoulas levee. She told me that part. The truce held. Nobody was punished. The Code doesn't reach the water."

"The wolf's name is here."

Silence.

I read it as I'd read a print in soft ground, for what it tells me about the person who put it there.

The handwriting around the name is Honorine's, steady and upright, a careful woman's script in a clean house. The wolf's name went in later. Darker ink, pressed harder into the fibers.

She found out who did it and she went back and wrote it in.

Had she already decided what she was going to buy? Or did the name come first and the decision after, as the blade comes before the cut?

"*Hippolyte Arceneaux.*"

"She never told me that." Very low. "She knew who killed her husband and she never told me his name."

---

"Second entry."

I keep my voice level. Level is the least I can give her.

"*Consideration: delivered. The Arceneaux wolf. Dead within the month by the arrangement. The broker introduced the instrument. The principal accepts the obligation is discharged.*"

The faucet drips. The cat shifts on the sill and finds a different patch of sun.

"She bought a killing," Ondine says.

"Yes."

"Through a broker."

"The ledger doesn't name him. But the notations in the margin—"

"Match Cray's letter." She wraps her arms around herself, fingers digging into her own elbows. "Same serifs. Same patience. Same slope on every capital letter. I've been staring at Cray's writing since the envelope came, Silas. I know it better than I know my own."

Cray. The polite bastard has been in this kitchen longer than she has.

She turns her head enough that I can see the line of her jaw, the flour from last night gone, the bruise from my mouth still there.

I watch her as I watch ground, for where the weight goes rather than what she says.

Her weight is forward, on the balls of her feet, a woman getting ready to move with nowhere to move to in her own kitchen.

"The neutral house," she says. "The witch who never picks a side. Who holds names for both courts. Who doesn't ask who the working's for." Her arms tighten against her own ribs. "She taught me that. She sat at this table and taught me substitution and impartiality and the sanctity of a held name, and she'd already bought a killing with my name on the receipt."

"Witch. Stay in the room."

She exhales. Her shoulders drop a degree.

"I'm in the room."

"Stay in it."

"Read me the third entry."

---

I don't want to read the third entry.

I have read a manifest with my own designation on it, in a room that smelled like pine tar and sweat and the bastard who owned me. I have read orders that ended with bodies I had to move. I have read a king's ledger where names stopped appearing on dates I can trace to graves I dug.

Two hundred years behind men at desks, reading everything upside down and sideways and between the lines where the real terms live.

This is the worst fucking thing I have ever read right side up.

"*Collateral.*" I hold my voice still, as I'd hold a blade. "*Pledged against the obligation of the Marchand line. One name, of weight, to be delivered upon demand.*"

Her breathing stops. In the silence I can hear the canal outside, a bicycle bell, someone calling a name that isn't hers.

"What name?"

She already knows. I can read it in the set of her spine — the direction of a woman who heard the answer before the question finished, the way you hear a door slam before you see what came through it.

"*Ondine.*"

The kitchen holds her name like one of her jars holds a working. Sealed. Contained. Nowhere for it to go.

"Written in the broker's hand," I tell her. "Not Honorine's."

Did Cray write it because she asked him to, or because he wanted his handwriting on what he was buying?

She turns around.

Her eyes are dry and her fingers are steady. The shaking lives deeper, in the structure, below anything fingers can reach. I know the difference between a man running and a man falling, and this is a woman standing perfectly still while the fucking ground moves under her.

"She signed my name," Ondine says. "As surety. Before my mother was married. Before my mother was pregnant. Before anyone in this house knew there would be—"

"There's a note. Below the collateral line."

She closes her eyes. Breathes. Opens them.

"Read it."

---

The last entry is in a dying woman's hand.

The letters list to the right, heavy, losing the careful upright of the earlier work. The ink is thin, and two words are smudged under a drop thicker than tears.

I can smell it in the paper under the dust and the roux grease — camphor, blood, the medicine for the chest. She wrote this drowning from the inside.

How long did she sit with the pen before she started? How many times did she put it down?

"*The wolf took my husband on the levee on a Sunday. The truce said I couldn't ask the court. The Code said I couldn't ask the King.*"

Ondine reaches behind her. Finds the counter. Holds on.

"*The broker said I could ask him.*"

"*I have nothing of weight but the line. The broker suggested the child my daughter will have. I don't know yet if my daughter will have a child. I am putting the name I would give her.*"

"Stop."

"*If there is no child, the name has no weight, and the collateral is void. If there is a child, she will be a Marchand, and her name will hold every name this house holds, and that is the weight they want.*"

"I said *stop.*"

"*I am sorry for it. I am sorry for all of it. But Jean-Pierre is dead on the levee and the wolf is still breathing and I cannot die knowing he is.*"

I close the ledger. The binding shuts with a sound like a fist closing.

---

She doesn't cry.

I have seen men cry and I have made hard bastards cry, and crying has a direction to it — it goes somewhere, burns through what it has to, reaches a bottom. What Ondine does is stand in her grandmother's kitchen in her grandmother's cardigan and go so still that the faucet drip sounds like a bell.

"She loved me."

Flat. Clear. The voice of a woman reading back the terms of a contract she didn't sign.

"She named me. She held me when I was born. She taught me salt-for-bone on this table when I was eight, and she'd pick me up afterward and there's still a salt stain on the wood grain from where I sat, and she loved me." Her fingers move along the counter, tracing the knife scars. "She loved me and she wrote my name on a piece of paper and gave it to a man in good shoes, and she died in her bed with a dead wolf and a clean conscience."

"Ondine—"

"She sold me before I breathed."

The sentence comes out of her as if she's been carrying it since the wax seal. Since *collateral* and the one name under it. The knowing was in her body since the envelope and her mind just caught up.

I stand. Seven boards between the table and the sink, and I know every one of them by the sound it makes, and I cross them without making any.

I take her hands.

She lets me. Her fingers in my scarred palm, my thumb on her wrist where the pulse is fast and hard and honest.

I can feel the beat of it against the old burn tissue, the one part of my body that still registers heat as more than information.

Then she pulls free. Gently. The way you set down a jar you've decided not to break.

"Don't." Her eyes come up. "Don't try to finish a sentence about this. I can hear when you're about to force one, and whatever you build will be a lie, and you don't lie to me."

She's right.

"What do you need?" I ask. Because that much I can finish.

"I need this house to not be built on a murder." She pulls the pencil from her hair, sets it on the counter. A small and precise motion that costs nothing, and the nothing is the point. "I need the neutral ground to be neutral. I need a whole working life of clients and held names and court obligations to not be resting on a grandmother who bought a dead wolf with her granddaughter's name."

She looks at the shelves, at a hundred and sixty jars of names in this kitchen.

Clients. Debts. Obligations from both sides of the canal, every one of them resting on the authority of the Marchand name.

All of them resting on her.

What does a woman look like when she finds out the ground under the house was salted before she was born?

She looks like Ondine. Standing in her own kitchen, arms at her sides, surveying the architecture of a life that was collateral before it started.

"I was paid for before I had a name."

She picks up the nearest jar. Green glass, small, a label in script I've never learned to read. She turns it in her fingers. Once. Twice.

She throws it against the far wall.

I read the throw as I read anything that moves. Overhand, from the shoulder, no wind-up. Aimed past the window, past the door, past me, at the one stretch of bare plaster in a kitchen where every other surface holds something she'd grieve.

Even now she's choosing where the damage lands.

Good. Let her break the whole fucking shelf.

The glass hits and the name-working inside it leaves a dark smudge that will never come out of the paint. It smells like burnt sugar and wet stone, and whatever name was in that jar is loose in the room now, and gone.

She reaches for another jar. Stops. Reads the label. Sets it back on the shelf.

"That's a child-naming," she says. "Belongs to a woman on Esplanade who's due in October."

Her fingers have stopped shaking, and the steadiness is worse than the tremor because the tremor was honest and this is a woman choosing to be precise about the damage she allows herself.

"One jar," she says. "That's all."

She looks at the dark smudge on the wall, the broken glass on the floor, the ledger closed on the table with my blood dried in its crease.

"I'm not her. I'm not Honorine. I don't spend what isn't mine."

"Witch."

She looks at me.

"I know."

She holds my eyes for a long beat, and I don't know what she reads in them, and then she walks out of the kitchen.

Her bedroom door stays open, because Ondine's doors always stay open, and the bed creaks once, and then nothing.

That nothing is worse than crying. A woman in a house that was never as clean as she thought.

---

I don't sweep the glass. The glass is hers to decide about.

I stay at the table with my palm on the closed ledger and through the wall her silence and on the shelf a hundred and fifty-nine jars that still hold.

In my coat, against my chest, is the index card I took from the Bywater firm. Yellowed at the edges. My old designation, and one word after it.

*The Roche boy — introduced.*

The same word sits in Honorine's second entry. *The broker introduced the instrument.*

I haven't held the card beside the ledger to compare the writing. I'm not going to. Not tonight, not in her kitchen, not with her glass still on the floor.

What the fuck would I even do with it, if it were mine to give her?

I look at the window.

The rosemary burns down to ash and I let it.
