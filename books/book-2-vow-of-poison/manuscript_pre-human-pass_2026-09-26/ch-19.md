# 19 · Silas

The envelope says the thing I have never let anyone say to my face.

It says it in brown ink on paper gone soft as cloth, and paper never has the decency to look away.

---

I've carried it since Barataria. Cream stock, nailed to a dead wolf's shed with my name on the front, riding in my coat where the writ used to live.

Ondine smelled it on me the minute I came through her door. She didn't ask about it. She took me to bed instead, and I talked more tonight than I have in my whole life.

Now she's asleep on her front with the sheet at her waist, and I'm out on her back step with her letter knife, the one that's never cut food.

Her garden smells of wet mint and the frog's green water and the last of the jasmine on the fence. The moon over the canal is thinner than it was last night. I don't count what's left of it, because I don't have to.

The envelope tells me things before I open it. There are two nail holes through the top edge, rusted brown, from a nail driven by someone who wanted it to hold in weather. The corners are soft where my coat has rubbed them. The seal hasn't been lifted and pressed back down. Nobody between the shed and my pocket wanted to know what was in it.

He knew I'd carry it for days before I read it. He wrote it for a man who waits.

I slit the seal.

---

There are two papers inside.

The top one is new, Cray's stock with Cray's patient slope on every capital. I set it face down on the step, because a man who puts the pleasant paper on top wants you to read it first.

The bottom one was folded in thirds by somebody who expected it to live in a drawer. The creases have gone brown, and it opens along them with a sound like dry leaves.

It's a receipt.

*Received of the house of Croix, for the Roche boy, the catcher's dog: consideration in full.*

*Turned too soon. Hands burned, will not heal. Tracks well. Holds what he's told to hold.*

*Does not speak of it.*

*Introduced by A. Cray.*

I read it upside down first, from habit, then turn it right side up and read it again. The clerk who wrote *does not speak of it* knew me better than anyone alive does now.

Dog.

Nobody has said that to my face since the yard. The man who kept the kennel said it every morning with his boot on the chain.

I have spent every night since making sure nobody else gets close enough to try. Aurelian never said it, and Lucian never has.

Introduced by A. Cray. Of course. Of fucking course.

I always believed Aurelian found me himself, that he came down to that yard big and warm and saw something in the mud worth the money. I never once asked who told him where the yard was.

I remember shoes.

Thin soles in the yard mud, and a man at the edge of the pen who never looked down at what he was standing in. I was chained low with my eyes on the ground, because the ground was the only thing in that place that didn't lie to me.

I watched those shoes come and go, and I never lifted my head to see the face above them.

I have been following those shoes around this city since the crowning.

I have been following them a hell of a lot longer than that.

He's the man who sold me.

I was—

I fold the receipt back along its creases. It doesn't matter what I was.

---

The new paper says less. Cray never needs much.

*Mr. Roche,*

*You were introduced once before and did well by it. The Marchand obligation may be discharged by any name of equal weight, freely given. I have considered the weights. Yours would serve.*

*I'm at home most evenings. Do come by. I'll open something good.*

*A.C.*

I look back at the kitchen window. Her lamp is off, and the rosemary on the sill has gone cold.

I go.

---

Bywater after midnight smells like river mud and bread proofing in somebody's back room, and the sweet rot of a banana tree dropping fruit nobody bothers to pick up.

The street reads the same as it always does. A man on a porch two doors down sees my coat and finds a reason to go inside. A cat crosses in front of me without hurrying, so nothing on this block has frightened it in a while. The gutters are dry except outside one house, where somebody rinsed the step tonight and swept the water toward the street.

Cray's house is a shotgun double painted the color of weak tea. I've stood across the street from it more fucking nights than I'll admit, in sight, never in custody, and not once has it looked like it was expecting me.

Tonight it does.

The porch light is on. The gate latch has been lifted and left resting on the post instead of in the catch.

The front step is worn into a shallow dip at the center by one stride and one pair of shoes, with no second set of wear anywhere on the wood. Nobody visits this man, and nobody has for a long moment.

Through the front window I can see a table, and on it, two glasses.

He set the second one out before I finished reading.

I don't knock. He'd enjoy a knock.

---

"Mr. Roche."

He's in the front room in shirtsleeves and a dark waistcoat, on his feet, as if he rose the moment my weight touched the step. He smells of clove and cold ash and beeswax, and under all of it, paper.

"You came in your working coat," he says. "I'm flattered."

"You were in the yard."

"I was." He pours. The bottle has wax on the neck and an old label I've seen in the Aldric cellar. "Sit, please. You won't, I know. It's polite to offer."

"You stood at the edge of the pen."

"I did. The man who kept it told me you'd bite."

He sets a glass at the near end of the table, exactly where a man who won't sit would stand. He knows where I stand.

"You didn't bite," he says. "You were very still. I remember thinking a stillness like that would suit a house with a great many doors."

"So you sold me."

"I introduced you." He says it gently, as if he's correcting a child's grammar at the dinner table. "I've never sold anyone anything, Mr. Roche."

"The paper says consideration in full."

"The paper was written by a clerk. There were river men who'd have paid more for a tracker. I chose the old King instead. I thought you'd do well in his house."

He lifts his own glass an inch.

"You did."

My fist is closed in my coat pocket. The burned palm doesn't feel my nails, and it hasn't felt much of anything since before I was turned.

"Why send it?"

"Because you'd have found it. You found the card at the firm." His eyes stay pleasant. "I preferred you hear it from the man who made the introduction. It seemed a courtesy."

He calls it a courtesy. The bastard is handing me the end of the leash so I'll know exactly how long it is.

"I bought your king a glass of this once," Cray says, and turns the bottle so the label faces me. "At a bar, before he was king. He didn't touch it. His father never did either."

"Lucian doesn't drink from strangers."

"No. He was raised well. I've always admired that house."

---

"Say it plainly," I tell him.

"Your name for hers."

He sits, since I won't. He crosses one knee over the other, and his thin sole turns up toward the lamp, clean as the day it was cut.

"Freely given, and the Marchand paper is discharged," he says. "She keeps her name. She keeps her shelves and everything on them. She keeps her cat."

"And me?"

"You go on exactly as you are." He opens his palm, generous. "You stand at your king's door. You keep his confidence. You read his rooms for him."

"And?"

"And you'd simply have somewhere else to bring what you notice."

A man inside the crown, reporting to the account. A dog in the King's house again, with a longer chain and a nicer fucking yard.

"The writ," I say.

"Stays, of course. Your king's protection over her house is a lovely piece of paper, and I'd never ask you to withdraw it." He considers a word before he spends it. "That would be unkind."

"You don't do unkind."

"I try not to. It's so rarely necessary." He tips his head toward the river, toward her house on the far side of the city. "Her grandmother refused me too, you know. The first time I came to that kitchen. She threw a jar at my feet."

Something goes still in my chest.

"Ondine threw one at a wall," I say, before I can stop it.

"Then she's very like her," Cray says fondly. "Honorine came around by the end of the month."

---

"Who's it for?"

"The account."

"Who's the account?"

He smiles. It's a real smile, warm and a little sorry, as if I've asked a good question at a funeral.

"Older than this house, Mr. Roche. Older than your king's. It has never been in a hurry about anything, and it has never once been refused."

Then who has been refusing it, for the length of every record I've ever read upside down? What does it want with a pair of eyes in Lucian's house that it can't already buy? Why does a thing that old need a dog at all? And how thin does the moon get before it stops asking this nicely?

"Cassian was yours," I say.

"Cassian was a client." He sips. "The old man never met the witch. He preferred a broker between."

Lucian gave me Cassian's last words the morning after, standing in the courtyard with the plain blade still on the bench.

These are the same words, near enough. One man said them with blood in his mouth. This one says them over brandy.

---

I could kill him before he sets the fucking glass down.

I know it in my shoulders. Across the table, one fist in his collar, his head into the corner of the mantel, then the throat. It would take less time than it takes him to swallow.

He watches me know it, and he doesn't move.

"You could," he says. "I'd understand. But the paper goes home when I do, Mr. Roche, and where it goes, nobody reads upside down."

He knows I read upside down. He watched me learn it, from the edge of a pen, and at every desk since.

He stands. He corks the bottle and holds it out to me by the neck, the same as he held the wine on her step.

"Take it, please. It was always meant for your house."

I take it by the neck.

I don't decide to close my fist. It closes. The glass gives with a flat crack, and brandy runs down my wrist into my sleeve and all over the rug the bastard is so proud of.

Shards go into the burned palm deep enough to stay there on their own. The scar tissue barely reports it. What it reports is heat, the old kind, and I stand in the smell of spilled brandy and my own blood and make myself not take the last step.

Cray looks at his rug, and then at me.

He takes a folded handkerchief from his waistcoat and holds it out, pressed and monogrammed.

"You're bleeding on the Aldric vintage," he says. "Keep it. I have others."

I don't take it. He sets it on the table beside my full glass, squared to the edge.

"Think about it," he says. "That's all I've ever asked of anyone."

I look at him for a long while.

"I'll think about it."

His face does nothing at all, and that's how I know I've given him more than he set the second glass out for.

---

Her kitchen light is on when I get back.

A whole bundle of rosemary is smoldering in the saucer on the sill. She's at the table in the holey cardigan and a pair of my socks, the letter knife back on its hook, watching the door as if she's watched it since the moment I went through it.

"You went to him."

It isn't a question and I don't answer it.

She sees my fist. She smells the brandy on me, and the clove, and she puts them together in the time it takes her to stand up.

"Oh, shit. Jesus Christ, Silas. Sit down."

I sit.

She brings the lamp and the tweezers and a bowl, and a brown bottle that smells of witch hazel and thyme. She pulls her chair in until her knees bracket mine, then takes my wrist and turns the palm up to the light.

She doesn't flinch at it. She never has.

"Hold still."

"It'll close by morning, witch."

"The cuts will." She lifts out the first shard and drops it in the bowl with a small bright tick. "This won't. That's why you keep it in your coat."

She works slowly. Every piece she lifts, she presses the heel of her thumb beside the wound until the next one rises.

Her hair is down around her face. Her breath lands warm on my palm, and the burned skin doesn't feel much, but it feels that.

When the last piece is out she washes the palm clean, and then she lifts it and puts her mouth to the center, right on the worst of the scar.

I stop breathing. I don't need to breathe. I stop anyway.

She climbs into my lap. Her thighs settle over mine on the narrow chair, and the cardigan falls open, and she doesn't pull it closed.

She puts her forehead against mine.

"What did he want?"

"What he offered you on the step."

Her fingers tighten in my collar. "And you said?"

I look at the window.

"Silas Roche," she says, very softly. "Don't you dare."

I don't say anything. I put my good palm flat on her lower back, under the wool, where she's warm and real and entirely her own.

I hold her there until her breathing slows and her weight goes heavy against my chest.

She sleeps like that, in my lap, in her own kitchen, with the rosemary burning down to the stems.

The receipt is in my coat, folded along its brown creases, saying what it says.

I told a man tonight that I'd think about it.

That's the most dangerous sentence I've ever said out loud.
