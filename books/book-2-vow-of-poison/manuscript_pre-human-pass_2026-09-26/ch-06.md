# 6 · Ondine

The French Market at seven in the morning smells like crushed bay and diesel, and somebody is following me.

---

I clock them at the bay stall, the one where old Thibodeaux's cousin sells bay by the armload and pretends not to know what I use it for. There are two of them, both human, both trying very hard to look like men who came to the market for satsumas.

Nobody comes to the French Market at seven for satsumas.

Christ.

The tall one has a baseball cap and a neck as thick as a fence post. The short one is wearing cheap white sneakers with the sole coming loose on the left foot, so every other step goes *slap* on the wet concrete.

They stay a few stalls back.

Amateur hour. When I stop, they stop. When I pick up a bundle of bay and smell it, the tall one picks up a mango and holds it as though he's never seen fruit before.

Hired, then. Nobody who works for Absalom Cray directly would be caught dead in those shoes.

So Cray hired out, and Cray thinks I'm worth two idiots and a pair of Payless sneakers.

I don't know whether to be relieved or insulted.

I settle on both.

I pay for the bay. I don't hurry.

Hurrying is for people with shit to hide.

My grandmother used to say you should never let them see you run. It tells them what you're afraid of, and then they know where to push.

---

I've got a list, and I work it.

Salt first, from the man at the end who sells it gray and coarse out of barrels, dug from the marsh down by Grand Isle.

Table salt is fine for a pot.

For a ward you want salt that remembers the water it came out of. I buy a sack of it and he wraps it in newspaper and winks at me, because he's known me since I was a girl buying it for Honorine.

Then bone. There's a woman two rows over who sells soup bones out of a cooler, and if you ask her the right way, she'll reach under the table and sell you the other kind.

Knucklebones, clean and dry.

I don't ask where they come from. She doesn't tell.

That's the arrangement.

Then hair. That one's harder. Hair only works if it was willingly given, so you can't buy it off a barber's floor.

You have to buy it from the person who grew it. There's a girl who braids hair on the corner of Decatur, and she saves me the cuttings from customers who say yes when she asks.

"Something big?" she says, handing me the paper twist.

"Something stupid," I tell her. "Something really fucking stupid."

The whole time, the sneaker goes *slap, slap, slap* a few stalls behind me, a metronome for idiots.

A working needs salt, bone and hair, and a price. I have three of the four. The price is the part I'm still trying to figure out, and it's the part I'd give almost anything not to be the one paying.

---

At the corner of Decatur and Ursulines, I stop pretending I haven't seen them.

I turn around. I look straight at the tall one, right in his mango face, and I give him my nicest customer-service smile.

"Follow me, then," I say. "I'll wait."

His mouth falls open. The short one actually steps behind a lamppost, as though that helps.

God help us all.

I turn and walk into the botanica on the corner.

---

Mireille's shop is older than both of us. Candles, powders, saint cards and dried snakeskins, a whole wall of oils in brown glass bottles, and a back room behind a bead curtain where Mireille keeps the things tourists can't handle.

She looks up from the counter when the bell goes. She sees my face, and she sees the two men through the front window, loitering on the corner and pretending to read a menu.

"Oh, *chère*," she says. "Who'd you piss off?"

"A banker."

"Jesus, Mary and Joseph."

"The worst kind." She lifts the counter flap. "Back room's open. You want me to call somebody?"

"No. I'm going to handle it myself, like a grown-ass woman."

"Course you are." She sighs. "Don't break anything expensive."

I go through the bead curtain into the back room and set my bag down. Then I get to work, fast, my heart going fast as a rabbit's.

I tear open the newspaper of marsh salt and lay a line across the doorway, right under the beads, wrist loose, never stopping. I lay it thick. Then I take one of the knucklebones out of its paper and set it at the center of the line, where the threshold would be if there were a real door, and I cut the tip of my finger with the little blade I keep on my keys and put one drop of blood on the bone.

Salt for the boundary. Bone for the lock. Blood for the price.

It's a small working. A cheap one.

It won't stop a vampire or a wolf, and it won't stop anybody who's been invited. It'll stop two hired humans who came in without asking.

I step back and wait.

---

They come in a few minutes later. Of course they do. I hear the bell, and Mireille's voice, bright and sweet, saying *can I help you boys with something?* I hear the sneaker, *slap*, and the tall one saying something low.

Then the bead curtain rattles, and the tall one puts his hand through it.

He stops.

I watch it hit him. His arm is through the beads and his foot is on the threshold, right over my salt, and he stops dead. He stops as a man stops when he's walked into a room and forgotten why he came in.

His face goes blank. He stands there with his hand in the air and his mouth a little open.

Behind him, the short one bumps into his back.

"Go," the short one says. "What are you doing? She's right there."

"I—" says the tall one.

He pulls his arm back out of the beads, and turns around, and walks out of the shop. I hear the bell go. The short one stands there for a second, looking from the curtain to the door, and then he follows, slap, slap, slap, out into the street.

The working will hold them off me until it wears through, sometime around lunch. After that they'll remember exactly what they came for, and they'll be angrier about it.

That's fine. By lunch I'll be home, and they can go cry to their boss about it.

I go out the back door into the alley with my salt and my bones and my hair, and I walk the long way home along the river, and nobody follows me.

No rescue. No enforcer. Just a witch and a sack of goddamn salt.

Holy shit, I did that.

I'm shaking the whole way. My thighs are shaking.

My fingers are shaking so hard I nearly drop the salt. There's a stupid, drunk kind of heat running all through me, the kind that comes after a close call, and it's settled right between my legs, and it's all his fault.

Every time I got scared this morning, what I thought about was his hand.

Never the King, never my grandmother, never the goddamn salt. His scarred palm on my tits, and his two fingers curling up inside me, and his voice telling me to look at him.

What kind of woman gets turned on by a close call? What kind of witch lays a ward with her pussy throbbing?

This kind, apparently. Fuck my life.

---

My phone rings as I'm crossing Esplanade.

"Where are you?"

No hello. There's never a hello.

"Crossing Esplanade."

"You went out alone."

"I went to the market, Silas. People do it every day. Nobody's ever died buying bay leaves."

"Somebody followed you."

I stop in the middle of the sidewalk. A man with a dog walks around me.

"How the hell do you know that?"

"Mireille called Lucian. Lucian called me." His voice is very flat and very even, and I've learned that flat means something terrible is happening underneath it. "Two men."

"Two very stupid men. One of them has shitty shoes. I handled it."

"You handled it."

"I laid a line in Mireille's back room and walked them out of it as easily as shooing dogs off a porch. I'm fine. I'm walking home. I'm perfectly fine."

A long silence on the line. I can hear his breathing, and he doesn't need to breathe, so I know he's doing it on purpose, to keep his temper.

"You didn't tell me you were going," he says.

"You were asleep."

"I don't sleep."

"You were doing that thing where you lie on the guest bed with your eyes shut and pretend. I heard you not sleeping through the wall. It was very loud."

"You'd have said no."

"I'd have come with you."

"Same fucking thing." I start walking again. "And speaking of saying no. You asked me this morning what the ledger needs. I told you it bites me. I've been thinking about it. It needs blood that isn't Marchand."

"Then use mine."

He says it at once. No pause at all.

I have to stop again.

"No," I say.

"Ondine."

"No. Absolutely not. Vampire blood in a witch's lock is a promise, Silas. It's no key. It's a contract. It binds the one who bleeds to the one who owns the lock, and neither of them gets to see the terms until they come due." My voice is shaking now, and I hate it. "That'll cost more than you've got."

"You don't know what I've got."

"Neither do you. That's the whole fucking problem with you."

Silence again. Then, lower:

"Come home, witch."

It goes through me as hot as a shot of whiskey. Heat, straight down my spine, pooling between my thighs.

"I *am* coming home. Jesus. I'm walking."

"Walk faster."

"Or what?"

"Or I'll come and get you. And I won't carry you, this time." He pauses. "I'll put you over my shoulder in front of the whole Marigny."

I laugh. It comes out breathless, too high.

"You wouldn't."

"Try me."

"You're furious."

"Yes."

"Good." I cross against the light. "I'm soaking wet, and I've been soaking wet since the market, and it's your fault for being the only thing I think about when I'm scared. So you can be furious all you want, Silas Roche. You can yell at me when I get home. Just do it with your mouth on me."

A sound comes down the line. It's not quite a word. It's the sound he made with his mouth on my tits at the table.

"Walk faster," he says, rough.

"Say please."

"Ondine."

"Say it."

A long pause. I can hear him breathing again. I can hear, I swear to God, the creak of his leather coat as he shifts, as if he's standing somewhere with his fist clenched against a wall.

"Please," he says. "Come home. I want to see you're whole. Then I'm going to put you on that table and find out how wet you are for myself."

He hangs up.

I walk so fast I nearly break into a run, and I'm grinning like an idiot the whole way down Chartres Street.

---

He isn't there when I get home. There's a note on the kitchen table in his square, careful hand: *Tobias.

Back by dark. Lock the door.*

I don't lock the door.

I lay out my salt and my bone and my hair on the long table, and I stand there looking at them, and I think about my grandmother.

She taught me salt for bone at this table. I was a little girl, sitting on the edge of it with my feet swinging, and she had the paper twist in her fingers, and she was pouring a line around a saucer so slowly I thought she'd never finish.

*You can use salt for bone if you have to,* she said. *If you're caught short. Salt remembers the water. Bone remembers the body. Salt can stand in, a little, if the bone's not there.*

*What can stand in for salt?*

*Nothing, baby.* She tipped the twist and let the last grains fall. *Some things have to be what they are.*

She got sick the winter after. The wet years, she called them, though I think they were just the years she was dying. Her chest rattled when she breathed, every breath like a roux about to catch, and at the end she couldn't get up from the bed in the back room, and I used to sit on the floor beside it and pour salt lines for her to look at, because it made her smile.

The last night, she took hold of my wrist.

Her fingers were so thin. I remember that. I remember how hot her skin was, and how dry, and how hard she held on, harder than I thought a dying woman could hold anything.

She pulled me close. Her breath smelled of camphor and blood.

*Ondine,* she said. *Listen to me. Don't let them read you, baby. Whatever they—*
