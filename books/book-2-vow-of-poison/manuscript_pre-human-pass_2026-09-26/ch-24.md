# 24 · Silas

"Same grammar," Selene says. "Different grave."

---

She's bent over the long table in Tobias's registry with her sleeves shoved to her elbows and her hair tied up in a knot, and she has the account's register open in front of her. The real one. The heavy black-spined ledger from the bottom drawer of the Bywater firm.

I went back for it the night of the kitchen.

I didn't tell anybody I was going. I went in through the back door with the new lock again, in the dead hours, with the smell of her broken jars still in my coat, and the file room was exactly as I'd left it.

The window still scraped loose around the frame.

The Marchand file still thin in its drawer.

I took the register. I took it out under my coat as you'd carry a sleeping child, and I brought it here, to the three rooms under the east wing that smell of paper and lamp oil and strong tea, and I put it on Tobias's table without a word.

Tobias looked at it for a long while. Then he took off his useless spectacles and polished them on his shirt and put them back on.

"Well," he said. "That's burglary."

"Yes."

"From a registered firm. Bloody hell, Silas."

It's the only time I've ever heard Tobias Vale swear.

"I'll need to repair the binding before anybody else can read it. The spine's cracked." He pulled it toward him with the tips of his fingers. "Give me a day."

He took two.

---

Now it's morning, and the binding is mended, and Selene has been reading the register for an hour without looking up.

She reads as she always reads, slowly, with her lips very faintly moving. She's a translator. She spent years in court rooms turning one language into another, and she hears what's under the words as I read what's under the ground.

She has her finger on the column of names.

"Look," she says. "Look at this one. And this one. And this one."

I look.

A name in brown ink, in an old French script, running for pages. Then a new name, in different writing, running for pages more.

Then another.

Every one different.

"The hands don't match," I say. "I know. I read it at the firm."

"The hands don't match," Selene agrees. "The grammar does."

She moves her finger down the margin, where each name has a line of notation beside it. Short phrases.

Terms. *Consideration received.

Obligation discharged.

Principal satisfied.*

"It's the same sentence," she says. "Every generation. Different words, some of them. French, Spanish, English, something I think is Latin. But the construction is identical. Same order. Same weight at the end. Same habit of putting the verb last as if it's the only thing that matters."

"One writer."

"One writer across—" She stops. She looks at the front of the register, at pages so brittle and brown I'm afraid to breathe near them. "I don't know how long. As long as there's been writing in this city. Longer, maybe."

"Wearing different names."

"Signing different names." She sits back. "Not wearing them. They're too careful for that. They're signing *somebody's* name. Every time."

"Whose?"

That's when Tobias clears his throat.

---

He's been at the other end of the table the whole time, with the court registry spread out around him in towers: burial records, marriage records, property rolls, the city's dead going back to the first burials, in his tiny immaculate script and the hands of every clerk before him.

"I have a thought," he says, "about whose."

He slides a sheet across to us. It's a list, in his own writing. Two columns.

On the left, every name from the account's register. On the right, a date, and a place.

"I ran them," he says. "Against the burial rolls. It took me most of last night."

I look at the column on the right.

A cemetery. A date.

A cemetery. A date.

Saint Louis. Lafayette.

A churchyard in Algiers. A wolf ground in the bayou.

Every one.

"They're all dead," Selene says slowly.

"They're all dead," Tobias agrees. "Every name that's ever signed for the account belongs to somebody who was buried in this city. And every one of them was buried within a season of the name appearing in the register."

Nobody in the room says anything.

"The account signs with the names of the recently dead," Tobias says. "It takes a grave, and it puts the name on the page, and then it signs with it until the next one."

"Same grammar," Selene whispers. "Different grave."

---

I don't like the fucking silence that comes after that.

It sits in the room like smoke. I read it as I read everything, and I don't like where it goes.

"Whose dead?" I ask.

Tobias looks at me over his spectacles.

"That's the other thing," he says, and pulls another sheet across. "I cross-referenced the buyers. The ones I could find. Every person who ever took a consideration from this account, the ones with any paper trail at all."

He taps the sheet.

"Every one of them had just lost someone. A husband. A son. A sister. A pack-brother. Every buyer on the account was grieving. And every grief was fresh. Weeks. A season at most, before the purchase."

"And every one had access," Selene says. She's reading his sheet upside down. "Look. A court clerk. A pack's keeper of names. A priest with the parish rolls. A witch with a shelf of jars." Her voice goes flat. "Every one of them was holding something the account would want. Names. Records. Access."

"Grief and access," Tobias says. "Every time. The account never buys from anybody without both."

I look down at the list.

At the name halfway down. A widow in the wet years, whose husband died on the Tchoupitoulas levee on a Sunday.

*Marchand, H.*

---

I stand up.

I don't decide to. My body does it. I'm on my feet and walking to the window, the high little window in the registry wall that looks out on nothing but the bottom of a courtyard, and I stand there with my back to them.

The account doesn't only buy grief.

It arrives with it.

A wolf kills a man on the levee on a Sunday. Everybody knows which wolf. Nobody says so.

The court won't ask. The pack won't ask. And within a week, a broker is standing in the widow's kitchen with his hat in his fingers, offering to introduce her to someone who'll see to the wolf.

Within a week.

Before she'd even buried him.

How did Cray know to be there? How did he know, that fast, which widow in the whole city had a shelf full of names and a husband cold on the levee?

Unless he knew before.

Unless somebody arranged the Sunday, and the wolf, and the levee, so that there would be a widow and a shelf and a grief fresh enough to buy.

Honorine Marchand sold her unborn granddaughter to pay for the death of the wolf who killed her husband. And the wolf who killed her husband may have been bought by the same account she paid.

She paid for her own grief, twice over. Christ. The poor bastard paid twice.

And Ondine is the receipt.

The glass is cool against my forehead. I didn't notice leaning on it.

I think of her in the salt on her kitchen floor, the morning after the jars. Kneeling in it with my arm across her ribs and her throat under my mouth, and her blood pouring into me with every feeling she had: the terror, the grief, how badly she wanted me. I think of her coming on my cock with my teeth in her neck, sobbing, and the salt grinding into her knees.

She gave me that. She ordered me to take it.

And whatever sold her before she breathed may have killed her grandfather first, to make the sale.

I'm going to find it. I'm going to find the bastard, whatever the fuck it is, and I'm going to make it pay for every jar.

---

"Silas," Selene says behind me.

I don't turn around.

"Silas. Your palm."

I look down.

My right palm is flat on the stone of the windowsill, and it's bleeding. The old burns have split again, the ones Ondine picked the glass out of. The linen she tied has come loose and there's blood seeping through the ropes of scar tissue, dark and slow, onto Tobias's windowsill.

I didn't feel it. I never feel the fucking thing.

Selene is beside me. She takes my wrist in her fingers, as Ondine does, and turns my palm up to the light, and she looks at it for a long moment.

"You did that on the glass," she says. "At Cray's. And again at her kitchen. And you've been clenching it all morning." She looks up at my face. "You're going to have to tell her what this is."

"It's nothing."

"It's not nothing. It's bleeding on a registry windowsill." Her grip tightens. "And you're going to have to tell *him*."

I know which him she means. There's only one him in this house.

"Tell him, Silas," she says. "Tell Lucian. All of it. The paper, the collateral, the offer, whatever's in your coat. Tell him about this." She shakes the list in her other hand. "Tell him the account buys grief and brings it with it. He's the King. He needs to know what's been buying names in his city since before there was a King."

I open my mouth.

"Not—"

It stops. It always stops.

Not yet, and not until I know what I'm going to do with my own name. Not until I've decided whether what I'm going to do is what my king would forbid.

None of it comes out.

Selene looks at me for a good while with her translator's eyes. She's reading the grammar of what I didn't say. She's always been able to do that.

She lets go of my wrist.

"Not," she repeats softly. "Right."

---

Tobias wraps my palm in clean linen from a drawer, neat and tight, without saying a word. He's very good at it. He's very good at everything that needs doing without fuss.

Then he goes back to his towers of paper.

"There are still questions," he says to the burial rolls, not to me. "I'll keep at them."

"Such as?"

"Who the account is. That's the obvious question." He turns a page. "What it wants with this city, specifically. Why here. Why every name it buys is a name that holds other names. And what happens at the new moon, when it gets the Marchand house, and every debt on those shelves."

He looks up at me.

"And why it wants you as well," he adds. "When it already has the witch."

I don't answer.

I pick up the list, the one with *Marchand, H.* halfway down, and I fold it once and put it in my coat, in the inside pocket, with the receipt and the index card and Cray's note.

My coat is getting heavy. Every pocket of it is somebody's grave.

---

Lucian is in the small study when I come up from the registry. The door's open. He's at his desk with a stack of letters from the Aldric house, and he looks up when my shadow crosses the threshold.

"Silas," he says. "Anything?"

I stand in his doorway with my palm wrapped in Tobias's linen and my pocket full of paper.

"Still confirming, sire," I say.

He studies me for a while.

He doesn't say anything. He doesn't need to. He can see where my eyes went, and they went to the window behind his head.

I turn around and walk out of his study, and I don't tell him.
