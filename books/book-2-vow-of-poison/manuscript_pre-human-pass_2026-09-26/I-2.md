# I-2 · Cray

*Placement: after Ch. 11.*

She threw the jar at my feet before I had taken off my hat.

Green glass, small. It broke on her kitchen boards and let out a smell of scorched honey and river clay. A name had been held in it. She did not care whose. She wanted something to break, and my shoes were nearest.

I stood in the wet and waited for her to finish.

It was the wet years, as she called them afterward. The gutters were full and the river was high against the levee, and Honorine Marchand had a chest that rattled like a roux about to catch. She was not yet old. She looked old that night. Her husband had been dead since the Sunday before.

"Get out of my house," she said.

"Of course," I said, and did not move.

---

He had died on the Tchoupitoulas levee, under a wolf.

Everyone knew which wolf. Nobody said so. The truce between the canal and the bayou was new, and thin, and it held on the understanding that what happened on the water stayed there. The court would not ask. The King would not ask. The pack had buried the matter and would bury anyone who dug.

So the widow sat in her kitchen with her husband's boots by the door, and she had nobody left to ask.

That is when a broker is useful.

"I can introduce you," I said, "to someone who will see to the wolf."

She laughed. It was a wet and hollow sound.

"And what does your someone want?"

"A name of weight."

"Take mine." She pressed her palm to her chest as though holding the rattle in. "It's all I have worth anything. Take it and bring me his heart in a jar."

---

She wrote it herself. I let her.

She put her own name on the collateral line in that careful upright hand, pressing so hard the nib nearly went through, and she pushed the paper across the table at me with her jaw set.

I looked at it for a long time.

It was a heavy name. It held every jar on her shelves. It would have served.

But her chest was bad, and she would never live to be old, and a name is only worth what it holds when the paper comes due. Would a dead woman's name hold anything at all by then?

I chose.

I drew bars through her name, neatly, and turned the paper so she could watch me do it.

"Not yours," I said. "Your daughter will have a child someday. That child will be a Marchand."

"So?"

"So she'll hold this house and every name in it. For far longer than you."

"She isn't born."

"Not yet."

"My daughter won't let a man near her."

"Then there's no weight on the line. You've lost nothing."

She said nothing.

"And if there is a child," I said, "you'll have bought your wolf with a name that comes due long after you. You'll never watch it paid."

---

She went to the door and took the salt from the shelf beside it.

She poured a line across the boards between us, over my feet, fine and even. She did not look at me while she did it. When she straightened, I found I could not step past that line toward her.

That was all it was for. So I would not come closer while she decided.

It took her a long time. I am very good at waiting.

Then she came back to the table and took up my pen, and could not make her hand write.

"You write it," she said. "Ondine. Write *Ondine*. It's what I'd name her, if anyone let me."

I wrote it for her, short and bright, in the space under the bars.

I have always thought that was the cleverest thing Honorine Marchand ever did, and the saddest, and I doubt she knew it was either.

---

The wolf was dead within the month.

I made the introduction the next evening, and the principal saw to it, and the wolf's name went into the widow's ledger in darker ink than the rest. She came around, afterward. She wrote me a note of thanks on good paper, and asked me never to come to her kitchen again, and I honored that until very recently.

The child has her mouth. I noticed it on the step.

I chose that name for a longer asset. A house of names, in a young woman's keeping, coming due long after anyone who might object was in the ground.

It seemed a very good choice at the time.

It still does.
