# 33 · Silas

"Mr. Roche," Cray says. "You're early. I did so hope you would be."

He's holding her up against his wall by the throat, her boots clear of the floor, and his voice is exactly the voice he used on her step with a bottle of wine hanging from his fist.

Her lips are going blue.

---

I read the room because I can't stop reading rooms, even now, with my palm split open and her face that color.

Salt across the threshold, broken where I came through it. Salt in a ring around the table, still whole. A knucklebone.

Three good cups on a tray, and one chair more than he set. The fourth chair sits at the foot of the table, empty, and every flame in the room leans toward it.

A presence sits in that chair. It has no interest in me. It's watching the paper.

On the paper, in the collateral line, in Cray's own patient slope, is his own name.

She did it. Fuck me, she actually did it.

And Cray is standing with his weight on his back foot. In all the nights I've watched this man from across his street, I've never seen him stand like that. Why would he? What has he ever had to be afraid of?

He's afraid now.

"Put her down."

"She's put me in the line, Mr. Roche." Polite. Reasonable. His grip doesn't shift. "Do you understand what she's done? Do you have the faintest idea what's sitting at the foot of that table?"

"Put her down."

"I'd like her to take it off first."

"She can't breathe, you bastard. She can't take off a fucking thing."

He considers that. He looks at her face, then at me.

Then he throws her.

He throws her across the room, low and hard, as easily as tossing a coat onto a chair, and she hits the floor inside the salt ring and rolls against the leg of the table. I hear her drag in air, one long ugly whoop of it, and then another.

She's breathing.

I don't look at her. If I look at her, he gets me.

---

He comes at me before the second breath is out of her.

I don't see him move. I read where he's going to be and I'm already turning when his fist lands on the side of my head, and it lands anyway. The room goes white. My shoulder hits the doorframe and the wood splits down its length.

He's fast. He's older than me and he's never had to prove it, and he proves it now.

I get my left arm up. He takes it by the wrist and turns it, courteously, as if he's helping me into a coat, and the bone goes with a crack I feel in my back teeth.

"I introduced you to a king," he says in my ear. "I could as easily have introduced you to the river."

I put my forehead into his nose.

It breaks. It's back in place before he's finished stepping away, but it breaks, and his blood is on my face, and it smells of clove with an older rot underneath.

He looks surprised. Nobody has hit him in a very long time.

My left wrist knits while I watch. My right palm doesn't. The burns I tore open on Ondine's ward hang off the heel of it in strips, and every time I close my fingers the old wounds scream at me.

So I fight him left-handed.

My blade is out. I don't remember drawing it. Plain steel, a bone grip, the knife I've carried since Aurelian bought me out of that yard.

I go in low, and he's gone. I go in again, and he's gone again, and his good cups go off the tray and burst on the floor, and he doesn't so much as glance at them.

"Stand still, you polite fuck."

"I'd rather not," he says.

He's drifting toward the table. Toward the paper.

If he breaks her salt ring, does her working hold? I don't know. I don't know anything about her craft except that she bleeds for it, and I'm not finding out tonight.

I throw myself between him and the ring.

His knee takes me under the ribs and I fold over it. A rib gives, then the one below it. I feel them go into the lung they're supposed to be keeping safe.

He brings his elbow down on the back of my neck, and I hit the floor a hand's width from her salt.

I don't touch it. I don't let so much as my sleeve cross the line.

---

He puts his shoe on my right wrist.

The thin sole. The one that's never known mud. He stands on the open burns with his whole weight and grinds, slow and patient, and I make a sound I haven't made since I was chained low with my eyes on the ground.

He crouches beside me. He's barely breathing hard. He takes my chin between his finger and thumb and turns my face up to his, gently, and his eyes are pleasant again. Almost fond.

"You were a dog," he says.

The room goes still.

Nobody has said it to my face since the yard, because nobody has ever been close enough. And he says it in the same flat tone the man with the kennel keys used every morning, never cruel, just accurate, a man naming property that belongs to someone else.

"I never cared for the term," Cray says. "I never once used it. But it was always true, Mr. Roche. You were a dog. You wore his collar and you called it a house. You'll wear mine and call it a crown. You were—"

"I was a dog."

My voice comes out steady. I don't know where from.

He stops.

I look up at him from his own floor, with his shoe on my ruined hand and her breathing behind me, and I say the rest of it.

"Never yours."

---

It's the first sentence about myself I've ever finished out loud.

It sits in the room with us. I can feel it there, finished and whole, like a knot pulled tight. It doesn't need anything added. It doesn't trail off into the floor. It ends where I ended it.

Cray looks at me as if I've undressed at his table.

And behind me, from the floor, Ondine speaks.

"It's dark," she rasps. Her voice is wrecked, barely there. "Look at the window, broker. The sun's down, and there's no moon in it."

The window behind the table is red going black. Past the roofs, the last light is draining out of a sky with nothing in it.

"The Marchand obligation falls due at the new moon," she says. She's up on one elbow in the salt, her bleeding thumb pressed flat to the ring. "You wrote that. Your hand. Collect him."

She isn't talking to Cray.

She's talking to the fourth chair.

---

Every flame in the room goes flat.

The cold comes down off the ceiling, river silt and wet stone and a candle burned to nothing, and the presence in the fourth chair takes its attention off the paper and puts it on the man crouched over me.

Cray feels it. It lands across his shoulders.

He takes his shoe off my wrist. He stands, slowly, and turns to face the foot of the table, and he tugs his waistcoat straight.

"I have always been courteous to you," he tells the chair. "Always. I have never once refused you anything. I am—"

He stops.

"I am Absalom—"

The name doesn't come.

I see him reach for it. I've watched a thousand men reach for things they dropped, and I know the look: the small frown, the fingers going to a pocket that's suddenly empty.

"Forgive me," he says to the chair, bewildered. "I seem to have mislaid—"

He can't finish it.

He tries again. His mouth shapes the start of a word and nothing follows it. He looks down at the paper on the table, at the collateral line, at his own patient slope, and I watch him read his own name and not know it.

"Who wrote that?" he says. "Whose writing is that?"

He's losing the end of every sentence about himself, and he's losing it in front of me.

How long have I lived like that? How many rooms have I walked out of with half of myself left on the floor?

I get up.

---

My ribs grind like gravel. My wrist is whole and my palm isn't, and I don't give a fuck about either.

I take him from behind, my good arm around his throat, and haul him backward away from the table. He doesn't fight me. He's still looking at the paper, still trying to read it.

I put him down on his knees on his own rug, on the stain where I bled on his brandy.

The front doorway fills.

Lucian first, in a black coat, wearing the face he wore in the chamber the night his uncle died. Selene behind him with her knife out and her eyes going everywhere at once, reading the salt, the chairs, the paper, Ondine on the floor. The Roche brothers fill the hall behind them, blocking the street.

Lucian looks at me with my blade at Cray's throat. He looks at Ondine. He looks at the fourth chair, and whatever he sees there sets his jaw hard.

"Under the crown," my king says. "Witnessed."

Selene says it after him, low, in her court voice. "Witnessed."

Cray lifts his face to me. His eyes have gone soft and puzzled and very young.

"Mr. Roche," he says. "Would you— I'd be so grateful if—"

He doesn't know what he'd be grateful for. He's lost that too.

I take his head.

---

It doesn't come easy, and it shouldn't. The Code wants it complete, because a partial cut on an Elder grows back, so I do it the only way it can be done: left-handed, my knee in his spine, the plain blade and every ounce of weight I have behind it.

It takes two strokes. The second goes through into his own floorboards.

The body folds onto the rug. The head rolls a short way and stops against the leg of the fourth chair.

And the chair lets go.

The cold lifts off the room.

It rises out of the chair and out of the salt and out of the walls, unhurried as a guest leaving a party it found tolerable, with somewhere else to be and no reason to rush there.

The lamp flames stand up straight. The air smells of clove and ash and blood, and nothing older.

It didn't lose. It finished here, and it went.

On the table, the paper curls at its edges. The collateral line goes blank. Then the consideration, then the date in old Marchand calendar, then Honorine's careful signature, letter by letter, until the page beside the knucklebone is nothing but old cotton stock with a faint brown stain where a dying woman's medicine once fell on it.

The holder was the collateral. There's nobody left for it to pass to.

It's void.

---

I'm on my knees beside her before I know I've moved.

She's sitting up in the salt with her back to the table leg and her boots splayed, her throat black with the shape of his fingers.

Her thumb is still bleeding. Her face is gray and streaked, and she's looking at the blank paper, and then at me.

"Witch." I don't recognize my own voice. "Breathe. Look at me and breathe."

"I'm breathing." It comes out a croak. "You finished a sentence."

"I know."

"About yourself."

"I know."

"I heard it." Her mouth wobbles. "I heard it, Silas. Out loud. In front of the goddamn King."

I take her face in my good palm and put my forehead to hers.

Behind me Selene is talking to Lucian, low. The Roche brothers are stepping over the broken door. Someone is lifting Cray's body off his own rug. I don't turn around for any of it.

"I can't remember her face," Ondine whispers.

"Whose?"

"Hers. My grandmother's. That's what it took. I paid it and I didn't even get to pick." She's crying now, the silent kind, with no breath behind it. "I can see the lace. I can see her fingers on the salt. I can't see her face."

I don't have anything to say to that. I don't have a sentence for it.

"I'm here, witch," is all I can manage. "I'm here."

I hold her and let her cry into my ruined shirt, and I read her the only way I know: where the weight goes, and where it's going next, and whether she's carrying something.

She's carrying all of it.

What do you give a woman who paid for her life with the face of the person who sold it? Two hundred years of trails haven't taught me that.

---

Selene finds the ledger in the writing desk under the window.

She brings it over and crouches beside us and opens it on her knee, so I can see without letting go of Ondine. Heavy stock, leather gone black at the spine. It isn't the register I took from Bywater. It's his own book, the broker's book: every introduction he ever made, and beside each one, in a column, the name the account was signing with that year. Hands that don't match. A patience that does.

Honorine is in there. So is the Aldric house, and Cassian, and names I don't know from cities I've never walked.

"He kept every one of them," Selene says.

Lucian stands over us with his coat open and blood on his cuffs that isn't his. He studies the ledger. Then he looks at me.

"It's still yours," he says. "The account. If you want it."

I look at the book on Selene's knee, at the first page, older than anything in this house, older than the King's house, in writing I've never seen.

Who keeps a column of names for that long and never gets tired of it?

"Yes," I say.

Then I look down at the woman in my arms, and the account can wait until the end of the world.

She lifts her head off my shoulder. She looks past me, at the body under Lucian's coat, at the blank paper, at the fourth chair standing empty at the foot of the table. Then she looks at me, and her swollen mouth moves.

"Take me home, Silas."
