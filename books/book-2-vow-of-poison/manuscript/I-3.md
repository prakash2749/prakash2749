# I-3 · Cray

*Placement: after Ch. 17.*

The catcher kept his dogs in a yard behind a feed store, north of the city, where the road went to mud.

A house on the canal wanted a tracker. A broker is only as good as his knowledge of who keeps what, and I knew the catcher kept something unusual. So I put on a good coat and my thinnest shoes and went up the mud road on a gray morning, and I stood at the edge of his pen.

The catcher smelled of tobacco and wet rope, and he was very proud of his yard.

"That one," he said, and pointed. "He'll bite."

---

The boy was chained low.

He was newly turned. I could see it in how he held himself, too still, as the new ones are before they learn to pretend to breathe. He crouched in the mud against the post with his arms around his knees, and his fists were wrapped in rags that had once been white.

Under the rags, the burns. I could smell them from the fence. Someone had turned him before the fire finished its work, and the skin would never be given the chance to heal. It would stay as it was, forever.

His eyes were on the ground.

He never looked at me, or at the catcher. He was reading the mud between his bare feet. I watched his gaze move over my prints where I had come up to the fence, then over the catcher's, then back to mine, and stop there.

He knew my shoes.

He had learned them from the ground in the time it took me to walk up.

"Doesn't talk," the catcher said. "Won't say a word since the fire."

"Does he track?"

"Best I've ever had." He spat. "Bites, though."

I waited. The boy did not bite. He crouched with his wrapped fists in his lap and his eyes on my shoes, so still that the flies walked on him.

I have seldom met stillness like that.

---

There were river men who would have paid a great deal more.

Smugglers, who would have put the boy in a skiff, pointed him at the dark, and used him until the marsh took him.

I could have introduced him to them. It would have been a very good introduction.

I chose the old King instead.

Why did I? I have asked myself, now and then, over the long years since. I believe it was the stillness. A house with a great many doors needs someone at them who does not fidget. A house with a great many rooms needs someone who reads them without being asked. The boy had already read the whole yard. He would have read a palace inside a week.

Would you send that into a swamp?

I would not.

So I made the introduction. The old King's man came up the mud road the next morning with the money, and the catcher counted it and unlocked the chain. The boy stood up out of the mud. He followed the King's man down the road with his eyes on the ground.

He never once looked back at the yard.

---

The catcher's clerk wrote the receipt in brown ink, at a table in the feed store, while the flies circled.

The catcher had a word for what he kept in his yard. He used it, and the clerk put it on the paper without looking up.

I have never cared for that word. I have never once used it.

It was always inaccurate, and inaccuracy offends me. But it was not my receipt to correct.

At the bottom, the clerk wrote my name, as the man who made the introduction. Then he folded it small and handed it to me, because brokers keep the paper.

I have kept it ever since, in a drawer, in a sleeve. I took it out now and then to look at it. A receipt is a key to a room not yet built. One keeps it, and waits to see whether the house goes up.

---

Tonight I took it out again and read it through in the lamplight. *Turned too soon. Tracks well. Holds what he's told to hold.*

*Does not speak of it.*

He still doesn't. I have stood behind an oak on a canal road and watched him not speak of it every night of a long season. He carries it as though it were part of his neck.

The witch refused me today, on her porch, with a paring knife in her fist. She told me more than once to get off a step I was not standing on. I admired it enormously.

Then she went inside and said his name, with the shutters open, over and over, until my men at the gate did not know where to look.

It was a very clear message.

So I will send him mine.

I put the receipt in an envelope with a brief note, sealed it with my mark, and chose where to leave it. Somewhere he will go looking for something else, and find it, and carry it home unread against his chest, as he carries everything.

He will read it eventually. They always do.

And then he will come to me, because he will want to know who stood at the edge of the pen.

I will tell him.
