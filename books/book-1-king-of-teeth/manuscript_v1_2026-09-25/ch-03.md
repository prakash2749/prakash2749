# 3 · Lucian

The council wants a war and hasn't said so yet.

I can tell because they've dressed for it. There's a formality in the room this morning that wasn't here last week — collars done, seats taken by rank, and nobody's glass has been touched. When vampires stop drinking in a room together they've already decided what the room is for.

Marguerite has given me two days since the last conversation. That's all the patience she has and she's used it properly, and now she's at the long table with a quorum and a face that says she's finished waiting.

"Barataria," she says.

"Yes."

"Six of Gaspard's were seen north of the canal last night. Armed. Three of them shifted."

"In the city?"

"On Tchoupitoulas." She folds her hands on the table. "Tchoupitoulas is not contested ground. It is not neutral ground. It is a street where our people keep houses, and three wolves ran down it with their teeth out."

Somebody at the end of the table says, "Traditionally—"

"Be quiet," I tell him.

He's quiet. The room adjusts around the silence, and I let it go long enough to mean something.

"How many of ours saw them?" I ask.

"Eight," Marguerite says. "One of them is Aldric's man. He'll want it in writing."

"He'll get it."

"He'll want more than writing, Lucian."

"I know what he wants."

---

Here's what the room wants, and none of them will put it in one sentence.

They want a strike. Barataria. Hard enough to hurt, fast enough that the pack can't call it a battle, targeted enough that the humans don't fucking see it.

It's a good plan. I'd have written it myself, and the man who drafted it — because somebody in this room has drafted it, probably two of them — knows his way around a map.

But a plan is a door, and the question about a door is always who gets to walk through it afterward.

If I send the court into Barataria tonight, the next thing that happens is a vote. War. Formal, recorded, and irreversible. Every House that's been waiting for a Croix to stop talking and start biting gets what they want, and the question of who killed my father becomes something nobody is permitted to reopen.

I've told Marguerite this. I've told her in one sentence.

She doesn't disagree. She's spent eight hundred years not disagreeing with kings, and she's very good at it.

"What are you proposing?" she asks.

"A retaliation. Not a war."

The room shifts.

"Targeted," I say. "Fighters only. Nobody who isn't armed. Nobody in a house. Nobody on the docks after the boats are in."

"That's a list of things you're not doing," says a man named Aldric, a cousin of a cousin, who has been waiting for this sentence since before I walked in. "What are you doing?"

"I'm going into Barataria with Silas and taking back what they took from us on Tchoupitoulas."

"That's a raid. That's not a damn retaliation."

"It's a raid at the volume I choose." I look at him. "And it's mine. Not yours."

"The court—"

"The court can witness it or wait for the report. Either way, the scope is mine."

Marguerite watches me from behind her hands. She has lived through every argument this room has ever had, and she is measuring this one against all of them.

"You're drawing a line," she says.

"I'm drawing a line."

"On our own retaliation."

"Yes."

"Your father—"

"Would have drawn the same one."

That lands in the room like a hand on a table. A few of them agree with me, which is useful. A few of them think I'm quoting a dead man's philosophy because I haven't built my own yet, which is also useful.

One of them — the Aldric cousin — thinks I'm weak.

Fuck him. I can work with that.

---

Silas is in the hall when I come out. He doesn't ask how it went, because Silas has never asked how anything went. He reads the room by the way people come out of it.

"Tonight," I tell him.

"How many?"

"You, me, and six of ours. Light."

He nods. His hands are in his coat and his eyes are on the door behind me, reading who's coming out after and in what order.

"Rules of engagement," he says. It's not a question. He's heard there are some and he wants them before the hall fills.

"Armed wolves only. Nothing civilian. Nothing domestic." I stop at the window. "If a man puts his hands down and goes back, he walks."

Silas's jaw shifts a quarter inch. Not objection. Recognition — he's placing this on a list of orders he's been given over two centuries, and it's landing near the top of a column he didn't expect me to be in.

"Sire."

"Don't."

"It's a good list." He says it the way he says everything — like a man who used his last extra word sometime around 1850. "Aurelian had one."

"I'm aware."

"His was longer."

"I'm aware of that too."

He almost smiles. On Silas that's a loosening around the jaw that lasts about as long as a match.

"The Marchand house," he says.

I look at him.

"Silas."

"It's on the route. Between the canal and the water." His eyes go to the window. "I'd like to skip it."

"Why?"

He doesn't answer. His jaw does the thing where it goes from loose to something else, and his hands come out of his coat and go back in.

"Because it's neutral ground," he says. "Ondine Marchand has kept it neutral for twenty years and she's never asked us for a thing, and I'd rather not put men through her yard."

"Fine. Skip it."

"Thank you."

He goes. Fast, the way he always goes — a man who has never learned to leave a room slowly.

I stand at the window and watch the courtyard. The morning has that clean look it gets after rain, and there's a delivery van at the kitchen entrance, and the driver is a man who has no idea whose breakfast he's carrying.

---

Marguerite finds me at the stair.

She's alone, which means she's about to say something the council would want to hear and she doesn't want them to hear her saying it.

"You're right to keep it small," she says.

"Thank you."

"I'm not complimenting you. I'm telling you I agree, so that later, when someone in that room claims I didn't, you can say otherwise." She adjusts her cuffs. The gesture is so like her brother's that for a moment the stairwell has someone else in it. "How long can you hold the line?"

"Depends on what comes back."

"What comes back is what always comes back. Dead wolves and a court that liked the taste of it." She looks up at me. "Your father held it for thirty years after the truce. He held it by being in the room every single time someone wanted to open the door wider. If you're not in that room, someone else will be."

"I'll be in it."

"You'll be in Barataria tonight."

"And in the room tomorrow."

She considers that. Then she says something that has no business in a conversation about a raid, and it passes between us without either of us stopping to look at it.

"We don't leave," she says. "This city. This house. Whatever happens with the wolves, we are not going anywhere. If someone in that room suggests relocating the court, even temporarily, you put that down. Immediately. Do you understand me?"

"Nobody's going to suggest—"

"Someone will." Her face is calm about it. "They always do, when there's blood. Someone will say it would be prudent to move operations to Baton Rouge for a season, or to the Roche estate, or to anywhere that isn't here. And you will tell them no."

"Why would they want to leave?"

"They won't want to. That's not the danger." She's already moving past me down the stair. "The danger is that someone proposes it who sounds reasonable, and the court agrees because the court is frightened, and then we are not here. And we have always been here."

She says it the way you'd say the walls are stone. A fact so established it has stopped needing a reason.

I don't ask for one. Fuck — it doesn't occur to me to ask for one.

---

By evening the house smells like preparation.

Not weapons — vampires don't carry much and what we carry doesn't smell. It's the absence of the ordinary sounds. No staff in the hallways. No kitchen noise. The household has been told to keep to the east wing, and the ones who've been here long enough to know what that means have gone quiet about it.

Silas is in the courtyard with six of our people. He's chosen well — experienced, fast, and not one of them has the look of a man who wants to be seen doing something.

That matters. The fucking court is watching from the upper windows. Aldric's man is probably counting heads.

I come down and Silas hands me my coat. There's a weight in the left pocket that's been there since Tuesday, and I put my hand on it the way I've been putting my hand on it for days.

My father's blade. Keyed to his blood, useless against anyone but him, and he is already dead.

I carry it because I cannot put the damn thing down.

"Route?" I say.

"Canal to the bayou. We go in by water. The dock at Lafitte's." Silas doesn't look at a map because Silas has never looked at a map. "Straight through the settlement, south to the levee, and out the same way."

"The Marchand house?"

"Wide."

"Good."

I look at the six of them. Young, most of them. Young for our kind, which means they were alive when the truce was signed and have spent forty years being told it mattered.

"Armed wolves," I tell them. "Nothing else. Nobody in a house. Nobody who puts their hands down."

One of them nods. The others wait.

"If you can't tell," I say, "don't."

We go.
