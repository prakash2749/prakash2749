# 2 · Selene

The man at the end of the bar says *condolences* and means *opportunity.*

I know because the word has two shapes. In English it's a hand on a shoulder. In wolf it's the sound a door makes when it unlocks.

He's been here an hour. Good suit, no tie, and a bourbon he ordered by name and hasn't damn well touched. He's watching the room the way they all watch rooms this week — like a man trying to work out which chair is about to be empty.

I pour him a refill he didn't ask for because it keeps me close enough to hear.

"Terrible thing," he says to the woman beside him. "A king like that. In his own house."

She nods. She doesn't know what she's nodding at.

"Makes you wonder what's next," he says. "For all of us."

In wolf, *all of us* has a fence around it. It means the pack, the blood, the people who sleep under the same noise. It does not mean the woman on the next stool.

He means his people. He means that a door has come off its hinges somewhere above him and he's here to see what falls through.

I wipe the bar in front of him and he doesn't see me do it.

Nobody does.

---

My name is Selene Ward and I work at a bar on Frenchmen Street where the tips are good and nobody has ever once asked me a question I couldn't answer with a bottle.

I've been here two years. Before that I was an assistant to a woman who translated contracts for the port authority, and before that I was a girl in a house off Elysian Fields with two parents and a yard.

The house is gone. My parents are in the ground on the human side of a cemetery that has another side nobody talks about. The translation work dried up when my boss moved to Houston, and she offered to take me with her, and I said no, and she looked at me like I'd said something in a language she didn't speak.

I couldn't leave. True answer. Damn useless one.

Something in this city has its hand on the back of my neck and I have never examined what the hell it is. Shit, maybe I don't want to. I stayed because I stayed, the way people stay — not because it's good, because it's theirs.

And because Rémy Thibodeaux lives on a shrimp boat in Barataria, and Barataria is across the water, and you can't kill a man from Houston.

---

The bar fills up around nine the way it always does.

Frenchmen after dark is a machine that runs on music and liquor and the specific willingness of tourists to believe that whatever happened to them last night was a good time. I've worked it long enough to hear the gears.

There's a grammar to a room like this. Two of them, actually.

The first one is English, which is what they're all speaking, and in English this is a bar where people come to drink and listen to a band and leave their credit cards open.

The second one is underneath it. Not a language anyone in here would name. More like a current — who is watching whom, and from where, and the small shifts in a room when something with teeth walks in and every human body adjusts without being told to.

I hear both. I've always heard both.

My mother gave me that. Or whatever she gave me that I don't have a name for — the thing that makes wolves stare at me for a beat too long before they decide to be somewhere else.

It isn't a gift. A gift is something you can put down.

---

There's a woman at the door. Wolf.

I catch it the way I always catch it — not a smell exactly, more like a sound at a frequency I can almost place. Something in the back of my head sits up and pays attention before I've made a decision to.

She's young. My age, maybe younger. Good coat, and she holds the door for a man behind her, which is a wolf thing — the person closer to the outside holds the barrier, not the person closer to the inside. In English that's courtesy. In pack it's perimeter.

She gets three steps into the room before she catches whatever I am on the air.

Her nose goes up. Not a full turn — a quarter-inch lift, like somebody catching a bad note in a song.

Then she looks at me.

I've been looked at like that by wolves my whole life. It isn't hatred. Hatred would be a goddamn improvement. It's the face a person makes when they bite into something that tastes like two things at once and can't work out which one is off.

And here's the part I don't tell anyone.

My body reaches for her. Not my hands — something underneath my hands. The thing under my skin that has been reaching for wolves since I was old enough to stand in a room with one. It reaches the way a plant reaches for a window — not because it chose to, but because the reaching is what it does.

It reaches, and she flinches, and the reaching falls short, and the distance between us fills up with the ordinary air of a bar on Frenchmen Street.

Twenty-five years of that. Twenty-five years of my body saying *there, that one, go* and the other body saying *wrong, spoiled, away.* I have been refused in a language I was born speaking, and the refusal never gets quieter. It just gets structural. God, it gets structural.

She turns and says something to the man behind her. He shrugs. They find a table near the window, away from the bar.

Away from me.

In wolf, that isn't a slight. It's a quarantine.

I finish the pour I'm working on and I don't spill it and my hands are steady and I'd like somebody to write that down somewhere, because it's taken me years to get here.

---

After midnight the crowd turns over and I take my break in the alley behind the kitchen.

It smells like grease and rain. There's a crate I've been sitting on for two years that has my exact shape in it by now.

I take my phone out. No messages, which is every night. I have three numbers that aren't the bar. My landlord. My dentist. A woman in Tremé who sells me the tea my mother used to make, and who has never once asked me why I buy it.

The whole inventory. I'm not feeling sorry for myself. I'm being accurate. Shit inventory, but accurate. No shit it's thin.

My father was a translator. He did contracts — shipping, some legal work, court business he never brought home. He was good at it. He had a gift for hearing what a sentence meant underneath what it said, and he taught me to hear it too, sitting at the kitchen table with two versions of the same document and a pencil he never sharpened.

"What did they say?" he'd ask.

And I'd tell him.

"Now. What did they *mean*?"

A pencil, a habit, and a bar where I practice it on strangers. The whole inheritance.

And a ledger. His work ledger — every job, every client, every fee, written in a hand so neat it looks printed. I've had it since the funeral. I've never opened it past the first page, because the first page has his handwriting on it, and his handwriting is the last thing of his I'm not ready for.

---

My mother was a wolf.

She was born Marisol Boucher, and she left the Thibodeaux pack to be with my father, which in pack custom is not a departure. It's a contamination.

There's no word for what she did in English. English says *she left.* In wolf there's a word that means the thing that happens to a limb the body cuts off — not removed, *rejected.* The blood stops going there and the flesh makes its own decision.

She stopped being pack the day she chose a human man, and nobody told her, because nobody had to. The blood just stopped.

I got her face. I got whatever it is that makes my nose work sometimes and never when it counts. I got the thing that wolves smell when they pass me — not *wolf,* not *human,* something else. Something unfinished.

I didn't get the shift.

That's the sentence and I'm not going to dress it up. Wolves shift. I don't. Never have. Something in me reaches for it the way a tongue reaches for a missing tooth, and nothing is there, and nothing will ever be there.

My mother could run four-legged through Barataria in the dark. Her daughter can tend bar and hear both sides of a conversation.

That's what came down.

---

They were killed four years ago.

Rémy Thibodeaux came to the house on a Thursday. He was the Alpha's son, twenty-seven, big, and he did it with his hands, and my mother was between him and the door, so he did her first.

I know this because everyone in Barataria knows it. It's the kind of story that travels through a pack the way weather does — everybody gets wet and nobody says where the cloud was.

What nobody says, because nobody thinks it's a question, is *why.*

A pack son doesn't kill a woman his own father's age for leaving. That's not how the hell it works. Pack has a long memory and a short arm — they stop calling, they stop looking, and the blood makes its own decision. Killing would be an acknowledgment, and an acknowledgment would be a door opened back up.

So why.

The official version — the one I've heard in pack grammar from three different wolves in three different bars — is that my father was into something. That he had his hands in business that got people hurt. That Rémy did what was necessary.

In wolf, *necessary* is the same word as *clean.* Both mean: there is no further obligation.

That word has kept me awake ever since. Not because it's wrong.

Because nobody ever says what the business was.

---

Here's what I've built.

A job. A routine. A body that can handle a double shift and a walk home at three in the morning through a city that is not as safe as it has decided to be.

A knife in my bag that used to be my father's. A sharpness to it I maintain the way other people maintain a car.

And an intention so clean I can see my own face in it.

I'm going to kill Rémy Thibodeaux. Not in a fantasy. Not as a comfort I take out and hold at night and put back in the damn morning.

As a plan. With steps. Steps I've been taking for four years, each one small enough to look like a life.

Learn the pack's grammar. Learn their schedules, their customs, the days they come north and the days they stay on the water. Learn which bars they drink at, and who talks too freely, and what a wolf says when a wolf has decided you don't matter enough to be careful around.

I've done all of that. Every bit of it. I am fluent in a language that does not want me.

What I don't have is a way in.

Barataria is pack ground. A wolf who smells wrong doesn't cross the water without being noticed, and a woman who can't shift is a woman who can't leave the way she came if it goes bad.

I need a door. I've been waiting for one.

---

I go back behind the bar and work the last three hours of the night, and the city does what it does. Tourists leave in twos and threes. The band plays its last set to a room that's stopped listening. A man pays his tab in cash and asks whether I'm from here.

"Born here," I say.

"You don't sound like it."

No. I don't. I sound like both places and neither one, which is the whole of me in a sentence, and he doesn't notice because the observation has already left him.

Antoine is closing out the register. He tells me the King is dead, as though I haven't heard it from every person in this room tonight.

"Terrible thing," he says.

"Yeah."

"You know what's gonna happen, right? With the wolves?"

"Something."

"Something *bad.*" He shuts the drawer. "Their boy's been down there all week. The son. Hell, people are talking."

I keep my hands on the rag and I keep my face on the glass I'm wiping.

"Which son?"

"Thibodeaux's kid. Rémy." Antoine shrugs. "Somebody saw him at the fuel dock yesterday. Fueling the boat, not going anywhere. Just — there."

In wolf, *staying visible* is the opposite of hiding. It's a declaration. You put yourself where people can see you because you want it understood that you are not ashamed of the ground you're standing on.

He's in the city this week. He's not running.

He's right across the water.
