# 5 · Lucian

The first wolf breaks before I reach him.

Not his body. His nerve. He comes out of a house on the levee side with a rifle — a rifle, which is so fucking human it almost earns him the time to use it — and he sees me, and his whole stance changes.

Fuck. I've seen it in men before. A decision unmakes itself in the middle of a body.

He drops the gun and shifts. The shift takes him two heartbeats and by the second one he's moving, going west, going fast, going away.

I let him.

Silas is at my left. He doesn't ask and doesn't need to. We've done this before, in other years, in other places, and the grammar of it is the same every time — I set the pace and he reads the ground.

The settlement is small and badly lit. Shell roads, tin roofs, houses built close enough to pass a cup of sugar. Dogs barking. A generator running somewhere on the far side.

It's a place where people live, and I am in it with six of my people and a set of rules that exist because I damn well said them out loud.

Armed wolves only.

The line. I drew it and I'm standing on it, and the night is about to test whether I mean it.

---

The second one is in the road.

Shifted already, which means he heard us coming. He's big. Rust-colored, broad across the chest. His ears are flat and his head is low and he's not running.

He's between us and a house with a light on in the kitchen.

I know what that is. In his language it's probably something with a word I don't speak, and in mine it's a man standing in a doorway.

I don't run at him. I walk.

I walk toward him the way you walk toward a door you intend to open — steady, unhurried, with the particular patience of a thing that has been alive for four centuries and has never once needed to rush.

His ears go flat. His haunches drop. Every line of him says *charge,* and every line of him stays where it is, because something in the walking is worse than the running. A man who runs at you has lost control. A man who walks toward you has decided, calmly, what's going to happen, and the calm is the thing you can't fight.

He doesn't charge.

He shifts. The bones rearrange under his skin and he's a man, naked, breathing hard, with his back against the wall of the house with the kitchen light on.

I put my hand flat on his chest.

Not a strike. Not a grip. My palm, open, against his sternum. The way you'd put your hand on a door to test whether it's locked.

I push. Gently.

He goes backward. Not because I pushed hard — because the wall went first. The clapboard buckles behind his shoulders. The studs give. His body goes into the wall and the wall opens around him like something soft, and he's standing in the gap of his own house with my hand on his chest and the kitchen light on his face.

I lean in close enough that he can feel my breath.

"There are people in that house who are not armed," I say. "I am not going through that door."

I take my hand off his chest and step back.

He stays in the wall. He stays in it for a long time after I've gone, and the kitchen light stays on, and somebody inside that house knows exactly what just happened to the man who was standing between them and the road.

My people take that image home. Not the violence — the restraint. A hand that could have gone through a man's chest, placed flat and pushed gently, and the wall giving before the wolf did.

The rules make it worse. Nobody in this city understands this about my father's philosophy. A man who could tear a room apart and instead walks slowly toward you is scarier than the man who charges. The rules aren't a leash. They're the proof that there's something that needs one.

The man in the road stays there. The house behind him keeps its light on.

---

Silas moves through the settlement the way water moves through a drain — always downhill, always toward the thing that hasn't been checked.

He reads the ground. Not distance. Direction. A footprint in the shell means a man was going somewhere, and where a man was going is what Silas wants to know.

He stops at a line of houses near the south end. Four of them, close together, with the same roofline and the same dog tied to the same tree.

He walks past three and skips the fourth without breaking stride.

I notice. I don't ask.

There are things Silas does that I've stopped asking about, because the answers he gives are always true and never complete, and a man who gives you the truth in pieces is a man who has decided which pieces are yours.

He skips the house and we go south, and the night opens up into bayou, and behind us the settlement is making the sounds a settlement makes when something has come through it.

---

They find us at the levee.

Three of them, shifted, coming out of the water where the land goes soft. They're not strays — they're organized, moving together, and the one in front is old enough to know what he's looking at.

The young one doesn't wait.

He comes out of the water fast, low, mud flying off his haunches, and he gets one of mine by the leg. I hear the cloth tear and the man scream.

I'm there before the scream finishes.

I take the wolf by the scruff — two hundred pounds of soaked fur and muscle — and lift him off my man the way you'd lift a dog off a table. He twists in my grip. His jaws snap at the air beside my face, close enough that I feel the heat of his breath.

I throw him. He hits the levee slope twenty feet away and rolls, and by the time he gets his feet under him he's looking at me from the ground with his ears flat and his belly in the mud.

He doesn't come back.

The old one in front stops. He puts himself between me and the remaining wolf, and there's something in the set of his head that isn't attack and isn't retreat.

He's reading me.

I let him read me.

It takes him about as long as a held breath, and then he looks at the ground behind me — at the road, at the houses, at the lights that are still on in all of them — and something changes in his stance.

The wolves behind him are waiting for a signal. He doesn't give one.

Instead he shifts back.

He's old. Fifty, maybe, with a face that's been out in weather for all of it. He's got a scar down one arm that was stitched badly and healed worse.

He spits on the ground between us.

"King of teeth," he says.

I know what it means. It's the wolf name for what we are — a king who rules by appetite. A man with nothing in him but the bite.

It's an insult. It has teeth of its own.

I show him mine.

Not a smile. Not the court expression, not the careful arrangement of a face that has learned to look human. I pull my lips back and I show him what's behind them — the full set, upper and lower, the ones that come down when the feeding starts. The ones my father kept hidden for three centuries because showing them was the one thing the Code never had to prohibit. Nobody did it. Nobody needed a rule against it, because nobody who'd seen them wanted to see them twice.

The old wolf takes a step back. Involuntary. The first honest movement he's made since the water.

I hold it for two seconds. Then I close my mouth and the face goes back to being a face, and the man in the mud is looking at me the way you look at a room you thought you understood and just found a wall missing.

I've been called worse by people I loved. He can have the name. I'll keep the teeth.

"You can have the levee," I tell him. "I'm done."

He doesn't thank me. He doesn't curse me. He turns and goes back into the water, and the two behind him follow, and they disappear into the bayou the way things disappear in the bayou — completely, as though they were never the thing you were looking at.

*King of teeth.*

Fuck him. I put it somewhere I can find it later.

---

We come back through the center of the settlement, and it's quiet now. The kind of quiet that happens after — not silence, which is the absence of sound, but the held breath of people standing very still inside houses and listening for what comes next.

Silas is counting. Not out loud — he counts with his eyes, tracking the houses we've passed, the wolves we've seen, the ones who ran and the ones who stayed.

"One unaccounted for," he says.

"Where."

"The dock. South slip." He nods toward the water. "Young. Armed. He was outside when we came in and he went into the shed."

"Still there?"

"Still there."

I consider the shed. It's at the end of the dock, tin-roofed, with a door that's standing ajar.

"Take him," I say. "Alive. If he comes out quiet, he stays quiet."

Silas goes. I watch him move down the dock in the dark, and he's fast, and he's silent, and I've never once seen him be uncertain about where to put his feet.

The man in the shed comes out alive.

He's young — forty, maybe. A pup by pack standards. He's got his hands up and Silas has his collar, and the man's face is the face of somebody who has just found out what his night is going to be about.

"What's your name," I say.

"Thibault." He swallows. "I was on the dock. I wasn't armed. I wasn't fucking—"

"You were outside with a knife on your belt."

His mouth opens and closes.

"That's armed," I say. "In my house, that's armed. Is it different in yours?"

He doesn't answer.

"Thibault." I lean down. "You were on this dock the night my father died. You were at the council meeting. You came in through the gallery with the rest of them."

His whole face goes slack — mouth open, eyes wide, the kind of naked recognition that I can read without any language at all.

"I was there," he says. "Yes."

"Then you're coming with me."

Silas puts him in the boat without asking whether there's a reason, because Silas has never once needed a reason to be supplied out loud. He reads the shape of a decision the way other men read weather.

---

We're on the water heading back when one of my people, the youngest, says it.

"Clean."

He means the raid. No dead, no burned houses, no women pulled out of beds. He says it with something close to pride, and the others take it the same way.

I don't answer.

Clean isn't the word. The word is *chosen* — I chose what to leave standing, which means I could have chosen otherwise, and every wolf in that settlement is awake right now knowing it.

That's not mercy. My father did mercy. What I've done tonight is carpentry — a precise amount of violence applied to the joints.

The message is: I drew a line and I stayed on it.

The cost of the message is that the line was mine to draw.

---

Thibault sits in the bow of the boat with his wrists tied and his head down. He hasn't said a word since the dock.

I look at the back of his head and think about what's in it.

He was in the room. He was in the gallery when the windows came down and the wolves came through, and he was there when my father was still standing, and he was there after.

If there's something to know about the last few minutes of my father's life, this man was close enough to know it.

I could ask him now. The water is dark and the engine is loud and nobody on either shore would hear a word of it.

But a question asked in the dark gets a dark answer. A man who tells you something because he's afraid of the water will tell you something else when the damn water is gone.

I want him under a roof with a lamp on and time to think about what the truth costs compared to the alternative.

My father would have asked on the boat. He would have put his hand on the man's shoulder and spoken to him the way he spoke to everyone — quietly, directly, with a warmth in it that made people forget they were answering a king.

I don't have that. Whatever gene carries that particular gift, it went into the damn ground with him.

I have a lamp and a locked room and the patience to wait.

The city comes up out of the dark ahead of us. Lights on the shore, the bridge lit up, and somewhere in the Quarter a house with a door that's been unlocked since Tuesday.

Silas brings the boat in. We tie off.

I take the prisoner by the arm and walk him up the dock, and behind us the six of my people go home to whatever they have, and not one of them knows what I want from the man at the end of my hand.

They did their job. I'm about to do mine.
