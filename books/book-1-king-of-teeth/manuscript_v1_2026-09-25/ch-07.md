# 7 · Lucian

The wolf has been waiting to be killed for two days, and he's stopped expecting it.

I'm watching for it — the change that happens in a body that has been braced for something that doesn't come — the shoulders drop first, and then the jaw, and then the eyes start looking at things in the room instead of looking at the room.

He's looking at the lamp. He's been looking at it for a while.

I had him put in the east basement, which is a room with a door and a chair and nothing else. No window, no fixture on the wall, nothing he could use and nothing to look at.

Except the lamp, which Silas brought down on the first night because the wolf couldn't see in the dark, and which I did not order removed, because a man who can see the room he's in is a man who knows he's being held by someone who thought of his eyes.

That's the door I walked him through. Not a fist. A lamp.

---

His name is Thibault. Forty-seven. A dock worker and sometime fisherman and, on the night my father died, one of about thirty wolves who came through the gallery windows of the council chamber.

He has told me this. He told me on the first night, sitting in the chair with his wrists tied and the lamp on the floor between us, and he told it the way a man tells something he's said before — to himself, in the dark, going over it until the edges of it went smooth.

"I was in the gallery. I came through the third window on the east side. The glass was already down."

"Who broke it?"

"The boys in front. I don't know their names. They were young."

He said *boys* and meant it. His voice went a certain way on the word.

I left him after that. Gave him water, a blanket, the lamp. Let him sit in a room with nothing to do but think about the difference between being killed and being kept.

Now it's two days later and he's looking at the lamp, and I come in and shut the door behind me.

He looks up.

"I thought you forgot about me," he says.

"No."

"That's worse, probably."

"Probably."

I take the chair from the corner — the second chair, the one Silas put in this morning because I asked him to — and I sit down across from the wolf at a distance that says *conversation,* not *interrogation.*

He notices. He's not stupid. Stupid wolves don't survive to forty-seven in a pack that shares a fucking city with us.

"You've been here two days," I say. "You've been fed. You have a light."

"I noticed."

"Do you know why?"

"Because you want something from me that hitting wouldn't get."

There it is. Damn him. Not stupid at all.

I pick up the lamp. The base is cast iron — heavy, old, the kind of thing you'd need two hands to carry.

I close one hand around it and squeeze. The iron deforms under my fingers. It folds inward with a sound like a can being crushed, and when I set it back down, the base has the shape of my grip pressed into it.

The lamp stays on. The light doesn't flicker.

Thibault looks at the lamp. Then he looks at my hand — the hand that just reshaped iron the way a man reshapes clay.

"That's not a threat," I say. "That's a fact about the room you're in. The threat is outside the room, and it wears a council's face, and it has been asking me to give you to it for two days."

He swallows.

"Tell me about the chamber," I say. "Start from the windows."

---

He tells it. Not the same way he told it the first night — this version has air in it, pauses where he stops to check what he actually saw against what he was told he saw.

"We came through fast. The whole point was speed — get in, get to the platform, do the damage, get out."

"Who gave the order?"

"Gaspard." No hesitation. "The Alpha. It came from the top."

"And the target?"

"The chamber. The council. Not one man — all of them." He rubs his wrists where the ties were. "The idea was chaos. Enough of us coming through at once that nobody could organize."

"That worked."

"For about a minute." His jaw sets. The muscle in his cheek pulls tight. "Then your father stood up."

He says it the way you'd say *then the weather changed.*

"He was at the table when we came in. Sitting. I saw him from the gallery rail — he had a glass in front of him and his hand was on the arm of the chair, and he didn't move."

"He didn't move."

"Not right away. Everybody around him was moving. Chairs going over, people going for the exits. And he sat there."

Thibault looks at the lamp.

"Then he stood up, and it was over."

"What do you mean, over?"

"I mean the ones who were close to him stopped being close to him." A pause. "The ones who were closest stopped being anything."

I know this. I've read the account. I've read Marguerite's account and Cassian's account and the account of three Elders who were in the room, and all of them say the same thing in different words — that my father rose from his chair and the wolves nearest him died so fast that the ones behind them didn't know it had happened.

But none of them told it the way this man is telling it.

"He was beautiful," Thibault says.

The word goes into the room and sits down.

"I'm a wolf. I'm supposed to say he was a monster, or that I was frightened, and I was frightened, but that isn't the word for it." He looks at me. "A man that old, moving like that. I'd never seen anything like it. None of us had."

"Keep going."

"We pulled back. Not a retreat — there wasn't time for a retreat. The boys in front were done. The ones behind them turned and went for the windows. I went with them."

"And my father?"

"Still standing. He'd taken bites — I could see the blood on his shirt. Both arms. One at the hip. He didn't seem to notice any of it."

"He was healing."

"He was healing while we watched." Thibault shakes his head. "I saw a wound on his forearm close up in the time it took me to get to the rail. That arm was open to the bone and it sealed like a zipper."

"Then what killed him?"

Thibault goes quiet.

This is the part. I've been waiting two days for this part, and he's been waiting two days to be asked, and we've both known it was coming since the dock.

"He was still standing when we quit," Thibault says. "He was standing, and he was healing, and the last of our people were going back through the windows. It was over."

Fuck.

"And?"

"And he turned."

"Which direction?"

"Away from us." The wolf's voice has gone careful. "Toward the east side of the chamber. Toward someone behind him."

"Someone."

"I couldn't see — I was on the gallery rail, going out. But I saw his face."

"What was in it?"

"Not fear." Thibault looks at me and his eyes are steady. "Surprise. He looked at somebody behind him the way you look at a man who's come through a door he's allowed to come through, and then he looked *down,* and I went through the window."

The room has no sound in it. The lamp hums.

"You saw him turn toward someone," I say. "Behind him. On the east side."

"Yes."

"Not a wolf."

"Our people were done. We were out or we were dead. There was nobody behind him in a wolf's body." He stops. "And the smell."

"What smell."

"I can't be sure. The room was — there was blood everywhere, it was hard to sort. But I've been in rooms with your kind before. There's a specific thing. Old ones have more of it."

"And you caught that."

"At the turn. When he turned." Thibault rubs his jaw. "It was close to him. Not across the room. Right there."

---

I sit with that for a while.

He watches me sit with it. He's given me the thing he has and now he's waiting to find out what it costs.

The smart thing — the clean thing, the thing every bastard Elder at my court would advise — is to take this man's head off his shoulders and put the story in the ground with him.

Because what he's just told me changes the whole fucking shape of the room.

Fuck.

Wolves came through the gallery. My father killed the ones in front and the rest ran. He was standing, he was healing, and the fight was over.

Then he turned toward someone behind him — someone he wasn't afraid of, someone he expected to be there — and that someone put him down.

Not a wolf. The scent was wrong for that.

One of ours.

I've been asking *who was in the room* since Tuesday. I've been asking it about the gallery, about the windows, about the wolves who came through them.

I've been asking the wrong damn door.

---

"You're wondering whether to kill me," he says.

"Yes."

"Figured." He doesn't look away. "I'd like to make a case for not."

"Go ahead."

"You could take my head off right now and nobody would think twice. A wolf captured during a raid, killed in custody — it happens. The pack would be angry and the pack is always angry, and it would go in a ledger somewhere and that would be the end of it."

"That's the case for."

"That's the case for. Here's the other one." He leans forward. "I just told you something about your father's death that nobody else is going to tell you. Nobody in your damn court was looking at him when it happened — they were looking at us. And nobody in my pack got close enough to see what I saw, because the ones who got close are dead."

"You're making yourself valuable."

"I'm making myself accurate." He holds my eyes. "I was on the gallery rail going through a window and I saw your father turn toward someone he wasn't afraid of. That's what I have. It's the only thing I have, and if you kill me it goes away."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you have a witness. You have somebody who saw it from outside the room, who has no stake in the answer, and who can say it again in front of anybody you put him in front of."

The lamp flickers. Something in the wiring, probably. It casts his face in two halves for a moment and then steadies.

I could kill him. I'm thinking about it clearly, the way you think about a door you could close. He's right that nobody would question it. He's right that the pack would fold it into the quiet and move on.

But a dead wolf tells one story. A live wolf tells the one I fucking choose.

"We didn't kill your father," Thibault says.

The sentence goes through me like a blade drawn across a nerve.

Not the words. The words I expected. It's the *we* — a wolf, sitting in a basement in a vampire's house, using a word that means *my people* in his language and *the enemy* in mine.

He's telling me his own side didn't do it.

Not out of loyalty to me. Not out of fear, which went out of him yesterday around noon. He's telling me because he was on a rail going through a window and he saw something, and the something has been sitting in him for a month, and he came to my city with it already in his mouth.

"We didn't kill your father," he says again. "He was still standing when we quit. He was winning. Whatever happened after that — whatever put him down — it wasn't us."

I look at this man for a long time.

Then I stand up, and I pick up the second chair, and I put it back in the corner.

"Silas will bring you food," I say.

"Am I staying?"

"You're staying."

"For how long?"

I don't answer that. I go to the door and open it, and the hallway is cold and empty and stretches in both directions, and somewhere at the top of this house there's a room with a desk in it and a question I've been asking wrong.

I don't kill him.
