# 15 · Selene

The pact has terms and I'm already outside them.

The deal was simple. His help reaching Rémy, my blood freely given. Two clean exchanges, one for the other, and the kind of arrangement that makes sense in a room with a lamp on.

It doesn't make sense out here.

Out here is the Bywater, midmorning, with a list of addresses Silas gave me and a face that gets me into conversations I have no right to be in. Lucian's name opens doors — not because anyone loves him, but because the doors have heard what happens to things that don't open.

I've been working Rémy's edges for a week. Not the man himself — the space around him. The people who sell him fuel, the woman who stocks his bait, the bar where his crew drinks on the nights they come north of the canal.

Each one of them is a sentence in a language I've been learning for four years, and each one of them gives me a little more of the grammar.

Rémy Thibodeaux doesn't drink. Rémy Thibodeaux goes to church, which is a sentence that makes no sense in wolf until you understand that in pack, a public ritual is a statement of territory — you put yourself in a building on a schedule so that everyone who sees you there knows you have a schedule and a building. It's not faith. It's a flag.

Rémy Thibodeaux has a sister who brings him lunch on Thursdays.

That one costs me something. I sit in my car outside a bait shop on the Lafitte highway and think about Delphine, who told me I smelled like her aunt and gave me water, and who brings her brother lunch on the day he takes the boat out.

She's not part of this. I need that clean in my own head, and it won't stay clean, and I know that, and shit, it won't stay clean anyway.

---

The wolf at the bait shop knows things.

He doesn't know he knows them, which is the best kind of source. He's old, wide, and he talks about the pack the way old men talk about the weather — as a permanent condition he's stopped trying to change.

"Thibodeauxs been on that water since before there was a dock," he says. He's cutting bait behind the counter and his knife is doing something practiced and absent. "Before the city. Before all of it."

"Why do they stay?" I ask.

It's an innocent question. I ask it the way you'd ask why someone lives in a particular neighborhood — casually, making conversation, buying shrimp.

He stops cutting.

Not dramatically. He stops the way a man stops when a muscle in his back reminds him it's there. A hitch, and then he goes on.

"Thibodeauxs have always been here," he says.

I wait for the rest of the sentence. The rest of the sentence doesn't come.

"Right," I say. "But the flooding alone — "

"This water's ours." He sets the knife down. "You don't leave water that's yours. That's — that's just how it is."

He says it the way the woman at my bar said *we don't leave.* The same flat certainty, the same absence of a reason behind it. Not stubborn. Not proud. Just — given. Like a law of physics nobody ever wrote down because it was too obvious to need writing.

I've heard this before. Marguerite said something like it in the stairwell. Different words, different language, same empty socket where the *why* should be.

Two species. Both of them planted in this city like teeth in a jaw. Both of them certain they can't leave. Neither one of them with a reason they can finish saying out loud.

I notice that. Both certain they can't leave, and neither one with a why they can hold to the end of a sentence.

Then I let it go, because I'm here about a man on a shrimp boat, and I can only carry one thing at a time.

---

Delphine is at the gas station on the corner of the highway.

I see her before she sees me — or before she lets me see her seeing me. She's at the pump, filling a truck that's too old to be worth the gas, and she's watching me the way she watched me at the bench. With attention that isn't casual.

I should drive past. I should keep my eyes on the road and my foot on the pedal and add her to the list of things I know and keep going.

I pull in.

She watches me park. She watches me get out. She doesn't move from the pump.

"You smell like him," she says.

"Like who?"

"Like the house." She pulls the nozzle out and hangs it up. "You've been sleeping there."

I don't answer, because the answer is in my skin and she already has it.

"I could smell it at the bench." She looks at me — not unfriendly, not friendly. The way a woman looks at a thing she hasn't decided about yet. "I thought maybe you'd been inside once. But it's on you now. In the skin."

"What does it smell like?"

"Old. Cold. Like a room that's been closed too long." She screws the gas cap on. "And underneath that — copper. Like something that feeds."

The word *feeds* sits between us on the concrete.

"If I can smell it," she says, "everybody on that dock can smell it. The old man at the bait shop. Gaspard's people. Everybody."

"What are you telling me?"

"I'm telling you that you walk through this territory smelling like the thing that raided us three weeks ago, and the only reason nobody's said anything is because they can also smell that you're not one of them." She looks at me. "You're something else. I don't know what. But you walk through here like you belong here and you smell like you belong there, and that's a thing that gets a person noticed."

"I've been noticed."

"By us. By the dock." She pauses. "Not by him. Not yet."

Rémy. She means Rémy.

"His nose isn't what it used to be," she says. "Too many years on the water. Salt kills it." She gets into the truck. "But it won't matter if you keep coming back. Somebody will tell him."

"Are you going to tell him?"

She starts the engine. She looks at me through the window for a long time.

"I bring him lunch on Thursdays," she says. "It's Wednesday."

She pulls out. I stand at the gas station and watch her go, and the sentence she left me is the clearest goddamn thing anyone in this city has said to me since I arrived.

She's giving me one day.

---

The addresses Silas gave me are careful.

Not safe — careful, which is different. Safe means nobody's watching. Careful means the person who put the list together thought about who might be, and routed me around them.

I can feel Silas in the list. The order of the addresses makes a circuit that avoids the main roads through wolf territory, stays in the human zones between pack and court, and doesn't cross any line that would put me in someone's jurisdiction.

He's good at this. Better than good. He reads the city the way I read a conversation — the thing that was said, and the thing underneath.

I wonder what he thinks of me. I've seen him twice — once at the house, when Lucian told him I was staying, and Silas looked at me the way you look at a delivery you weren't expecting and can't return. Once in the hall, where he handed me the list without a word and went up the stairs.

He doesn't like this arrangement. He doesn't like me in the house, me in the car, me between his king and whatever Silas thinks is coming.

He'll work with it anyway, because Lucian told him to, and Silas does what Lucian tells him to with the same precision he applies to everything — completely, silently, and with a set of opinions he keeps in a room nobody else has access to.

---

The last address on the list is a bar in Tremé. Neutral ground, more or less — the kind of place where wolves and the edges of the vampire world overlap without anyone acknowledging it.

I've been here before. I know the bartender. He's human, and he doesn't know what his clientele is, and he makes a good Old Fashioned, and he talks.

"Rémy? Sure." He wipes a glass. "He was in here last week. Tuesday, I think. He doesn't order. Just sits."

"By himself?"

"Sometimes. Sometimes with the big one — Gaspard's man. The one with the ear."

"Does he meet anybody?"

The bartender gives me a look. It's the look people give when they've been asked a question that sounds casual and isn't.

"He met a woman once," he says. "Month ago, maybe. She was — not his type. Too put-together. Nice coat."

"Vampire?"

His face does something. He doesn't know the word applies to real things. He thinks I'm making a joke.

"Rich," he says. "I figured she was a lawyer."

A woman in a nice coat who met Rémy in a bar in Tremé a month ago. Hell. Not nothing. Shit, the opposite of nothing.

I put it away. Not the way a man puts something away — in a drawer, with a label. The way I put it away. In the other language, in the space where sentences sit until I know which conversation they belong to.

---

I'm in the car on Esplanade when I notice the truck.

It's been behind me since the bait shop. Dark, no markings, and it keeps the same distance through three turns, which is one more than coincidence and one fewer than proof.

I take a fourth turn. Down Dauphine, which goes the wrong direction for home and the right direction for the Quarter.

The truck takes the turn.

My hands are steady on the wheel. That's a fact I'm going to hold onto.

I know how to be followed. I've known since I was twenty-two, when a wolf in a pickup trailed me from my apartment to the bus stop for three mornings in a row and then stopped. I never found out why he stopped. I never found out why he started.

This isn't a wolf.

I can't explain how I know that. The truck is too far back and the windows are tinted and I can't smell anything through the car. But there's something in the way it moves — patient, unhurried, with the specific confidence of a thing that knows it doesn't have to close the distance.

Wolves close the distance. It's in their bodies. A wolf who's tracking you leans forward even in a car.

This is leaning back.

Shit.

I take another turn. Then another. I'm in the Quarter now, narrow streets, one-ways, and the truck has to commit or lose me.

It commits.

I pull over on Royal Street, in front of a shop that's closed, and I sit in the car with the engine running and watch the mirror.

The truck passes. It doesn't slow down. It goes past me and turns left on St. Philip and disappears.

I sit there for a while.

Then I drive home, and I lock the door, and I push the dresser in front of it, which is a thing I haven't done since the first year.

The pact has terms. I didn't ask about the damn fine print.

---

In the apartment I sit on the floor with my back against the dresser and think about the list of things I know and the list of things I don't.

I know Rémy is on the water and he doesn't drink and his sister brings him lunch. I know an old wolf can't finish a sentence about why the pack stays in Barataria. I know a woman in a nice coat met Rémy in a bar a month before the King died.

I know someone followed me in a truck that moved like a vampire's truck.

I don't know who killed the King. I don't know why the house-mark in Lucian's private correspondence is in my head like a splinter I can feel but can't reach. I don't know why Lucian looks at me the way he looks at a door he's deciding about.

He calls me ghost. I should hate it. I don't hate it. Jesus, I don't hate it.

I don't know what I'm going to do when I finally get close enough to Rémy Thibodeaux to use the knife.

Four years ago the answer was simple. Now Delphine brings him lunch on Thursdays, and a woman smelled copper on my skin, and I'm starting to wonder what's underneath the intention I've been standing on.

God. I'm tired of wondering.

The phone buzzes. Lucian.

*Tomorrow. My father's study. Bring the ledger.*

I stare at the text. He means my father's ledger — the one I've had since the funeral. The one with the handwriting I'm not ready for.

I text back: *Which ledger.*

I know which ledger. I ask because asking is the only control I have left tonight, and the hell of it is he probably knows that too.

His reply is immediate.

*The one you haven't opened.*

And there it is. He knows I have it. He knows I haven't opened it. He knows things about me I haven't told him, because he's been paying the kind of attention that doesn't require words.

I put the phone down and look at the dresser against the door.

Someone is following me, and it isn't vampires.
