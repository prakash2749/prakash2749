# 29 · Selene

I wake up in the west wing with Lucian's cold still on my skin and something wrong in the house.

Not a sound. The absence of one. The particular silence a building makes when everyone in it has stopped doing what they were doing and started doing something else.

I sit up. The bed is empty — he left without waking me. The window is still fogged where my breath hit the glass, and the knife is in the bag on the desk where I dropped it, and for a breath the only thing in my head is the shed. Rémy's hands down. Delphine in the doorway, watching.

Delphine stood in that doorway and chose to witness. Not to speak — she'd already said her piece on the dock. She stood there with her arms crossed and watched me decide whether to open a man's throat, and when I didn't, she said *he'll testify* in a voice so quiet I almost missed it.

In wolf there's a word for the thing a family member does when they step aside. It doesn't mean *betrayal.* It means the opposite — it means *I love you enough to let the true thing happen to you, because the true thing is the only thing that might fix what you did.*

There's no English for that. English calls it letting go, which is a phrase so soft it could be about a goddamn balloon. Shit.

And the answer is Cassian, and Cassian is in a house in the Quarter right now sitting in a chair that somebody turned around this morning.

---

I smell it before I reach the hall.

Copper. Something animal underneath the copper — wet, close, the smell of a body opened in a room that wasn't built for it.

I pull on the shirt I was wearing and I walk barefoot down the corridor, and the house is wrong. Not empty — occupied in a new way. Footsteps below, moving with purpose. A door closing that doesn't usually close.

The kitchen door is open — the delivery door, the one that faces the service alley — and there's a man standing in it I don't know.

He's one of Silas's people. I know because he has the stance — hands loose, weight forward, the posture of a man who was taught to stand in doorways by someone who believed doorways were the point.

He sees me and his mouth tightens.

"Ma'am—"

"What happened?"

"You should—"

"What *happened?*"

He doesn't answer. He steps aside, which is an answer.

The kitchen is worse. The smell has weight in here — thick, iron-sweet, coating the back of my throat. Blood on tile holds its heat longer than blood on wood, and this floor is still warm with it.

There's blood on the floor. Not a pool — a pattern. It goes from the kitchen door to the dining room in a line that tells a story about how a body moved through a space it was never meant to be moving through.

Hell.

I follow it.

---

The small dining room is destroyed.

The word isn't enough. The table is against the wall — not pushed, thrown, the legs snapped and the top split down the center. The chairs are pieces. There's a hole in the plaster where something went through it and kept going, and the shape of the hole tells me what made it.

A body. Somebody went through that wall backwards, and the lath behind the plaster is splintered outward, and on the other side of the wall is a room I've never been in with a stain on the floor I'll never ask about.

The floor is a map.

I can read it the way I read a conversation — the thing that happened, and the thing underneath. Five distinct blood patterns, and each one tells a story. One long drag from the door to the wall. Two pooling marks where bodies fell and stayed. One spray across the ceiling that arcs from the fireplace to the window — a carotid, opened at speed, and the body was still turning when it bled out.

And one clean space. Rectangular. Body-shaped. Where Bastien fell and someone — Silas, I think — laid him straight before I got here.

There are men in the room. Silas's people, moving quietly, doing the work that comes after. One of them has a bucket. The water in it is red. One of them is taking the pantry door off its hinges — the door has claw marks gouged into its surface, four deep furrows from something that shifted mid-fight and tried to get through to whatever was on the other side.

A tooth on the floor. Wolf. The root still attached, pulled out of the jaw it grew in.

The door to the pantry is standing open.

And in the middle of all of it, on the floor, with his hands down at his sides and his throat open to the spine, is Bastien.

---

I don't make a sound.

I want that written down. I'm standing in a doorway looking at the only person in this house who ever grinned at me, and he's on the floor with his throat gone, and I don't make a sound.

God. His hands are down. Somebody put them down — carefully, the way you'd straighten a collar. His eyes are closed. His face has the look of a thing that was still deciding what expression to make when the decision was taken away from it.

He was eighty-nine years old. He worshipped Lucian. He carried papers into rooms where nobody asked for them, and he called me a conduit because I told him it was nicer than a drain.

He held a doorway. I can see it — the scuff marks on the floor, the way the blood falls around a space where a body stood and didn't move. He put himself between the wolves and whoever was behind him, and he held it.

I go to him.

I kneel on the floor beside him, and the blood soaks into my jeans, and I don't care about the jeans.

"You held it," I say.

He doesn't hear me. He's been gone for hours and the room is cold.

"You held it, Bastien."

One of Silas's men looks at me. He doesn't say anything. He goes back to the bucket.

---

Lucian is in the study.

I know because Silas told me — Silas, who appeared in the kitchen doorway with his hands in his coat and his face set to a frequency I've never seen on him. Not grief. Not anger. Something underneath both of those, where the machinery is.

"He's upstairs," Silas said. "Don't go in."

"Is he hurt?"

"He's not hurt." A pause. "Don't go in."

I go in.

The study door is open. It's always open. The lamp is on and the desk is clean and the chair — the guest chair, the one Lucian turned around this morning — is facing the desk.

There's a hole in the wall.

Not the dining room — this is the study. A fist-shaped depression in the plaster beside the window, clean, precise, with the knuckle marks still visible in the dust. One strike. Not the dramatic thing from the dining room — not the fury, not the animal. A single punch, measured, delivered to a wall that gave the way paper gives.

He did this before the kitchen. Before the wolves came through the delivery door, before Bastien held the doorway, before the dining room became what it became. He was standing here, in the study, after some piece of news or some conversation or some last straw that I wasn't here for, and he put his fist through the wall.

And then he pulled it out, and flexed the fingers, and walked to the next meeting.

I know this because there's no blood on the plaster. No rage in the mark. Just the precise impression of a hand that needed to go through something, and the wall was closer than the man who deserved it.

Micro-fracture. The last one before the dam.

Lucian is at the window.

His back is to me. His hands are at his sides and there's blood on them — not his, not all of it — and his shirt is ruined.

He doesn't turn around.

"Silas told you not to come in," he says.

"Silas tells me a lot of things."

"This time he was right."

I cross the room. I stand behind him, close enough to touch him, and I don't touch him.

"Bastien," I say.

"Yes."

"He held the doorway."

"He held it." His voice is level. "He put two humans behind him and he held it, and he'd never been in a fight that mattered, and he held it until I got there."

"And after?"

He doesn't answer. His hands are at his sides and the blood on them is drying, and the moonlight through the window puts his face in a light I have to be careful about reading.

"Lucian."

"I killed them all." He says it the way you'd say you'd taken the garbage out. "Six wolves. One of them had shifted back. He put his hands down."

"And?"

"And I took his jaw off." He turns. His face is the face of a man who has looked at himself and found something he expected. "Because he was talking."

Jesus.

I hold his eyes. I don't flinch. I don't step back. I don't do any of the things a body does when it's standing in front of something that just told it what it's capable of.

"And the pantry," he says.

"What about the pantry?"

"There was a woman in the pantry. The linen girl. She was behind Bastien when he held the door." His hands open and close at his sides. "I went for the pantry."

"Did you open it?"

"Silas stopped me."

The room is very quiet.

"He said my name," Lucian says. "*Sire.* One word. From the hall. And I turned around, and there she was behind the door, and I had my damn hand on it."

He looks at his hands. The blood is dark on them, almost black in the lamplight.

"I wanted to keep going," he says. "Not that I did it — that I wanted more of it. The pantry door was the next door and I was going to open it."

Shit. The pantry.

I take his hands.

He lets me. They're cold and stiff and the blood on them is someone else's, and I hold them the way he held my father's ledger — carefully, from the edges, with the attention of a person handling something that matters.

"You stopped," I say.

"Silas stopped me."

"You turned around." I tighten my grip. "A man who can't be stopped doesn't turn around because someone says his name. You turned around because the name was enough."

"That's a generous reading."

"That's the correct reading." I look up at him. "I'm a translator. Trust the translation."

His jaw loosens. Not a crack — something underneath a crack. The thing that's been holding since he walked into the dining room, letting go one fraction at a time.

"Bastien was eighty-nine," he says.

"I know."

"He called me *sire* every time I told him not to."

"I know."

"He grinned when I corrected him."

"I know, Lucian."

He puts his forehead against mine. His eyes are closed and his breath is uneven and his hands in mine are shaking.

"They came through the kitchen," he says. "Somebody let them in."

"I know."

"The same hand. Every time. The same hand on the same door."

I hold his hands. The house is quiet around us. Somewhere below, men are washing a wall and taking a door off its hinges.

"Tomorrow," I say.

"Tomorrow."

"He comes at the usual hour."

"He comes at the usual hour."

I let go of his hands. I go to the desk and I pick up the blade — the one that was his father's, the one that's keyed to one man's blood — and I put it in his coat, in the pocket where it lives.

"Go sleep," he says.

"You won't."

"No."

I leave him in the study with the lamp on and the chair turned around and the blood drying on his hands. The hallway is dark and the house smells like what happened in it tonight, and I go to the west wing and I shut the door.

For the first time since I moved in, I shut the door all the way.

Then I open it again.

Because a door standing open is a decision, and tonight I'd rather decide than be safe.
