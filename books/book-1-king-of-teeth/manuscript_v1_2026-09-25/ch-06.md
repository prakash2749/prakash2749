# 6 · Selene

The bar is full and nobody is drinking.

I know the tell. I've worked enough Friday nights to know what a crowd that came for the music looks like, and this isn't it. These people came because they didn't want to be home.

Antoine has the news on behind the bar with the sound off, and nobody is watching it, and everybody is watching it.

The crawl at the bottom says something about a disturbance in Barataria. Gas leak, they're calling it. An evacuation of several homes on the south end, with two injuries reported and no fatalities.

In English, that's a gas leak.

In the other language — the one nobody in this room speaks but everybody in this room understands in their damn joints — something came through Barataria last night and the city can feel the bruise.

The old locals know. They don't know the words for it, but they have the grammar. Two centuries of living on top of this have produced a folk knowledge that works the way a tourniquet works — not gracefully, but enough.

Don't take the second drink. Don't walk the levee after dark. If a man at a bar doesn't blink for too long, find somewhere else to sit.

If the news says *gas leak* in Barataria, stay out of the Quarter for a week.

That one doesn't have a rhyme or a reason you could put on a bumper sticker. But the woman at the end of my bar — local, sixty, with a purse she's held in her lap all night — she knows. She came in here because being in a crowd is safer than being in a house, and she ordered a tonic water, and she's been nursing it for two hours.

She doesn't know about vampires. She doesn't know about wolves.

She knows that the air changed yesterday, the way you know a storm is coming because the birds go quiet.

"Another?" I ask her.

"I'm fine, baby." She pats the bar. "Just sitting."

"Kitchen's closing in an hour."

"I know." She looks at the TV. "They say what started it?"

"Gas leak."

"Mm." She turns her glass on its napkin. "My grandmother used to say when the gas leaks in Jean Lafitte, you stay on this side of the canal."

"Smart woman."

"She was a frightened woman. That's different." She picks up her purse. "Same thing, though, in the end."

She puts a five on the bar for a tonic water that cost three dollars, and she leaves, and I put the five in the jar and wipe the bar and think about the difference between smart and frightened, and whether a woman who can hear both languages has the hell of a right to be either.

---

The raid was last night.

I know this because the wolves in Tremé know it, and the wolves in Tremé know everything that happens in Barataria about four hours after it happens, which is faster than the news and slower than a phone call. The information travels in a specific way — not gossip, not rumor, but the pack equivalent of a weather report. Factual, clipped, and always in the passive voice.

*Vampires came through the settlement. Houses were left. Fighters were hit. Nobody died.*

That last part has been said three times in my hearing today, by three different wolves in three different places, and each time it lands the same way. Not relief. Confusion.

In wolf, a raid that doesn't kill is not a mercy. It's a statement. It says: *I chose not to, and the choosing is the point.*

A raid that kills is simple. The response is simple. You bury your dead and you go to war, because war is the answer to dead, and the grammar of it is old enough that nobody has to look it up.

A raid that leaves your houses lit and your people breathing is something else. It's a sentence in a language wolves don't have a damn word for, and they're going to spend the next week trying to translate it.

I've been translating it all day.

What the new King of New Orleans said last night is: *I am not my father. I could have been worse.*

That's two sentences. Most people will only hear one of them.

---

The rush doesn't come. Nine o'clock, ten, and the crowd stays thin and frightened and nursing drinks they don't want.

Antoine cuts the kitchen early. He sends the dishwasher home and does the pots himself, and I hear him back there banging a stockpot against the sink with more force than the grease requires.

"You okay?" I ask through the window.

"My cousin's in Barataria."

"Is she all right?"

"She's fine. Everybody's fine. That's the problem." He sets the pot down. "When everybody's fine, that means somebody decided everybody gets to be fine. And that somebody gets to decide again tomorrow."

Shit. He's right about that.

In wolf, that would take one word. The word for a debt you owe because someone declined to collect it.

Antoine doesn't have that word. He has a stockpot and a sink and the feeling in his forearms when he scrubs.

"Go home," I tell him. "I'll close."

"You sure?"

"I'm sure."

He takes his apron off and hangs it on the hook and looks at me for a second like he's going to say something, and then he doesn't.

The door swings shut behind him and I'm alone in the bar with four customers and a television set nobody has turned off.

---

I close at midnight, which is early, which will cost me. But the crowd is wrong tonight and a wrong crowd doesn't spend money, it just takes up space, and space is the one thing I can't afford to damn well waste.

I lock the door and count the register and put the envelope in the safe, and then I sit on my crate in the alley and look at my phone.

No messages.

I open the map. Barataria is a green smear south of the canal, and the settlement is a cluster of dots near the water, and somewhere in that cluster is a dock with a shed on it and a man who takes his boat out on Thursdays.

I went there. I sat on a bench. A girl told me I smelled like her aunt.

And I left without doing what I've been building toward for four goddamn years.

---

Here's what I'm going to do instead.

The city is afraid. Both sides of it — the human side that doesn't know what it's afraid of, and the other side that knows exactly.

When a city is afraid, people go to the places that feel safe. Humans go to bars with crowds and light. Wolves go to Barataria and close the gate.

Vampires go to the places they own.

There's a club on Perdido Street that I have never been inside. I've stood on the sidewalk across from it and counted the cars, and the cars are always expensive and the people who get out of them are always beautiful and they always go in through the side door.

It's not listed. There's no sign. The windows are painted black from the inside, and the music that comes through the walls when the door opens is the kind that makes your sternum hum.

Everyone who works the bars on Frenchmen knows about Perdido. Nobody goes. The understanding is unspoken and total — it's theirs, the way Barataria is the wolves' and the Marchand house is the witch's and certain blocks of Tremé are nobody's. Hell, even the drunks know better.

I've heard things. That the drinks are free if the right people like you. That the people who go in come out looking tired in a way that sleep wouldn't fix. That the tips are extraordinary and the staff doesn't talk about the back hallways.

The King of New Orleans drinks there. Or he did, before his father died.

I don't know whether he'll be there tonight. I don't know whether the raid changed his schedule or kept it, and I don't have a way to find out without walking through the door.

But the city is afraid, and afraid means distracted, and distracted means that a woman who walks into a room she doesn't belong in might get a few minutes before someone notices.

A few minutes is enough. A few minutes is a conversation. A conversation is a door. Shit, it's the only door I've got.

And I need a door into Barataria that doesn't involve a bus and a bench and a knife I can't seem to use. Jesus.

---

I go home. I shower. I stand in front of my closet and look at what's in it.

Two years of bartender clothes — black shirts, dark jeans, shoes that don't slip on a wet floor. Nothing in here was chosen. Everything in here was selected, which is a different damn thing, and the difference is the space between a sentence and a translation.

At the back there's a dress.

It's the one nice thing I own that I didn't buy for someone else's funeral. Black, simple, and it does something to my shoulders that I've never liked and have been told twice is the point.

I put it on. I look at myself in the mirror on the back of the bathroom door — the mirror with the crack in the corner that I've been meaning to replace for a year.

The woman in the mirror is small, dark-haired, and she's going to a club where the most dangerous things in this city gather to drink, because the man she needs is across a body of water she can't cross alone. God help her.

Something moves along the back of my neck. Not a sound — below sound. A frequency in the skin, the kind the wolf-half registers before the rest of me gets the memo. My shoulders draw together. The fine hair on my forearms lifts, and for one breath the dress isn't fabric — it's a membrane, and everything on the other side of it is paying attention.

I stand very still.

The reaching. The thing that has been reaching for wolves since I could stand in a room with one — the thing that always falls short.

It isn't falling short.

Something is out there tonight. Not wolf. Older than wolf. The frequency is lower, colder, and it smells — I swear to God it smells, even through a wall, even through a mile of city — like winter in a room that has no windows. My body is leaning into it the way you lean toward a voice you can't quite hear, and the voice is not getting quieter.

The reaching stretches. Farther than it's ever gone. My pulse picks up and my skin goes tight and I can feel the shape of whatever's out there the way you feel a hand before it touches you — the heat of it, the weight of it, the promise that the gap is about to close.

It doesn't close. It passes. Whatever it was moves on, and the reaching comes back empty, the way it always comes back empty, and I'm standing in my bathroom in a black dress with my hand on the mirror and my breath fogging the glass.

But it was close. Closer than it has ever been.

My hands are shaking, and not from fear.

The knife is on the counter. I look at it.

I leave it there.

Tonight I'm not carrying a blade. Tonight I'm carrying something else — a face and a voice and the ability to hear both sides of any sentence in any room.

The inheritance. My father taught me to listen. My mother gave me something to listen with.

I check myself one more time. I put my keys in the small bag, the one with no room for anything else, and I lock the apartment door behind me, and I walk out into a city that is afraid of itself tonight.

I'm not afraid. I should be. That's probably important.

The club on Perdido is eleven blocks from here, and I'm going to walk them in shoes that aren't mine, in a dress I've worn once, into a room full of things that drink.

I'm going where they go.
