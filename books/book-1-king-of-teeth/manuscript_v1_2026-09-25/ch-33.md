# 33 · Selene

I hear him knock.

The first wrong thing. Cassian has never knocked on this door. He's walked through it every evening for longer than this country has existed, the way you walk through a door that was left open for you.

Tonight he knocks.

The sound goes down the hall and finds me on the bench. Three knocks, even, with the deliberate rhythm of a man who has already decided what the knocking means.

He knows. He knew before the hall. He knew when he buttoned his coat, and he knew when he walked through the front door, and he knows now, standing in front of a study where the chair has been turned around.

"Come in," Lucian says.

---

I can't see them. The angle is wrong — the bench is down the hall, past the turn, and the study door is open but facing the other way. I can see a stripe of lamplight on the floor and the edge of the doorframe and nothing else.

I can hear everything.

"Boy."

"Come in."

Footsteps. Cassian's — I know them now, the way I know the footsteps of everyone in this house. His are deliberate and slightly heavier than they should be, because he has always walked as though the floor were something he was doing a favor.

Then a pause. A long one.

"You moved it," he says.

"It was facing the wrong way."

"Your father never sat facing the window."

"No. He didn't."

The chair. He's seen the turned chair and he's commenting on it, and Lucian is answering, and the exchange is the last ordinary thing that will happen in that room.

---

Marguerite said *close enough to hear.* She said it this afternoon in the hall, standing in front of me with her hands folded and her face doing the thing that Lucian's face does.

I'm close enough.

I'm close enough to hear the sound of a man sitting down in a chair that's been turned to face the desk. I'm close enough to hear the creak of old wood and the small sound of a coat being adjusted.

And I'm close enough to hear Lucian say: "Did you kill my father."

"Yes."

One syllable. It goes down the hall and lands on me like a hand.

No hesitation. No performance. No pause to arrange the face or choose the word. Just *yes,* delivered in the voice of a man who has been carrying the word for a month and is setting it down.

---

They talk.

I can hear the shapes of sentences without catching every word. Lucian asks questions in a voice I haven't heard him use — level, precise, with the particular coldness of a man who is conducting the last interview of a life.

Cassian answers. His voice carries better — older, rounder, with the quality of a man who has never had to raise it to be heard.

Fragments come down the hall.

*— the wolves —*

*Mine. Somebody had to leave them unlatched.*

*— the translator —*

*— asked good questions —*

*— the poison —*

*— a broker between us —*

I sit on the bench and I listen to the man who ordered my parents' deaths explain himself in a warm voice to the son of the man he murdered, and the warmth is real. God. That's the part that goes into me like a nail. The warmth is completely, entirely real.

He means every word he's saying. He means the confession the way he means the questions about people's families — genuinely, fully, with the plain sincerity of a man who has never once faked a feeling in his life.

He's a monster who loves the boy he raised. Both of those are true. Both of them are in his voice at the same time, and I can hear them both, because hearing both is the only thing I've ever been able to do.

---

Then the part I've been waiting for and dreading.

"Because your father took my daughter's head off in a courtyard while I asked him not to."

The sentence comes down the hall complete. Every word of it, clear, in a voice that has been building that sentence for longer than I've been alive.

I knew about the daughter. Not the details — the shape of it. A girl who killed someone. The law applied. The father present.

Lucian's father did it personally. Because the Code required it, and because the man who wrote the Code would not hand the ugly part to someone else.

And Cassian stood in a courtyard and watched.

In wolf there's a word for what happens to a person who watches their child die. It's not the word for grief. It's the word for the thing that goes wrong in the foundation — the crack that doesn't show on the surface, that the building stands on for years, that opens only when the weight finally shifts.

English doesn't have that word. English says *he never got over it.*

What Cassian never got over has been running this city for four hundred years. It arranged a translator's death, unlatched a window, put a blade under a friend's ribs, and sat in a damn chair every evening asking about someone's mother's house.

The grief became a hand. The hand became a plan. The plan became a city on the edge of a war.

Hell of a legacy.

And at the center of it, a man who loved his friend and killed him anyway, because the friend was the law, and the law took his daughter, and the daughter is the room he will not walk through.

---

I hear the charge.

Lucian's voice, steady. The killing of a king. A kinsman. A false witness.

*You have no recognized justification. Grief isn't one. Being right isn't one either.*

Then: "On your knees."

I close my eyes.

---

The draining takes a long time.

I don't hear it happen — not the act itself, which is silent, or close to it. What I hear is the absence. The room goes quiet in a way that has weight to it, the heavy silence of something being taken from a body that doesn't want to let go.

Minutes pass. Many of them.

Then Cassian's voice, low enough that I have to hold my breath to catch it.

"Good boy."

Everything in me goes cold.

Damn him. Damn him for using those words. Fuck him for this.

I said those words. Yesterday. On the floor of the west wing, with my hand on his throat, with his body inside mine and his grief inside both of us. I said *good boy* and I meant: *I see the animal and the man and they are the same person and I am not leaving.*

Cassian says it and he means something else entirely. He means: *I raised you. I taught your hands. I put the blade in them and I put the courtyard under your feet, and you are doing exactly what I built you to do, and I am proud of you, and the pride is real, and it has always been real, and it is the worst thing I have ever done to you.*

Same words. Two meanings. In two rooms, twenty-four hours apart, said by the two people who love him most.

Jesus. I am the only person alive who heard both.

My hands are gripping the edge of the bench. The wood is cutting into my palms and I don't let go.

"Don't," Lucian says.

"Good boy," Cassian says again, and his voice is thinner now, and the warmth in it is leaving the way heat leaves a cup — from the surface first, then all the way down.

A sound. Cassian's hand, moving. Finding Lucian's wrist. The same grip — I know it without seeing it, because Lucian described it to me once, the way a man describes a thing he's carried his whole life. The grip from the courtyard. The grip that taught him where to put the weight.

---

More silence. A long stretch of it, filled with the sound of the house doing what houses do at night — settling, cooling, the small sounds of a building that has been standing for three centuries and intends to keep standing.

Then Lucian's voice.

"Was it only you?"

A pause. Long enough that I think it's over.

Then: "No."

"Who."

Another pause. Longer.

A sound I can't place. A hand, moving. Finding something.

And then one word, in a voice that has almost nothing left in it.

"Verity."

---

The word comes down the hall and it means two things.

In French, in the old way, in the language Cassian has been speaking since before this city had a name — *véritée.* The truth. He's telling Lucian he's given him the truth of it. The first meaning.

The second one is a name.

I know this because I hear both, the way I hear both of everything, and the two meanings sit on top of each other like transparencies on a light table, and I can't tell which one he intended.

A man with no air left in his body, saying one word that is either the truth or a person, and the person who hears it doesn't know which.

---

The silence after goes on for a very long time.

Then a sound. Quick, heavy, final.

Then Silas's voice, flat as a closed door: "The hour is nine-fourteen."

---

I sit on the bench.

The hall is quiet. The house is quiet. Somewhere in the study, Silas is writing something down, and the Roche brothers are standing where they were put, and Lucian is in a room with a body that used to be his uncle.

I sit on the bench and I hold the edge of it and I breathe.

Shit. Shit. Shit.

*Verity.*

Truth or a name. The last word of a man who spent his whole life being warm, and genuine, and present, and murderous.

I don't know which one it is. Lucian doesn't know either — I can hear that in the silence, in the absence of anything coming out of that room. He's sitting with the word the way I sit with sentences that won't translate.

The study door is open. It's always open.

I get up off the bench. My legs work, which is a fact I'm grateful for. I walk down the hall to the doorway.

I don't go in.

I stand in the frame. The room is lamplit. The desk is clean. The chair — the turned chair — has a folded coat over the back of it, and the coat has been smoothed flat, because Cassian was neat his whole life.

Lucian is on the floor.

He's on his knees. His hands are on the boards and his head is down, and there's blood on his knees and blood on his shirt and blood on his hands. He's not shaking. He's not making a sound.

He's holding still.

Silas is by the door. He sees me and he doesn't move to stop me, and he doesn't move to let me in. He waits, the way Silas always waits, for the situation to tell him what it needs.

"Lucian," I say.

He doesn't look up.

"Lucian."

Nothing.

I go in.

I cross the study and I kneel down next to him on the wet floor, and I put my hand on the back of his neck. Ghost, he'd say. Not now. Now there's just my hand and his skin and the quiet.

He doesn't flinch. He doesn't pull away. He stays where he is, with his head down and his hands on the boards and the weight of the house on his back.

"I'm here," I say.

"I know."

"The word he said."

"I know."

"Do you know what it means?"

"No." His voice is nothing. "Not yet."

I keep my hand on his neck. Silas stands by the door. The lamp goes on humming.

After a while, Silas clears his throat.

He has never cleared his throat. Not once in the time I've known him. The sound is so wrong coming out of that man that I look up.

He has a paper in his hand. Folded once, with something written on the front in his small, square hand.

"When you're able," he says. To Lucian. Not to me.

Lucian lifts his head. His eyes are wrecked.

Silas holds the paper out. "A writ of protection. For the Marchand house and its keeper. Signed by the crown, bearing the seal."

Lucian looks at the paper. Then at Silas.

In two hundred years of service, Silas Roche has never asked for a thing. He has stood behind doors and sharpened blades and written down what needed writing and gone where he was sent without a word about where he'd rather be. He has never once spent a favor.

He's spending it now. On a witch in a house on neutral ground.

Lucian takes the paper. He doesn't read it. He picks up Cassian's pen from the desk — Cassian's pen, still warm from the dead man's coat — and signs.

Silas folds it back. Puts it inside his coat. Steps back to the wall.

Neither of them says anything else about it. The lamp goes on humming.

The word is in the room. It's going to be in the room for a long time — sitting in the air like something unfinished, waiting for someone to pick it up and decide what it is.

Truth, or a name.

We'll find out. Not tonight. Tonight we're on the floor, and the door is open, and the man who sat in the turned chair has gone out of the room and left a word behind him.

I stay.
