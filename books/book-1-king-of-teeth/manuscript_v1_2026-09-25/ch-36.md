# 36 · Selene

My apartment smells like dust and the ghost of coffee.

I haven't been here in weeks. The plant on the windowsill is dead — it was dying when I left, and the leaving finished it.

There's a cup in the sink with something growing in it that I'm going to deal with later or never.

The door locked behind me the way it always does. The deadbolt and the chain — two locks on a door in a building where nobody has ever tried to come through it.

I stand in the kitchen and I look at the counter.

The knife is on the counter. Where I put it the last time I came back from the house — back in the bag, blade clean, set down next to the toaster with the intention of never picking it up again.

---

I pick it up.

It's my father's. A short blade, wood handle, a nick in the bolster where somebody dropped it. I've sharpened it more times than I've used it, and the last time I sharpened it I was sitting on this floor with a king on my linoleum and an intention so clean I could see my own face in it.

The intention is gone — gone the way a word goes when you've been using it wrong for years and somebody shows you the real one. It doesn't fade. It was never the thing you thought it was.

I was going to kill Rémy Thibodeaux. That was the shape of my life for four years. Every day arranged around it — the language learned, the schedules memorized, the bars worked, the bus rides taken. A woman building herself into a weapon aimed at one man on one boat.

And the man on the boat was an instrument. Somebody else's hand, somebody else's lie. A wolf sent to kill a translator by a vampire who asked about his mother first.

God.

I didn't kill Rémy. I stood in a shed with a knife in my hand and a man with his hands down saying *go ahead,* and I lowered it. Not because I'd forgiven him. Because he'd saved my life on a dock days before, and you can't kill a man you owe, and the debt was the only honest thing left in the room.

He's alive. My parents are still dead. The man who ordered their deaths is dead too, drained and beheaded in a study with the door standing open.

The equation doesn't balance. It was never going to balance. That's the thing about revenge — it promises you a zero at the end, and the zero doesn't exist, and you find that out standing in your own damn kitchen holding a knife you didn't use.

---

I open the drawer next to the stove. The junk drawer — batteries, a screwdriver, a menu from a restaurant that closed ages ago.

I put the knife in the drawer.

Not thrown — placed. The way you place a thing you're keeping but aren't carrying anymore. It goes in beside the screwdriver and the menu, and I close the drawer, and the drawer shuts.

A woman puts a knife in a drawer in an empty apartment, and the intention that organized her whole life goes in with it, and nobody sees it happen.

Shit, the whole damn ceremony.

---

The bar is the same.

Antoine is behind the register. He looks up when I come in and his face does something between relief and the plain annoyance of a man who's been doing two people's work.

"You're alive," he says.

"I'm alive."

"You look different."

"I've been sleeping somewhere else."

"Yeah, I can tell." He shuts the register. "You sleeping somewhere better?"

"Somewhere with higher ceilings."

He doesn't ask. Antoine has never asked me a question that required more than a one-sentence answer, and that's why I've worked with him this long, and that's why I'm going to keep working with him.

"We're short tonight," he says. "Tanya quit."

"When?"

"Tuesday. She said the energy was wrong."

"The energy."

"Her word." He shrugs. "Can you close?"

"I can close."

---

The bar fills the way it always does. Nine o'clock, the crowd turns over, and the room becomes the machine I know how to operate.

I pour drinks and wipe the bar and listen.

Two languages. They're still there — the English on top and the other one underneath, the current of who's watching whom and the small shifts when something with teeth walks in.

Same as it ever was. Same damn frequencies. Does it feel different now, or do I?

A vampire comes in around ten. Young, well-dressed, with the particular confidence of a man who knows what he is and enjoys the knowing. He orders a bourbon and he looks at me the way they all look at bartenders — briefly, absently, the way you'd look at furniture.

Then he looks again.

His nostrils flare. Assessment. He's caught whatever I am on the air, and he's trying to place it, and he can't.

"Do I know you?" he says.

"No."

"You smell—" He stops. "Sorry. You smell familiar."

"I get that."

Hell, that's going to keep happening. How long before every vampire in the Quarter has caught it?

He drinks his bourbon and leaves, and the tip is extraordinary, and I put it in the jar without looking at it.

---

After midnight the crowd thins and I'm alone behind the bar with two regulars and the television set.

The news is on. Something about the Quarter — a renovation, a parade, the ordinary life of a city that sits on top of two worlds and has decided to notice only one of them.

I wipe the bar and I think about what I know. What do I actually know?

My father was a translator. He did private work for a man who used a hooked mark and paid too much and never gave a name. The man killed him to close the account, and the killing was done by a wolf, and the wolf was done by a lie.

The man is dead. The wolf is alive. The lie is in a ledger in a house on the other side of the Quarter, being held by a king with an ugly crown and clean hands.

And the word — *Verity* — is sitting in both of us like a splinter neither of us can reach.

Truth, or a name. Cassian's last word, delivered with no air, in a room with the door standing open.

If it's truth, the story is finished. A grieving uncle killed a king and was killed in turn, and the Code held, and the city goes on.

If it's a name, the story has a second volume.

I don't know which. Lucian doesn't know. Silas is looking and finding nothing, which is either an answer or the absence of one.

I'm going to find out. Tonight I'm going to close a bar and count a register and walk home through a city that doesn't know what the hell lives in it. But I'm going to find out.

But I'm going to find out, because my father left me a ledger and wrote *for S, when she asks* on the last page, and I've been asking since the day I opened it, and I'm not done asking.

---

I close up, count the register, put the envelope in the safe.

Then I sit on my crate in the alley and I take my phone out.

Three contacts that aren't the bar. My landlord. My dentist. The woman in Tremé who sells me the tea.

I add a fourth.

Not Lucian. I already have Lucian — he's been in my phone since the night he texted me about the ledger, saved under a name I'm not going to share with anyone.

The fourth number is Delphine.

She gave it to me at the treeline, the day I came for Rémy. She wrote it on the back of a gas receipt with a pen that was running out, and she said, "In case you need a wolf who's not my brother."

I haven't called it. I'm not going to call it tonight.

But the number is in my phone, and the phone is in my pocket, and for the first time in my life I have a wolf's number that isn't attached to an intention.

Jesus, that's new.

It's attached to something else. Something that started the night I walked into a club on Perdido Street and has been growing ever since, quiet and stubborn, in a part of me I didn't know had goddamn soil.

---

I walk home.

The city is warm. The Quarter at two in the morning has the heavy quality of a place that has been up too long and is starting to say things it wouldn't say in daylight.

A man on a corner is playing a trumpet with his case open. The notes go up into the air and stay there, the way notes do in this city — held, suspended, as though the air itself has a memory.

I pass the block where Lucian pulled over in Silas's car. I can see the spot — dark, residential, the exact stretch of curb where I climbed into his lap and the horn went off.

I don't stop. But I smile, and the smile is a new one — not the smile I use at the bar, not the one I use for wolves, not the one I used to practice in a mirror that has a crack in the corner.

A real one. Small, and private, and nobody sees it, and that's fine.

---

The apartment is dark when I get home. The plant is still dead. The cup is still in the sink.

I lock the door. Deadbolt and chain, the way I always do.

Then I unlock it.

I stand there with my hand on the chain and I think about doors.

Open doors. Lucian's house, where nothing closes all the way. My bedroom in the west wing, where I slept with the door open because opening it was a decision.

Cassian, walking through a door that was always left open for him.

My mother, who left a pack and never closed the door behind her, because the door was already closed for her.

I take the chain off. I leave the deadbolt. One lock, not two.

It's not much. A woman in an apartment, taking one lock off a door that nobody is trying to come through.

But it's a decision, and tonight decisions are the only language I've got that works without translation.

---

I go to bed.

The bed is mine. The sheets are mine. The window is the one that opens, not the one that's painted shut, and I leave it cracked, because the air outside smells like rain and the Quarter and something green.

My phone is on the pillow beside me. Four contacts. My landlord, my dentist, the tea woman, and a wolf named Delphine.

And Lucian, saved under a name I chose, in a city that is afraid and alive and mine.

I close my eyes.

The knife is in the drawer. The ledger is in the house. The word is in the air.

And for the first time since I came to this city, the last thing I think about before I sleep isn't the man I came here to kill.

It's the man I'm going back to in the morning.
