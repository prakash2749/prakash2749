# 30 · Lucian

I have a name and no proof and a court that wants blood.

The chamber is full for the first time since my father's funeral. Every House with a seat has sent a body to sit in it, and some of them have sent bodies that haven't been seen in this room in decades. Marguerite is counting heads. I watch her do it and I watch the number please her.

War fills rooms. My father said that once and I didn't write it down because I thought I'd remember the rest of the sentence. I didn't. I remember the fact.

Cassian is standing where he always stands — to my left, one step behind, close enough to speak without the room hearing it. He's been there since I was a child. The position was never assigned. He chose it.

"Full house," he says.

"Marguerite's doing."

"Marguerite's doing would have been half this. The rest came on their own." His voice is warm. It's always warm. "They want to see you decide."

They want to see me commit. There's a difference, and Cassian knows it, and the warmth in his voice is doing the work of making me forget he knows it.

I don't forget.

---

The motion is simple. Cassian wrote it, which means it reads like statute and sounds like a eulogy.

War against the Thibodeaux line. Full mobilization of the Houses. Territorial enforcement at the river. Reprisal authority delegated to House commanders, which means Marguerite gets the leash she's been pulling against since the funeral.

It's clean. It's proportional. It's exactly what the court wants and exactly what a guilty man would build if he needed the court looking outward instead of inward.

I read it and I say nothing.

"The motion carries a sunset," Cassian says. "Ninety days. Renewable by vote."

"Generous."

"Practical. A court that votes for war without a limit votes for something else entirely." He looks at me. "Your father taught me that."

Did he? Or did Cassian teach himself and put my father's name on it afterward?

"The council will hear speakers," I say.

Marguerite stands first. Her argument is brief and contains no sentence longer than the one before it. She is very good at this.

Then Alençon, who runs the docks and wants them defended. Then Brodeur, who hasn't spoken in this chamber since the last succession and stands up to say one sentence about a nephew who died in the raid, and sits down.

Cassian watches each of them with the expression of a man witnessing a process he respects. It's convincing. It was always convincing.

---

Selene is at the back of the room.

She's not supposed to be here. I told her the west wing. She heard me say it and she came here instead, because she has decided that she will be in whatever room I'm in when it matters, and she is correct, and I don't have the time to be angry about it.

She's standing against the wall near the door. She's dressed for court, which means she's dressed the way a woman dresses when she has one good thing and wears it like a dare. Her arms are at her sides. Her chin is up.

Nobody is looking at her, which means everybody has already looked at her.

Laurent Desrosiers sees me notice.

Laurent is Marguerite's second cousin. He has held a minor seat since before the last war and has never once said anything worth the air it displaced. He is standing near the third column, closest to the door, and he has been watching Selene since she walked in.

He turns to the man beside him and says something.

I hear it. The room is loud and he pitched it under the noise and I hear it anyway, because I have my father's ears and the room is mine.

"The hybrid."

Two words. A description, and not an inaccurate one.

The man beside him laughs.

Did Marguerite send him? Or did Laurent convince himself this was his own idea and arrive at it the way small men arrive at courage — slowly, with someone else's hand on his back?

---

The speakers continue. I give them the room because the room is what the Code requires. Everyone speaks who wants to speak, and nobody is told to sit down, and if something tightens behind my teeth that has nothing to do with hunger, the chamber doesn't need to know about it.

Then Laurent stands.

He's not on the speaker list. He rises from his seat without being recognized, which is a small breach, the kind Marguerite commits and gets away with because she's Marguerite, and which Laurent is committing because he has confused proximity to Marguerite with being her.

"The council should address the matter of the King's arrangements," he says.

The room shifts. Not a sound — a quality of attention, the way a dozen predators all point at the same thing at once.

"Arrangements," I say.

"The woman." He doesn't look at Selene. He looks at me. "The hybrid woman who has been in this house for a month. Eating our food. Using our name as cover. Being seen with the acting King."

"She has a name."

"She has a species, and it's neither of ours." He's been rehearsing this. I can hear the rehearsal in the rhythm — too even, too clean. Somebody told him this was a good idea. "The court's concern is simple. An unclassified creature in House Croix, with access to the household, during a time of war—"

"We are not at war."

"—during a time of *imminent* war, presents a security risk that the council has a right to address."

He's looking at me with the expression of a man who believes he is being brave. He isn't. He is standing in a room with me because the room is full of witnesses and the Code is in effect and he has mistaken those things for safety.

"Is that the whole of it," I say.

"The council should vote on her removal."

"No."

"With respect—"

"I heard you. The answer is no."

He doesn't sit down. That's the second breach and the one that tells me he's been sent. Who would gain from this? Who wants the room looking at Selene instead of at the motion on the table?

"The Code provides for a challenge on matters of household security," he says. "Any seated member may—"

"I know what the Code provides." I stand up. The chair doesn't make a sound because I don't let it. "I wrote the enforcement commentary on that clause when I was a hundred and twelve. It requires a named grievance, a named respondent, and a named consequence, presented before the challenge is heard. Would you like to name them?"

The room is quiet.

"Go ahead," I say. "Name the grievance."

His mouth opens.

"Name the respondent. She's standing right there." I nod toward Selene. "And then name what you'd like done to her, Laurent. Out loud, in this room, in front of everyone here. Because that's what the Code requires. Not an implication. Not a concern. A sentence, spoken, with your name attached."

He's pale. Vampires don't go pale the way humans do — the blood doesn't leave the face, the face leaves the expression. What's underneath is something older and flatter, and Laurent is wearing it now.

"I have no specific—"

"Then sit down." He doesn't sit down.

---

This is the part where a different man would let it go. The point is made. The room heard it. Laurent is standing in front of a full council with his rehearsed speech in pieces on the floor, and any reasonable reading of the situation says I've won.

I'm not interested in winning. Winning is what happens when two people disagree and one of them is louder.

I'm interested in this never happening again.

I step down from the chair. Not quickly — I take the stairs the way my father took them, one at a time, without the rail, and the room tracks me the way a room tracks weather.

Laurent takes a step back. He catches himself and stops, but the step happened, and every person in this chamber saw it.

I walk to him. I stop at arm's length. The distance a man keeps when he's being polite.

"You pointed at her," I say.

"I didn't—"

"You gestured toward the back of the room when you said *the hybrid woman.* Your left hand. I saw it." I hold his eyes. "I want to be precise about this because the Code requires precision."

The room is not breathing.

"Article eleven," I say. "A guest of the House is under the protection of the House. A slight against a guest, in the presence of the host, is answered by the host. The answer is proportional."

"She's not a guest, she's a—"

"She is whatever I say she is in my house." I take the last step. We are close enough now that he can feel the cold coming off me. "You pointed at a woman under my protection and asked a room full of people to remove her. In my father's chamber. While I was sitting in his chair."

His chin comes up. It's the last brave thing he does.

"I'm going to break your hand," I tell him. "The left one. The one you used to point at her."

"You can't—"

"I'm telling you before I do it. That's what the Code is for."

---

I take his wrist.

There is a way to do this that my father showed me when I was young. Not a combat grip — a physician's grip. You hold the wrist with both hands, thumbs on the back, fingers wrapped underneath. You find the third and fourth metacarpals, which are the bones that run from the knuckle to the center of the hand. You apply pressure at the midpoint.

It takes very little force. Less than opening a jar.

I've never done it. My father showed me and then said, *You will never need to, because you'll tell them first, and telling them first is usually enough.*

It is usually enough.

Laurent screams. It's a short sound, cut off when I let go, because I hold the break for a beat and then release. His hand is still attached to his arm. The bones will set in a day.

It is precisely proportional to a pointed finger and a sentence spoken out of turn, and every person in this room knows it, because every person in this room grew up under the same Code and knows the graduation by heart.

I let go of his wrist.

He staggers. He doesn't fall, because I've left him enough to stand on, and because falling would cost him the rest of what he walked in here with.

"Sit down," I tell him.

He sits.

The room is so quiet I can hear the clock in the hallway through the door.

---

I go back up. Same pace, same posture — one at a time, no rail.

I sit down and Cassian is looking at me with an expression I have never seen on his face. It isn't horror and it isn't admiration. It's recognition.

He is watching me become my father.

"The motion for war is on the table," I say. "Are there further speakers?"

There are not.

"Then we vote."

It passes. Every seat. Marguerite's hand goes up first, which is expected, and Brodeur's goes up second, which is not — a man who hasn't spoken in this chamber since the last succession votes with the speed of someone who has seen enough.

Laurent votes with his right hand.

Cassian doesn't have a vote. He never has. He stands to my left with his hands behind his back and watches the room commit to a war he built, and his face is the face of a man who has gotten exactly what he wanted and isn't sure he should have.

Was that the point? Did I just give him the thing he's been angling for since the funeral?

---

After, the chamber empties the way water empties from a basin — fast at first, then slow, then the last few hanging on the rim.

Selene stays.

She's still at the back wall. Her arms are still at her sides. Her face has not changed since I broke a man's hand in front of her.

That worries me, and it should. A woman who watches that and doesn't flinch is either numb or dangerous, and she isn't numb.

I come down the stairs a third time.

"You were supposed to be in the west wing," I say.

"You were supposed to not break a man's hand in front of me."

"I told him first."

"I heard you." She looks at my hands. "You told him which hand and why. You quoted the article number."

"That's the process."

"That's not a process." Her voice is steady and her eyes are not. "That's a man telling another man exactly what's about to happen to him and then doing it while a room watches. That's a warning label, not a law."

I look at her.

"My father called it a fence," I say.

"I know what your father called it." She pushes off the wall. "Your father built a fence. You just showed them what's on the other side of it."

She's shaking. Her hands at her sides, her jaw set, and a fine tremor through her shoulders that she is not going to acknowledge and I am not going to name.

"Are you all right?" I ask.

"Don't ask me that right now."

"Selene—"

"I watched you break a man's hand because he pointed at me." Her voice is still steady. The steadiness is costing her. "I need a minute to decide which part of that is the part I'm supposed to feel."

She turns and walks toward the door. At the frame she stops.

"You told him first," she says. Not a question.

"Yes."

"Every time?"

"Every time."

She nods once. Then she's gone, and the door closes, and I'm standing in my father's chamber with the smell of Laurent's broken bone in the air and a war I voted for and a man to my left who arranged the whole of it.

"Well done," Cassian says.

Two words. Warm.

I don't turn around.

"Get me the deployment terms by morning," I say. "And Cassian."

"Yes?"

"If anyone points at her again, I won't name it first."

Silence. A beat of it, the length of a held breath.

"Understood," he says.

The court voted for war. The evidence hasn't caught up to the decision yet. And the man who killed my father just told me *well done* in a voice that sounded like pride.

I sit in the chair and let the clock in the hallway mark the hour.
