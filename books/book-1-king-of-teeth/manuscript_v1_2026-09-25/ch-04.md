# 4 · Selene

He takes the boat out alone on Thursdays.

I know this because a wolf at a bar in Tremé told me six months ago while trying to impress a woman who wasn't listening. He said it the way men say things about other men's routines — as evidence that the man is predictable, which is another word for safe, which is another word for not worth worrying about.

In wolf, *routine* and *territory* are the same word. A man who does the same thing on the same day is a man who has marked the ground under it.

Rémy Thibodeaux goes out at dawn on his boat and comes back at noon with shrimp, and the whole dock knows his schedule, and I have been carrying that sentence like a key for half a year.

Today is Thursday.

---

Barataria smells like itself and nothing else.

Mud. Salt. The low green rot of standing water in a place where water never finishes standing. And underneath it, everywhere, the other thing — the thing I can almost catch and never hold, the smell that makes something in the back of my skull sit up and go very still.

Pack. The whole ground is soaked in it.

I came in by bus and got off two stops early, and I'm walking the last mile on a road with no shoulder and no sidewalk and no reason for a woman in city shoes to be on it.

That's a mistake. I knew it was a damn mistake when I put the shoes on this morning, and I put them on anyway, because I don't own the other kind. The kind you wear to a place like this. Boots with mud on them and a weight to the step that says you know what the ground is.

My mother would have known what shoes to wear.

I stop thinking about that and keep walking.

---

The dock is at the bottom of a shell road that goes from pavement to gravel to nothing in about a quarter mile. There's a fuel station with a tin roof and a dog sleeping under it, and beyond that, the water.

Boats. A lot of them. Shrimp trawlers with their arms up, and smaller things — skiffs, flatboats, a pontoon with a cooler roped to the rail. Everything down here has the look of something that works for a living and doesn't care whether you know it.

I find a spot near the fuel station where I can see the slips without standing in the middle of anything. There's a bench, sun-cracked, with a coffee cup somebody left on it last week.

I sit. I take the cup off and put it on the ground because I need to do something with my hands.

The knife is in my bag. It's been in my bag since the house, and I can feel the shape of it against my hip the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue.

---

I see him at eleven.

He brings the boat in bow-first, which I've been told is wrong for the slip but is how he does it, every time, because — according to the drunk in Tremé — he doesn't like having his back to the water when he ties off.

In wolf, that's not paranoia. It's manners. You don't put your unprotected side to the direction something could come from. It's the same reason they hold doors from the outside.

He's bigger than I expected.

That's a stupid thing to think. I've known his height, his build, the weight of him, from a dozen descriptions in a dozen bars. I could have drawn him from the talk.

But knowing and seeing are different languages, and the translation is never clean. God, it's never clean.

He steps off the boat and ties it with hands that know the rope without looking at it. He's sunburned across the nose and down his forearms, and his shirt is dark with sweat, and his right arm hangs wrong.

That arm. A vampire did that — the King, years back, when Rémy crossed a line on vampire ground. Every wolf in Barataria tells that story the same way. In wolf, the word for the injury is the same as the word for a lesson.

He hauls two crates off the stern and stacks them on the dock, and a man I don't know comes down the slope to help, and they talk.

I can't hear them. I'm too far and the wind is wrong.

But I can see his face, and his face is not the face of a man who killed my parents.

It's the face of a man unloading shrimp on a Thursday. Ordinary. Easy in it. He says something and the other man laughs, and Rémy grins, and his whole body changes shape when he grins — he gets smaller somehow, younger, the bigness of him pulls in.

I hate that I can see that. I hate that I'm sitting on a damn bench cataloging the way a man smiles.

He doesn't drink. I know that because the same drunk told me, unprompted. *Rémy doesn't touch it. Not since the thing with the Ward family.* Shit. The drunk said it as a curiosity, not a confession. A detail about a man's habits, slotted into the kind of information people carry about their neighbors.

I wanted him to drink. I'd planned on it. A drinking man is a careless man, and a careless man is a man whose schedule has soft spots in it.

He's unloading shrimp and grinning at a friend and he doesn't drink.

The knife in my bag is not any heavier than it was this morning. That's a fact.

---

I could do it now.

The dock is quiet. The friend has gone back up the slope with one of the crates, and Rémy is alone with the second one, and there's nobody between me and the end of the slip.

It would be fast. I know where to put it. I've known for years — talked to a man at a bar who described a wolf's vulnerable points while explaining why silver doesn't work, and I sat there with a beer and memorized the geometry of a body I hadn't seen yet.

Fast, and then done, and then the bus back, and the rest of my life would start, and the rest of my life would be the thing I haven't thought about because the damn intention was taking up all the room.

Shit. My hand goes to the bag.

And a voice says, "You look like you need water."

---

She's young. My age, and small for a wolf, and she's holding out a bottle with condensation running down the side of it.

I didn't hear her come up, which means she's either quiet or I was somewhere else. Both of those are bad. Hell, both of those are bad.

"I'm fine," I say.

"You've been sitting in the sun for an hour." She puts the bottle on the bench between us. "The bus doesn't come again until two."

She knows I came by bus. She knows how long I've been here. I don't know whether she's been watching me or whether everybody on this dock knows everything that happens on it, and both options are the same shape.

"Thank you," I say, because there's nothing else to say that isn't a door I don't want to open.

She sits down on the other end of the bench. Not close. Not far enough to be a point about distance. She's wearing a jacket that's too big for her and her hair is pulled back, and she has the kind of face that's going to be something in a few years and hasn't figured that out yet.

Then she breathes in, and her whole expression changes.

Not the way wolves usually change when they catch me. There's no flinch, no pull-back, no face like biting into something wrong.

She tips her head and looks at me the way you'd look at a song you recognize from a room you can't place.

"You smell like my aunt," she says.

The bench is very solid underneath me, and I need it to be, because something in my chest has just come apart at a seam I didn't know was there.

Nobody has ever said that to me.

No wolf has ever stood close enough to smell me and responded with a memory instead of a judgment. I have been read as *wrong* my entire life. Not once as *familiar.*

"Your aunt," I manage.

"She left." The girl shrugs. Not casually — the way you shrug when the thing you're shrugging about is the size of a room. "A long time ago. I was little."

In wolf, *left* and *contaminated* are not the same word, but they share a root. She's used the smaller one. The one that means a person walked out under her own feet.

I don't know if she chose it on purpose. I don't know if she's old enough to know there's a choice.

"I'm sorry," I say. It's the wrong thing. In English it means sympathy. In wolf it means you're accepting a portion of the loss, which I have no right to.

She doesn't seem to mind. She picks at the label on the water bottle and looks out at the dock.

"What's your name?" she asks.

"Selene."

"I'm Delphine." She tears a strip of label off and lets it drop. "You shouldn't be down here, you know."

"I know."

"My dad would — I mean, it's not safe. For someone who—" She stops. Recalibrates. "You should go before the boats come back in."

Her dad. Gaspard. This girl is Gaspard Thibodeaux's daughter, which makes her Rémy's goddamn sister, and I am sitting on a bench twelve feet from the man who killed my parents having a conversation about water with his sister.

She doesn't know. She can't know — she's too young, too open, too willing to sit next to a woman who smells wrong and offer her a bottle. If she knew what I was here for, she wouldn't be on this bench. She'd be between me and the dock, the way wolves stand between their family and a threat.

She's not standing between us. She's sitting beside me, picking at a label, and the sitting is either trust or innocence, and both of those are things I'm about to betray.

"My aunt made tea," she says. "The kind you have to get from Tremé. She used to bring it when she visited."

My mother's tea. The tea I buy from a woman who has never asked me why.

I can't speak. There's a sound in my throat that won't go up or down, and Delphine stands there long enough to see it happen, and she doesn't ask about it.

Then she goes up the slope toward the houses, and I'm alone on the bench with a bottle of water and a knife in my bag and a sentence that has just rearranged something I can't put back.

---

Rémy is still on the dock.

He's hosing the boat down. His back is to me. His bad arm does most of the work and the good one holds the hose, and he has the radio on in the wheelhouse, and I can hear it from here.

I could do it. He's alone, and I'm close, and the girl is gone, and there's nobody.

My hand is on the bag.

My hand stays on the bag.

---

The bus comes at two, and I'm on it, and the knife is still in the bag where it started, and I cannot say why.

That's the sentence. I have lived inside a single intention for four years, and I had a clear path to it today, and I left without doing it, and if you asked me in either language why, I would not have an answer.

Something happened on that bench. A girl said I smelled like her aunt.

That isn't a reason. I know that isn't a reason.

I'm taking the bus home anyway.
