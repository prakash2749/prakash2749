# 27 · Selene

"Three days," Lucian said last night. "Gaspard hands him over on neutral ground. No blood."

Three days? I've had four years.

That sentence is still in my head when I wake up, and it doesn't leave.

I said yes. I looked him in the eye and I said *I'll wait* and I meant it when I said it, and that's the worst part, because a lie you mean when you tell it is the kind you can't take back.

I'm already dressed, boots and jacket and the bag with the knife in it — my father's knife, the one that was always for Rémy Thibodeaux.

A controlled handoff: diplomatic language and neutral ground and the careful machinery of two species agreeing to hand a man across a line.

To a wolf, that's a season, plenty of time for a man to run. Rémy hasn't run since the funeral, but that was before a vampire king started asking about him by name.

Can I wait? I can't, and I've known it since he said it.

---

I take the car, mine, not Lucian's, the one I drove him in the first night, the one with his blood still in the upholstery because I haven't cleaned it and I'm not going to. It starts on the second try, like it always does.

I don't tell Lucian or Silas, and I don't leave a note or send a text or do any of the shit a person does when they're going somewhere they're supposed to go.

I drive south, over the bridge, into the West Bank. The city drops away behind me, a coat I took off. The Quarter is gas lamps and money and the particular silence of things that live forever, and Barataria is water and diesel and the sound of engines that have never stopped running.

My grip is different on the wheel this time, because last time the knife was in the bag like a verdict, final and certain.

The knife is in the bag again, but my grip is loose, as you hold a wheel when you're driving toward something you don't want to do.

Last time I went to Barataria to take a life from Rémy Thibodeaux.

This time I'm going to take one from myself: the version of me that came for him, the wait Lucian asked for, the controlled handoff, the version of this that doesn't end with a dead wolf on a dock and a war in my name.

I'm going to stand in front of him, and I'm going to not kill him, and that is going to be worse than anything I've ever done.

I know where he'll be, because I've known his schedule by heart since I learned it: the dock, the boat, the routine of a man who gets up before dawn and works until the light is wrong. Silas's reports confirmed what I already knew. Thursday, last light, the boat comes in, and he unloads alone.

I park on the shell road above the dock and I wait.

---

He comes in with the tide, right on time.

The boat is small — a shrimper, white hull gone gray, the kind of thing that lives in the water instead of on it. He ties off with one hand because his right arm doesn't work right. It hangs at his side with the patience of a thing that's been broken and healed and never been the same.

Aurelian did that. He put a wound in this man's arm that it has carried for years. The arm still works, and the man still uses it, because wolves don't stop. They just get slower.

He's bigger up close, and sunburned, and he works the rope as if he's done it since he could hold one.

He doesn't see me. Two men are with him on the dock — wolves, both of them, I can smell it from the road — helping with the catch and talking about something that makes Rémy almost laugh.

He has a life. How does the man who killed my parents get a life with ropes and bad jokes and a boat that comes in with the tide? I knew this. I saw it from the bench. But knowing it from a distance and standing in it are different languages, and I speak both.

---

I get out of the car, and the dock goes still when I step onto it.

Wolf-quiet, the silence of a pack that's scented something wrong. The two men stop moving, and their heads come up, and their nostrils flare.

I smell like the vampire house, like Lucian's sheets and Lucian's study and the particular cold that lives in a building full of things that don't age. I've been in House Croix for weeks and it's in my skin and my hair and my clothes, and to a wolf that scent is a flag.

"You're in the wrong fucking place," one of the men says.

"I know where the hell I am."

"Then you know you shouldn't be." He steps forward, measured, the step of a man who's been taught that you don't attack what smells wrong. You identify it. "You're from the house."

"I'm from a bar on Frenchmen Street. Got a problem, asshole? Or just a shitty nose?"

"You know you smell like a vampire's bed? You think we can't tell?"

"I smell like a lot of things. Go fuck yourself."

The second man is moving, flanking wide around the edge of the dock, positioning, because if I'm a provocation, they want to know what kind before they respond.

And then Rémy sees me.

He's at the bow of the boat with a line in his good hand and his face changes as a sky changes, across the whole of it, from the jaw to the eyes.

Does he know who I am? He knows.

He knows what I am, anyway: the daughter, the one who survived, the one who's been standing at the edge of this water for years. He's known it, and he's been waiting for it like a man on a dock waits for weather.

"Stop," he says, to his men, not to me.

"She's from the house—"

"I know where she's from." He ties the line off one-handed, without looking at it. "She's from a kitchen table where a man used to translate contracts, and she's Daniel Ward's daughter, and you're going to step away from her."

The men don't move a goddamn muscle.

"Step away from her," Rémy says. "Now."

They step, because his voice changed, and a wolf's voice changing is its own kind of command.

He looks at me across the dock: weathered wood and salt air and everything he did.

"You came alone?" he says.

"Yes."

"The king knows? Does he know where you are right now?"

"No."

His jaw sets, and he's weighing the same odds I should have weighed before I got in the car.

A woman from the vampire king's house, in Barataria, alone, at night, without permission. What the fuck was I thinking?

"Do you have any idea," he says, "what this looks like?"

"I know what the hell this looks like. Do you think I'm stupid?"

"It looks like a provocation. It looks like Lucian Croix sent a woman into Barataria to start something, and every wolf between here and the bridge is going to hear about it inside the hour."

"Lucian didn't send me. Nobody sent me, goddamn it."

"Then *you* are the provocation, and that's worse." He comes off the boat. His boots hit the dock and the sound of it is heavy and real and I can smell him now — wolf, salt, diesel, and underneath all of it a smell I know from my own skin: guilt. Guilt has a scent, and it smells like a house the morning after.

"You promised him you'd wait," Rémy says.

I go cold. "How do you know that?"

"Because Gaspard told me. He told me the king would send for me, and he told me the woman would wait." He's close now, close enough that I can see lines in his face that weren't there the night he came to our house. "I've been sitting on this dock knowing you'd come, because I've met one other person who carried something for that long, and she didn't wait either."

"Your mother?"

"Mine, yeah."

The silence on the dock is the kind you can hear blood in.

---

One of the wolves moves, the flanking one, the one who went wide. He's behind me now, between me and the road, and his posture has changed from identification to decision.

"She's a truce violation," he says. "The king's woman, in Barataria, unescorted. Doesn't the Alpha need to hear about this?"

"The Alpha is going to hear about it from me," Rémy says, "not from you, and not tonight."

"Rémy— are you fucking serious?"

"I said not tonight."

The man looks at Rémy, and Rémy looks at the man. Something passes between them that I can read in wolf but couldn't translate into English if I had an hour — the full weight of a man's son telling a man's soldier to stand down, and the soldier deciding whether the son has earned it.

The soldier stands down, and he hates it.

Rémy turns back to me. "Go home," he says.

"I'm not— who the fuck are you to send me home?"

"Go home, Selene."

He knows my name. When did he learn it, and from whom? The sound of it in his mouth makes my stomach turn over, because the last man who said my name with that particular weight was my father.

"You killed them," I say.

"Yes."

"You killed my mother and my father and you're standing on a dock telling me to go home? Are you out of your goddamn mind?"

"Yes." He doesn't move, doesn't step back. "And I'm standing between you and two wolves who want to take you to Gaspard as a truce violation, and if Gaspard gets you, the diplomatic version dies tonight, and the king starts a war to get you back, and Cassian gets what he's been building toward for decades."

The name, Cassian's name, in his mouth. Jesus Christ.

"You know?" I say.

"I know who told me to kill your father. I know why. I've known the whole time, and I've been carrying it like you've been carrying that knife."

My hand is on the bag, and I haven't opened it. The knife is inside, under the flap, and my fingers are on the zipper. I can feel the weight of it like a third person on this dock.

"Go home," he says again. "Come back with your king, and let this happen the way it's supposed to happen."

"The way it's supposed to happen is you dead on this dock."

"Maybe." He looks at the knife, through the bag, like the canvas isn't there. "But not tonight. Tonight you're in my territory, and my men have your scent, and the Alpha is going to know by morning. And if you kill me here, right now, it isn't revenge. It's murder. It's a woman from the king's house killing a wolf on wolf ground, and that's the war Cassian has been engineering since before you were born."

I can't fucking breathe.

He's right, and I can hear both versions of it. The English version is logic, and the wolf version is simpler: *You owe me now. I'm standing between you and a war, and you owe me for it, and you can't kill a man you owe.*

I came to Barataria to take something from him. He gave me my life instead, and I can't afford what that goddamn costs.

---

Delphine comes out of the dark from behind the truck, where she's been, I think, since before I stepped onto the dock, watching, like she watched me at the gas station.

She doesn't interfere. She stands at the edge of the light with her arms crossed and her face steady. She looks at me like I looked at Isabeau, a woman who sees exactly what's happening and has decided not to speak.

Then she speaks. "He told you what he was told," she says, to me, not to Rémy. "Ask him who told him. Ask him that, and then go home."

Rémy's face goes still.

"Who told you?" I say.

"Don't you know who told me?"

"Say it. Say the name, damn you."

"Cassian Vale." He sets the name down like something heavy. "Cassian Vale told me your father was a vampire informant, that he had a report that would get Gaspard assassinated, and that the report had to be stopped before it reached the court." He looks at the water. "I was twenty-seven, and I believed him. I believed him because he knew things about the pack that no vampire should know, and he spoke to me like a man speaks to someone he respects, and he asked about my mother's house."

*He asked about my mother's house.*

The sentence goes straight through me. Cassian, the warmth, how he asks about your family because he means it, and the meaning is the weapon.

"I'm not asking for forgiveness," Rémy says. "I don't have the right. I killed your parents and I knew what I was doing and I was wrong, and there isn't a version of that sentence that makes it smaller."

I look at him, and at the dock and the boat and the water and the wolves and Delphine standing in the dark with her arms crossed.

I take my hand off the bag.

He saved my life on this dock tonight, and the man I came to kill is the man I owe. There is no word for that in either language.

I walk back to the car without running or looking at him. I put one foot on the shell road after the other and I get in and I start the engine and I drive north.

Behind me, in the mirror, Delphine walks Rémy to the edge of the dock. Is she the only one who knew I'd come? She's the only one who stayed.

---

I drive home, and *home* is wrong. I drive to the house where a vampire king is waiting for me to come back from a place I promised not to go.

The bridge, the city, the Quarter, the gate.

Silas is at the door, and he looks at me and he knows, maybe not where I went, but that I went somewhere I shouldn't have. He reads it like he reads ground.

"He's in the study," Silas says.

I go to the study. Lucian is at the desk with the lamp on, and he looks up when I come in. His face does a thing I haven't seen before: recognition, a man who told a woman to wait and knew she wouldn't.

"You went, ghost," he says.

"I went."

"Alone?"

"Alone. Who else would I have taken?"

"To Barataria. Into pack territory. Without telling me, without telling Silas, and without the diplomatic cover that was days from being ready."

"Yes."

He stands and comes around the desk, moving as he does when the control is costing him, slowly, each step a decision instead of a habit.

"They have your scent now," he says. "Every wolf on that dock knows who you are, and that you came from this house, and that you came alone."

"I know."

"If Gaspard reads this as a provocation— do you understand what I'd have to do?"

"He won't." I swallow. "Rémy stopped it. He stood between me and his men and he sent me home."

Something happens in Lucian's face. The cold goes out of it and something worse comes in: the hurt of a man who trusted someone with a promise and watched them break it.

"You promised me," he says. "Didn't you?"

"I know."

"You looked me in the eye and you said *I'll wait.* Christ, Selene."

"I know."

"And you went anyway."

"Yes." I don't look away. "Because I've waited my whole adult life and three more days was too long and I was wrong. I was wrong and Rémy saved me from the consequences of being wrong, and my parents' killer just saved my life, and I don't know what the hell to do with any of it."

The desk is between us and the lamp makes his face half gold and half dark, and his hands are very still.

"Don't do it again, ghost," he says. "Do you hear me? Don't you ever fucking do that again."

"I won't. I swear to God I won't."

"You will." His mouth doesn't quite smile. "But not tonight."

The study is still, and the chair behind him is turned to face the desk — I only notice it now. He turned it. Who is he getting ready for?

I sit in the guest chair and I put the bag on the floor and I close my eyes.

The knife is still in the bag. The knife was always for Rémy Thibodeaux, and tonight I stood on a dock with my hand on the zipper and a man who killed my parents stepped between me and a war.

I don't have a word for what I owe him.

He comes around the desk and pulls me up out of the chair by the front of my jacket. The bag falls off my knee and the knife inside it thumps on the floor. He kisses me hard enough to split my lip on my own teeth. I taste blood, and so does he. He makes a sound against my mouth that I feel in my knees. His hand closes on my throat, not squeezing, just there, just proving I'm still under it.

"I listened to your pulse from across a river tonight," he says against my mouth. "Don't you ever make me do that again."

My thighs are shaking, and I'm wet, and furious about it, and I don't let go of his shirt.

"You're angry," I say against his mouth.

"I'm fucking terrified, ghost. Angry is just the part you can see." His tongue drags over my split lip, tasting it, and his thigh pushes between mine, and I grind down on it without deciding to, soaked through my jeans. "Feel that? That's what an hour of not knowing does to me."

"Then stop not knowing."

"I intend to. All night."

"Bed," he says. "Before I decide the dock deserves a punishment."

I open one eye. "Is that a threat, Your Majesty?"

"It's a menu."
