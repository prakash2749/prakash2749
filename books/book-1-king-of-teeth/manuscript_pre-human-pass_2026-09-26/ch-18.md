# 18 · Lucian

Thibault has stopped looking at the lamp and started looking at the door.

That means he's decided. A man who watches a light source is a man waiting. A man who watches an exit is a man who's made a plan for what comes next, and the plan includes leaving.

I've kept him ten days, fed, watered, allowed to stand and walk the room. Silas brings his meals, and nobody else comes down here.

He's more useful than I expected, a prisoner who has become a witness. A man who saw the last minutes of my father's life from the gallery rail, who has no stake in any version of the answer, and who tells it the same way every time I ask.

I've asked three times and the story hasn't moved. That's either a man with a good memory or a man with a better lie, and I'm betting on the first.

Today I'm asking a different fucking question.

---

"Tell me about Gaspard," I say.

He looks up from the chair. His face does a small recalculation — he's been waiting for the next round of questions about the chamber, and this isn't that.

"The Alpha."

"Your Alpha. Tell me about him."

"What do you want to know?"

"Everything you'd tell a man who's never met him."

Thibault rubs his jaw. It's a habit — he does it when he's sorting through what he knows, trying to decide which piece goes on top.

"He's old," he says. "Not old the way you're old. Old the way a man is old who's done one thing for forty years and the thing has worn a groove in him."

"What's the thing?"

"Keeping us alive." He says it simply. "The whole job. The pack is — you have to understand, from outside it looks like an army. It's a town. People with kids and boats and mortgages, and Gaspard's job since his father died is making sure the town stays standing."

"Against us."

"Against everything. The water, the city, the humans, you." He counts them on his fingers. "In that order. You're not the biggest thing trying to kill us. You're not even second."

I sit with that. It isn't what the court would say. The court would say the wolves are a military force at our border, organized around aggression, bred for it.

Thibault is telling me they're a fishing village with teeth.

"Why did he send you into the chamber?"

"Because somebody told him there was an opening."

"Who?"

"I don't know. Above my head." He shifts in the chair. "The order came down through the captains. We were told the gallery would be open — the windows unlatched — and that the council would be seated. We were told to go fast and go hard and get out."

"And the target?"

"Chaos. Not a kill." He looks at me steadily. "I know what you think. You think we came for your father. We didn't. We came for the room."

"And my father was in the room."

"Your father was the room." His voice shifts down, into a register that sounds like remembering. "You have to understand what he was to us."

"A predator."

"A king." Thibault leans forward. "A king with teeth who never once used them on us."

The sentence goes into the room and the room holds it.

*King of teeth.*

The old wolf on the levee said it as an insult. A man who is nothing but the bite. Appetite crowned and called a king.

Thibault is saying the opposite with the same words.

A king who had teeth — the worst teeth, the oldest, the ones that could have taken the whole pack and the water it sits on — and who never once put them in a wolf.

"Say that again," I tell him.

"A king with teeth who never used them." Thibault's hands are on his knees. "Forty years of truce. Forty years where the Alpha woke up every morning knowing the man across the water could end it before lunch. And every morning that man got up and went to his table and didn't."

"That's restraint."

"That's more than restraint. That's a decision. Every day."

He looks at me as the old wolf on the levee did, measuring and unafraid, a man trying to understand.

"The wolves at the levee called me that," I say. "King of teeth. They meant the other thing."

"They meant the first thing. The young ones always mean the first thing." Thibault shakes his head. "The old ones know. Your father was the king of teeth because he had them and he kept them in his mouth. That's harder than using them. Fuck. It is. Anybody can bite."

Fuck. He's right.

I've been carrying that phrase since the raid. I put it in my pocket and I liked the weight of it — the insult, the sharp edges, the name for a thing that is nothing but appetite.

I didn't know there were two of them.

Two versions of the same words. One that means *monster* and one that means *the man who chose not to be.* And the difference between them is what I've been arguing with my father about since I was old enough to argue.

I told him mercy was weakness.

A wolf in my basement is telling me my father's restraint was the most frightening thing about him.

---

"He was winning," I say.

"He was winning."

"And the wolves knew it. Your captains, your Alpha — everybody who put men through those windows knew they were sending them at a man who would tear them apart."

"Yes."

"So why go."

"Because somebody told Gaspard there was an opening." Thibault's voice goes careful again. "Not a chance to win. An opening. A window that was unlatched and a room that was full. The idea wasn't to kill your father. The idea was to make it look like we tried."

The fucking lamp flickers.

"Make it look like you tried," I say.

"So that afterward, when the council voted, the vote would already be made." He meets my eyes. "Wolves in the gallery. Wolves at the windows. A whole court watching the glass come down. That's a war vote, Your Majesty. That's the vote already cast."

I've been asking who killed my father. I've been asking it about a blade and a wound and a man with a key.

Thibault is telling me a larger story. He's telling me that the wolves were brought into that chamber not to kill a king but to provide a reason — a spectacle, a narrative, a room full of witnesses who would agree that the wolves had declared war.

And the person who arranged the opening — the person who unlatched the gallery windows — would have needed to be inside the chamber.

On our side.

Fuck.

---

"The man who told Gaspard," I say. "Did he have a name?"

"If he did, Gaspard never said it to me."

"What did Gaspard say about him?"

"That he'd been reliable. That the information had been good before, and that the cost was reasonable." Thibault pauses. "And that the man asked about his wife."

The room goes cold.

It's pressure more than temperature. The air before a fist closes, except the fist is inside the building and it has a name I'm not going to say.

Both my hands press down on the arms of the chair. The wood creaks. I can feel the grain finding the line where it would split if I let it.

I don't let it. I hold the pressure and the pressure holds me, and the chair takes the weight of a sentence I can't say out loud.

*He asked about his wife.*

Cassian asks about wives. He asks about mothers and houses and the health of a grandmother's chest. He does it like other men breathe. Constantly, and with a warmth that is entirely real.

"Describe him," I say.

"I never met him. I told you — this was above me."

"Gaspard described him."

"Gaspard said old. Older than he looked. Silver. Good coat." Thibault shifts. "He said the man was sad."

I close my eyes.

*He was sad.*

Forget the silver and the coat. A sad old man in a good coat who provided intelligence and asked about your family and was reliable.

I could have a hundred men who fit that. This city is full of old men in good coats who carry grief like dock workers carry rope. So long that the weight has become the posture.

But it sits on only one of them like that. And I fucking know it.

---

I don't say his name.

I don't say it because saying a name in a room with a witness is a door that closes behind you, and I'm not ready for what's on the other side.

What I do is stand up, and push the chair back, and walk to the door. My hands are steady, and that's worth something, if not much.

"Am I done?" Thibault asks.

"Almost."

"Almost means a lot of things in a room with a locked door."

"It means I have something to check." I turn. "You'll be fed tonight. Silas will come."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow I'll either let you go or I won't."

He nods. He's been useful, and he knows useful has a shelf life.

"Your Majesty," he says.

I stop.

"Your father earned that name. He earned it by having it in him and not using it." Thibault leans back in the chair. "What you do with it is your business. But I'd like it on the record that a wolf said it to you and meant the second one."

I go up the stairs.

---

The archive room. Six letters, all signed with the hook.

I spread them beside the ledger and look at the marks side by side, and they are the same writing. Selene told me that. I didn't hear her because the hearing would have cost me more than I was ready to pay.

Five of the letters are routine — archive instructions, wine for the council, Ambroise's schedule.

The sixth is about a payment to a translator in the Bywater.

My father didn't write it. The writing is neater than his, more deliberate, with a particular way of crossing a *t* that I have seen on every note Cassian has left on this desk since I was a child.

Cassian.

I let the name into the room. It sits where it sits, and the room doesn't change, and nothing in the house breaks, and the lamp goes on humming.

Tomorrow I'm going to sit across from my uncle and ask him about a translator in the Bywater. I'm going to ask like he taught me to ask. Around the edges, with warmth, with the question about somebody's family that sounds like care and is.

I'm going to use his own instrument on him.
