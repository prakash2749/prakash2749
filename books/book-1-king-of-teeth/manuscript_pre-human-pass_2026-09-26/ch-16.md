# 16 · Lucian

I have decided to lie to the only person I trust, and I am sitting in my father's chair rehearsing it.

The words are simple. *Wolf bites, heavy damage, the worst at the hip.* Any of those sentences could be true. Most of them were true, once, before Ambroise turned the body over and showed me what was underneath.

What I'm rehearsing is the rest of me, the hands and the eyes, the part of a body that people read when they've stopped listening to the mouth.

Cassian has been reading me since before I could read myself. He taught me to sit still. He taught me that silence was a room you could put someone in and see what they brought to it.

He's going to walk in here and sit down and ask what Ambroise found, because he's asked every few days since the funeral, because he loved my father more than anyone alive except me.

And I'm going to lie to him. I'm going to tell him the wolves did it, and it's going to come out of my mouth as plain as a fact, and if I do it right he'll carry it home with him.

---

He comes at the usual hour.

There's no knock. There has never been a fucking knock. The door is open — it's been open since Tuesday — and he comes through it like he comes through every door in this house. As if it was left open for him.

It was. It always had been.

"Boy."

"Come in."

He takes the chair across the desk. The one by the window — my father's guest chair, the one that faces the desk instead of the courtyard. He sat in this chair every evening for longer than I've been alive, and his body knows it like a bed.

Selene is on the window seat. She's been there since I asked her. She's occupying the room more than sitting in on anything. The ledger is closed on her knee, her hand flat on the cover like it might open on its own. Her body is angled away from the desk as though she's part of the furniture.

She's not part of the furniture. She's the only person in this room who can hear both layers of what's about to happen.

Cassian glances at her. The glance is warm — his glances are always warm — and it carries a weight I can now read. He's placing her, deciding what she changes about the room.

He settles without rushing. He has a glass — he poured it himself on the way in, from the decanter on the shelf, and the fact that he's comfortable enough to do that is the whole history of his place in this house.

"Ambroise," he says.

"I went down yesterday."

"And?"

"Wolf bites." I keep my hands on the desk. "Four of them. Heavy damage. The worst was at the hip — took a piece out."

He nods. His face settles, as it always does when we talk about the death, water finding the bottom of a well, and it isn't performed.

"And the chest?"

"Ambroise thinks one of the bites went deep enough to catch an artery underneath. The healing would have prioritized the artery over the surface wound, which is why it looked—"

"Like he was still standing."

"Like he was still standing. He was. The internal damage was catching up."

Cassian turns his glass on his knee. A slow half-turn. He turns everything like that, a man who has outlived the need to hurry.

"So he was fighting," he says. "And winning."

"And winning. And bleeding underneath it."

"And nobody saw the moment."

"Nobody in the room was watching him at the end. They were watching the wolves."

"Yes." He takes a sip. "Nobody looks at anything else, in a room with wolves in it."

He's said that before, the same sentence in the same cadence, and I didn't notice the first time.

I notice now, and the noticing goes into a room in my head where I'm keeping the things I notice about Cassian, and the room is getting furnished.

God help me if the fucking furniture matches.

"How is Ambroise?" he asks.

"Tired. I took him upstairs. Made him eat."

"Good." Cassian smiles, and it reaches his eyes, because with him it always does. "Did he tell you about the wife?"

"He mentioned her."

"Lovely woman. Died young. Young for a human, I mean, which is any reasonable schedule." He shakes his head. "He keeps her portrait in the cold room. Between the instruments. I've always found that either very sweet or very troubling."

"Both."

"Both. Yes." He drinks. "You're being careful with this."

"With what?"

"The information. Ambroise's findings." His eyes come up to mine. "You haven't brought it to the council."

"Not yet."

"Why not?"

This is the moment. The lie has to live in my answer, and the answer has to sound like a man being cautious rather than a man being dishonest.

"Because the council will turn wolf bites into a war inside an afternoon," I say. "And I'm not ready for a war."

He takes that in. He holds it like a glass he's going to taste before he commits.

"Your father would have told them on the first night," he says.

"My father would have told them everything, and the council would have drafted a declaration before the wine was finished."

"That's true." A pause. "The cost of honesty in a room full of people with agendas."

"I'd rather have the facts before I give them the feeling."

His chin lifts, a movement smaller than approval. A man who expected to be right, finding that he is.

"You're growing into this," he says.

"Don't."

"I'm not flattering you. I'm observing." He sets the glass down on the desk. "Your father was a great king and a poor politician. You are going to be the opposite, and the city will be better for it."

I don't answer that, because the answer would be something about the desk I'm sitting at and the man who sat at it before me, and the words for that are in a language I speak badly.

"How is your mother's house?" he asks. "In Toulouse."

"Standing."

"Go, when this is finished."

"You always say that."

"Because you never go." He stands. He gathers his coat off the back of the chair, where he draped it when he came in, and he does up the buttons with the same patient attention he gives to everything. "Lucian."

"Mm."

"You're going to need people you can tell the truth to." His hand is on the doorframe. "Choose them carefully. And choose them soon."

He goes.

---

I spread my hand on the desk where my father's papers were and push down.

The wood creaks. A long, low sound, the sound of old growth being asked to hold a weight it wasn't built for. My fingers are white against the grain.

"He didn't ask," I say.

Selene is still on the window seat. The ledger is closed on her knee. She's been watching the door Cassian went through with both registers running, and her face has the expression I'm learning to recognize — the one that means she heard a second sentence under the one everybody else heard.

"I know," she says. "That's worse."

"He should have asked about the blade wound. He should have asked whether the bites were survivable. He should have—"

"He should have questioned it." She looks at me. "A man who doesn't question a lie is either trusting or has already seen the body. I know which one you're thinking. I know which one is true."

"They're the same."

"They're not." She stands. "You're thinking he trusts you. That he took the lie because he loves you and has no reason to doubt you. That's the version on top."

"And underneath?"

"Underneath, a man who already knows what Ambroise found doesn't need to ask about it. He sits in a chair and turns his glass and nods because the performance requires a nod, and the nod is as honest as anything else he's ever done in this room."

The desk creaks again. I realize I'm still pushing.

I lift my palm off the wood. There's a mark in the finish — a white crescent from the pressure of my palm.

"He told me to choose people I can tell the truth to," I say. "He said it while standing in the door of a room where I'd just lied to his face."

Fuck.

Selene looks at the mark on the desk. She looks at my hand.

"Your hands do that," she says. "When the rest of you is still."

"Do what?"

"Tell the truth."

---

I sit with it. Fuck.

The lie is in the room now, and the room is the same as it was before, and Cassian is walking down my father's hallway with his coat buttoned, and he's going home to a house I've never been inside, and the glass he left on my desk is still warm from his hand.

The bastard didn't question it.

I keep arriving at it. He asked what Ambroise found, and I told him a version, and he turned it over, and he set it down.

He didn't ask about the blade wound. He didn't ask about the entry point. He didn't ask whether Ambroise had found anything that didn't fit the story of a king savaged by wolves.

And I'm sitting here trying to decide whether a man who doesn't question a lie is a man who trusts you, or a man who doesn't need to question it because he already knows what the truth is.

I don't decide. I can't fucking decide.

I pick up his glass and I put it on the shelf with the decanter, and I wipe the ring it left on the desk, and I go to the window and look out at the courtyard where he taught me to hold a blade.

Every evening, for years. My father was busy being a king, and Cassian was in the courtyard with a boy and a piece of steel, and the boy is sitting at the king's desk now, and the steel is in his coat.

Choose them carefully, he said. From the doorway.

The bastard.

I stand at the window until the courtyard goes dark.

Then I go down to the archive and I pull every letter Cassian has written to this house in the past year, and I look at the mark at the bottom of each one.

My father's hook. The little fishhook figure from the household notes. Cassian signs with it too, and he always has. I've seen it a thousand times and I have never once asked which of them drew it first.

I look at it for a long time.

Then I put the letters back, and I go and find the woman in the west wing.

"Ghost."

She looks up from the bed. She's been waiting, and she won't admit that.

"Open it," I tell her. "The ledger."

Her hand goes flat on the cover. "Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow."

That mark is in my head now. It stays there until it tells me what it means.
