# 6 · Selene

The bar is full and nobody is drinking.

I know the tell. I've worked enough Friday nights to know what a crowd that came for the music looks like, and this isn't it. These people came because they didn't want to be home.

Antoine has the news on behind the bar with the sound off, and nobody is watching it, and everybody is watching it.

The crawl at the bottom mentions a disturbance in Barataria. Gas leak, they're calling it. An evacuation of several homes on the south end, two injuries reported, no fatalities.

In English, that's a gas leak.

In the other language, the one nobody in this room speaks and everybody in this room feels in their joints, something came through Barataria last night and the city can feel the bruise.

The old locals know. They don't have the words, but they have the grammar. Two centuries of living on top of this have given them a folk knowledge that works as crudely as a tourniquet. Ugly, but enough.

Don't take the second drink. Don't walk the levee after dark. If a man at a bar doesn't blink for too long, find somewhere else to sit.

If the news says *gas leak* in Barataria, stay out of the Quarter for a week.

That one doesn't come with a reason you could put on a bumper sticker. But the woman at the end of my bar, local, sixty-something, with a purse she's held in her lap all night, knows it anyway. She came in because a crowd is safer than a house. She ordered a tonic water and she's been nursing it forever.

She doesn't know about vampires, or about wolves.

She knows the air changed yesterday, the same way you know a storm is coming when the birds shut up.

"Another?" I ask her.

"I'm fine, baby." She pats the bar. "Just sitting."

"Kitchen's closing in an hour."

"I know." She looks at the TV. "They say what started it?"

"Gas leak."

"Mm." She turns her glass on its napkin. "My grandmother used to say when the gas leaks in Jean Lafitte, you stay on this side of the canal."

"Smart woman."

"She was a frightened woman. That's different." She picks up her purse. "Same thing, though, in the end."

She puts a five on the bar for a three-dollar tonic and leaves. I put the five in the jar and wipe the bar and think about the difference between smart and frightened, and whether a woman who can hear both languages has any fucking right to be either.

---

Around ten, two wolves from Tremé come in and sit where I can hear them.

I know them like you know regulars you'd rather not have. The heavy one is Luc. The one with the bad teeth is his cousin, and I've never needed his name. They drink Abita and tip in quarters and talk in front of me like I'm furniture that pours.

Tonight they don't even order, they just sit.

"Nobody died," Luc says.

"I heard."

"Houses left lit. Old Fontenot's kitchen light was on the whole time. The whole time, man."

"I *heard.*"

"So what's that?"

The cousin stares at his hands. "That's him saying he could have."

"Could have what?"

"All of it." He finally looks up. "The son walked through the settlement like he was looking for his keys. Put Arceneaux through his own wall with one hand. Flat. Like this." He lays his palm on the bar. "And then he *left.*"

"That's worse."

"That's what I'm saying."

I set two Abitas down in front of them that nobody asked for.

Luc looks at me for the first time in a year of drinking here. "On the house?"

"You look like hell. You need it."

He doesn't thank me, because wolves don't thank people who smell like me. Fuck 'em. But he drinks it.

---

The cousin's right, and it's worse than he knows.

A raid that kills is simple. You bury your dead and you go to war, because war is the answer to dead, and the grammar is old enough that nobody has to look it up.

A raid that leaves your lights on and your people breathing is a sentence the pack has no word for, and they're going to spend the next week trying to translate it.

I've been translating it all goddamn day.

What the dead King's son said last night is: *I am not my father. I could have been worse.*

That's two sentences, and most people will only hear one of them.

And I keep thinking about the other thing. The man at my bar, turning a glass he didn't drink, reading my windows like a lease. He walked into Barataria and every wolf there got out of his way.

I said I needed somebody the wolves were afraid of.

Jesus Christ. Be careful what you say out loud in this city.

---

The rush doesn't come. Nine o'clock, ten, and the crowd stays thin and frightened and nursing drinks it doesn't want.

Antoine cuts the kitchen early. He sends the dishwasher home and does the pots himself, and I hear him back there banging a stockpot against the sink with more force than the grease requires.

"You okay?" I ask through the window.

"My cousin's in Barataria."

"Jesus. Is she all right?"

"She's fine. Everybody's fine. That's the problem." He sets the pot down. "When everybody's fine, that means somebody decided everybody gets to be fine. And that somebody gets to decide again tomorrow."

Shit. He's right.

Pack has a single word for that. It means a debt you owe because somebody declined to collect it.

Antoine doesn't have that word. He has a stockpot and a sink and the ache in his forearms when he scrubs.

"Go home," I tell him. "I'll close. Get the hell out of here."

"You sure?"

"I'm sure."

"Lock the alley door."

"I always lock the alley door."

"Lock it twice." He takes his apron off and hangs it on the hook and looks at me like he's going to say something about my tourist, and then he doesn't.

The door swings shut behind him, and I'm alone in the bar with a few customers and a television nobody has turned off.

---

I close early, at midnight, and it'll cost me. But a wrong crowd doesn't spend money, it just takes up space, and I can't afford to waste either.

I lock up and count the register and put the envelope in the safe, and then I sit on my crate in the alley and look at my phone.

No messages.

I open the map. Barataria is a green smear south of the canal, and the settlement is a cluster of dots near the water, and somewhere in that cluster is a dock with a shed on it and a man who takes his boat out on Thursdays.

I went there and sat on a bench, and a girl told me I smelled like her aunt.

And I left without doing what I've been building toward for four goddamn years.

---

Here's what I'm going to do instead.

The city is afraid, both halves of it, the human half that doesn't know what it's afraid of, and the other half that knows exactly.

When a city is afraid, people go where they feel safe. Humans go to bars with crowds and light. Wolves go to Barataria and close the gate.

Vampires go to the places they own.

There's a club on Perdido Street I've never been inside. I've stood across the street from it and counted the cars, and the cars are always expensive and the people who get out of them are always beautiful and they always go in through the side.

There's no listing and no sign. The windows are painted black from the inside, and when the door opens, the music makes your sternum hum.

Everyone who works a bar on Frenchmen knows about Perdido, and nobody goes. It's theirs, same as Barataria is the wolves' and the Marchand house is the witch's and certain blocks of Tremé belong to nobody. Even the drunks know better.

I've heard things. That the drinks are free if the right people like you. That the people who go in come out tired in a way sleep won't fix. That the tips are extraordinary and the staff doesn't talk about the back hallways.

The dead King's son drinks there. Every wolf in Tremé knows it and hates knowing it.

Will he be there tonight? I have no idea, and no idea whether the raid changed his habits or kept them, and no way to find out without walking in.

But afraid means distracted, and distracted means a woman who walks into a room she doesn't belong in might get a few minutes before anyone notices.

A few minutes is a conversation. Shit, a few minutes is plenty. A conversation is a way across the water, and it's the only way across I've got.

---

I go home and shower and stand in front of my closet.

Two years of bartender clothes, black shirts and dark jeans and shoes that don't slip on a wet floor. Nothing in here was chosen. Everything in here was selected, and that's a whole different language.

At the back there's a dress.

It's the one nice thing I own that I didn't buy for somebody's funeral. Black, simple, and it does a thing to my shoulders that I've never liked and have been told twice is the point.

I put it on. I look at myself in the mirror on the back of the bathroom door, the one with the crack in the corner I keep meaning to replace.

The woman in the mirror is small and dark-haired, and she's about to walk into a club where the most dangerous things in this city go to drink, because the man she needs is across a body of water she can't cross alone.

God help her. Honestly.

Then the back of my neck goes cold. Shit.

It's below sound, a frequency in the skin, the kind the wolf-half registers before the rest of me gets the memo. My shoulders draw together. The fine hair on my forearms lifts, and for one breath the dress stops being fabric and becomes a membrane, and everything beyond it is paying attention.

I stand very still. Oh, fuck.

It's him. It's the corner of my bar when he walked in, the air going out of it, except the bar is eleven blocks away and I'm in my bathroom.

It's older than wolf, lower, colder, and I swear to God I can smell it through a wall and a mile of city. Winter in a room with no windows. My body leans toward it as if it's a voice I can almost hear, and the voice isn't fading.

The reaching stretches farther than it's ever gone. My pulse picks up and my skin goes tight and I can feel the shape of whatever's out there as you feel fingers a breath before they touch you. The heat of it. The weight. The gap about to close.

It doesn't close, it passes. Whatever it was moves on, and the reaching comes back empty, same as always, and I'm standing in my bathroom in a black dress with my palm flat on the mirror and my breath fogging the glass.

But it was closer than it has ever been.

My hands are shaking. Holy shit, my hands are shaking.

It isn't fear, because I know what fear does to me, and this is the other thing. This is heat, low and stupid and getting worse. Oh, for fuck's sake.

I'm wet. From *that.* From a frequency.

"Get it together," I tell the mirror. The mirror doesn't have notes.

The knife is on the counter, and I look at it.

I leave it there.

Tonight I'm not carrying a blade. Tonight I'm carrying a face and a voice and the ability to hear both sides of any sentence in any room.

That's the inheritance. My father taught me to listen, and my mother gave me something to listen with.

I put my keys in the small bag, the one with no room for anything else, and lock the apartment behind me, and walk out into a city that's afraid of itself tonight.

I'm not afraid. I should be. That's probably important.

Perdido is a long walk in shoes that aren't built for it, in a dress I've worn once, toward a room full of things that drink.

I'm going where they go.
