I go out in the evening, which I haven't done since the funeral.

I go as a man with a coat and a thirst and no desire to be recognized, to a bar on Magazine, a place my father liked because nobody in it knew what he was and the bartender called him Aury.

It's still here, and the bartender has changed and the drinks haven't.

The booth in the back corner is empty. My father used to sit there, never in the center, always against the wall, with his back to the wood and his coat folded on the bench beside him. He held his glass by the base and turned it twice before he drank, and I never asked why, and now I can't.

He ordered rye, neat, and he'd give the bartender a bill that was too large and say *keep it,* and the bartender would nod. Nothing else would pass between them: no name, no title, no history. A man sits down next to me, two stools closer than the empty bar requires.

"Terrible business," the man says, looking at his own glass. "About the King."

"Yes."

"I didn't know him well. Met him once, at a thing at the Marchand house, big man, warm." He drinks. "The condolences of a grateful city."

The formula is wrong, and who gets it wrong in this city?

I know the condolence forms of this city. *The condolences of a grateful city* is what you say to a widow, not to a son, one register off — the register of distance rather than proximity.

A man who knew the forms would have said *the condolences of a grieving house.* A man who was coached would have said *our sympathies to the Crown.*

This man said neither. Who coaches a man that badly?

He finishes his drink and puts money on the bar, more than the drink costs: another form, and it's the right one.

"A good man," he says. "Hard to replace, isn't he?"

He leaves, and I sit with the drink I didn't touch and the wrong word, and I don't let go of either.
