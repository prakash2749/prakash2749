# 21 · Lucian

Two people want two different wars from me, and I'm running out of ways to say no.

Marguerite has me cornered in the morning room. She's brought three Elders, because Marguerite never brings one person to a conversation that requires three.

"The continent has noticed," she says. "I've had a letter from the Aldric cousins in Lyon. A *letter*, Lucian. They don't write letters. They send them when they want the postmark in the record."

"Let them send what they like."

"And the Conclave has inquired."

That lands differently, because the Conclave doesn't inquire. The Conclave ratifies, at a distance, with the disinterest of an institution that considers the colonies a hobby. If they're asking, someone has been talking, and the talking has a fucking purpose behind it.

"Who wrote to the Conclave?" I say.

Marguerite's mouth thins. "I'm looking into it."

"Look faster."

The Elders shift. One of them — Roche, old line, no ambition past his chair — clears his throat.

"There's a feeling in the council," he says. "That we're waiting for something that may not come."

"It's coming."

"You've been saying that for weeks."

"I'll keep saying it."

---

Cassian arrives after the Elders leave. The bastard always knows when.

He's warm, always warm, and the warmth has been the same temperature since I was a boy, whether from consistency or practice. I used to know which.

"The council is restless," he says. He sits in the chair across from the fireplace, the one my father used to offer guests.

"The council is always restless."

"This is different. They want the war, Lucian. As a feeling, not a policy. They want to *do something*, and a crowned king is a king who can authorize them to do it."

"I'm fucking aware."

"You're stalling."

"I'm investigating."

He gives me the old look, warm eyes and a patient mouth, a man who has never once been in a hurry to tell you you're wrong.

"Your father once told me," he says, "that the difference between patience and hesitation is that patience has an end in mind. Does yours?"

The sentence fits. It slides into the conversation like every piece of advice he gives. True, useful, arriving in the exact shape of a clean conscience.

But I've heard that sentence before, the construction if not the words. The same rhythm, the same pivot on *your father,* the same warmth in the handing-over. He's said something shaped like this to me at least three times since Tuesday. Each time it's pointed me somewhere true.

I'm not sure anymore whether the pointing is the point.

"I have an end in mind," I say. The bastard doesn't need to know which.

"Good." He stands. "How is the Marchand woman? Silas was there again."

"She's well."

"And her house?"

"Standing."

"Good." He adjusts his cuffs. "Houses should stand."

He goes, and the warmth goes with him, and I sit with the shape of it, the concern, the pivot to somebody's wellbeing. He makes it feel like love every time.

---

I go out in the evening, which I haven't done since the funeral.

I go as a man with a coat and a thirst and no desire to be recognized. The bar is on Magazine, a place my father liked because nobody in it knew what he was and the bartender called him Aury.

It's still here. The bartender has changed and the drinks haven't.

The booth in the back corner is empty. My father used to sit there, never in the center, always against the wall, with his back to the wood and his coat folded on the bench beside him. He held his glass by the base and turned it twice before he drank, and I never asked why, and now I can't.

He ordered rye, neat, and he'd give the bartender a bill that was too large and say *keep it,* and the bartender would nod, and nothing else would pass between them. No name. No title. No history. Just a man with a glass of rye in a bar on Magazine Street, turning the glass twice because the turning was the ritual, and the ritual was the rest.

The booth smells like wood and old varnish and the warmth of a seat sat in by strangers who never knew the man beside them had been alive since before the city was named.

I sit at the end of the bar with a glass of red and I look at the room and I don't know why my father chose this city.

That thought catches me. It's been sitting at the back of my mouth like a taste I can't place. Why New Orleans. He had a whole fucking continent. He had houses in Toulouse and a standing in Lyon and a name the Conclave treated with near-warmth.

He came here. He stayed.

I try to finish the sentence — *he came here because* — and I don't have the end of it.

Commerce. The port. The old families. The infrastructure a species accumulates over centuries. Those are the words everybody uses and none of them are the reason.

He came here because —

I change the subject in my own head.

---

A man sits down next to me.

He's vampire. I know that like I know weather. Established, comfortable, the kind of person who has been in this city long enough to have habits and not long enough to have history.

He orders a glass of a brandy I've seen at the Aldric house and slides a second one down the bar.

"For the gentleman," he says. To the bartender, not to me.

I don't touch it. I watch the bartender bring it to my end of the bar and set it in front of me without making eye contact.

"Terrible business," the man says. He's looking at his own glass. "About the King."

"Yes."

"I didn't know him well. Met him once, at a thing at the Marchand house. Big man. Warm." He drinks. "The condolences of a grateful city."

The formula is fucking wrong.

I know the condolence forms of this city. *The condolences of a grateful city* is what you say to a widow, not to a son. It's one register off — the register of distance rather than proximity.

A man who knew the forms would have said *the condolences of a grieving house.* A man who was coached would have said *our sympathies to the Crown.*

This man said neither.

He finishes his drink. He puts money on the bar, more than the drink costs. That's another form, and it's the right one.

"A good man," he says. "Hard to replace."

He leaves.

I sit with the drink I didn't touch and the wrong word, and I put them in a room in my head with a door on it.

---

I come home to Selene in the study.

She's in the chair — the one across from the desk, the one Cassian usually takes. The ledger is open in front of her. She's been reading.

"You went out," she says.

"I did."

"You smell like a bar."

"Magazine Street."

She looks at me like she looks at everything. Both versions running at once.

"We need to talk about the word," she says.

"Which word?"

"The one you used in the west wing. The one you said to me the first week." She closes the ledger. "*Mine.*"

I sit at the desk. This conversation has a shape I haven't agreed to, and she's already inside it.

"It's a position," I say. "In this house—"

"In this house it means claimed. In your court it means property with a flag on it. And in wolf—" She stops herself. Starts again. "In wolf, *mine* is what an Alpha says about a kill. Not a person. A thing he's going to eat."

"You're not—"

"I hear all three, Lucian. Every time you say it, I hear the version that means *this belongs to me* and the version that means *this is attached to me* and the version that means *this is something I'm consuming.* And none of them say what I need them to say."

The room goes still.

"What do you need them to say?"

"That I'm choosing. That I'm here because I walked in, not because you put me here." She leans forward. "I'm not yours. If I stay it's because I choose to stay, every morning, and the choosing is mine. Not the being."

I look at her across the desk. My father's desk. The desk where a king sits and gives words to things.

She's right. She's right in a way that goes past *mine* and into what I've been building around her without asking whether she wanted walls.

"Then teach me, ghost," I say. "The word for what you are. Because *mine* is the only language I've had for four hundred years, and I'll learn yours tonight if you'll stay long enough to teach me."

Her mouth changes. Arrival, as if a sentence she's been waiting for has finally shown up.

"I'll stay," she says. "Not because you asked. Because you offered to learn."

Then she reaches across the desk and takes my wrist, and taps it with two fingers.

Once. Twice.

I haven't touched her. There's nothing to let go of. She's showing me she can stop this any time she likes, and that she isn't.

She picks up the ledger and goes to the west wing, and the door doesn't close behind her, and I sit in the study with the word *mine* on the desk between us like a coin she's turned over to show me the other side.

I have spoken one language in every room I've ever been in, and a woman who hears two has just told me the first one isn't enough.

Lessons start tonight.

She left the door open.
