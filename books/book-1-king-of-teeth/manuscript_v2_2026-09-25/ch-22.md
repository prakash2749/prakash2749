# 22 · Lucian

I let the wolf go on a Tuesday.

That's a choice I make without explaining it, and the people who want an explanation will have to do without one. Thibault walks out of my house at midmorning with the clothes he came in and a cut on his wrist from the ties and nothing else.

He stops at the gate. Of course the bastard stops.

"Your Majesty."

"Go home, Thibault."

"I intend to." He looks at the house behind me — at the windows, the courtyard, the door that's been open since his arrival. "You know what I told you. About the chamber."

"I know."

"It's going to come apart. Whatever's holding this thing together — the story, the arrangement, the version everybody's been telling — it's going to come apart." He puts his hands in his pockets. "When it does, I'd like to be somewhere useful."

"You've been useful."

"I'd like to stay useful." He says it plainly. "A wolf who can say what he saw, in front of whoever you put him in front of. That's worth something."

"It's worth getting you killed."

"It's worth getting me killed by the right people." He shrugs. "Better than the wrong ones."

I watch him go. He walks down the street with the gait of a man who's been sitting for ten days, and the morning sun is on his back, and he doesn't look around.

He's going back to Barataria. He's going to tell Gaspard whatever he chooses to tell Gaspard, and I'm going to have to live with whatever that is.

It's the right thing to do. My father would have let him go on the first day. My father would have sat him down with a meal and sent him home with a bottle.

I let him sit for ten days in a room with a lamp, and I'm not sorry about it, and I'd do it again.

---

Silas has Cray. Fucking finally.

Not in custody. In sight. He's been sitting on the Bywater address for three days, and the man hasn't run, which Silas reads as confidence and I read as instruction. Somebody told Cray to stay, and staying is what he's doing.

"He has a routine," Silas says. We're in the study. He's standing. "Coffee at eight. A walk at ten. The house from noon until evening. One visitor per day, late afternoon."

"The visitor."

"Different each time. I've logged four." Silas doesn't use paper. He keeps it in his head, organized by a system I've never been able to replicate. "Two are human. One is a wolf I don't know. One is a vampire."

"Which vampire?"

"Young. Established, not Elder. She wore a coat I've seen at the Aldric house."

"Aldric."

"The house. Not the cousin you sent away." A pause. "The cousin's people, though. Same tailor."

Fucking Aldrics. Fuck their whole house.

I let that sit. The Aldrics are a large house, and their domestic arrangements are complicated like very old families are complicated — layers of obligation, of favor, of money that moves between accounts nobody examines.

"The money going into Cray's account," I say. "Have you traced it?"

"I've traced it to a holding. The holding is managed by a firm in the Bywater. The firm has one other client."

"Who?"

"Daniel Ward."

The room changes, somewhere under the air and the light.

"Her father," I say.

"Her father's estate. What's left of it." Silas looks at the window. "The same firm that holds whatever Ward left behind also manages the account that's paying Cray to stay in his house."

"That's a connection."

"It's a pipe. Money goes in one end and comes out the other, and somebody put the plumbing in before Ward died." He turns back to me. "Cray was paid through the same channel that Ward was paid through. Different accounts, same firm, same holding."

"And the holding?"

"Has a signatory I don't recognize. A name that isn't a person — it's a device. Legal fiction."

"Can you open it?"

"I can try. I'll need Ondine."

"The Marchand woman?"

"She holds names. That's what witches do." His jaw works — the same tightening I've seen before. "A name held by a witch can be traced. A name held by a lawyer can be hidden."

"Then go to her."

"I'd rather she came here."

"Why?"

"Because I don't want to be in her house when I ask for this." He says it evenly. "She's done enough. If I'm going to ask for more, I'd like it on ground where she can say no without it costing her."

That stops me. Silas is being decent about it, God help him. Decent in a way that makes the rest of what we're doing look like exactly what it is.

In weeks of investigation, of asking and pushing and turning people over, Silas has never once expressed a preference about where a conversation happens. He goes where the information is. He reads the ground. Bastard's never wrong about it.

This isn't about the ground. This is about a woman in a house on neutral ground, and the particular care a man takes when the person he's asking something of is the person he's most careful about.

"Bring her here," I say. "Make it comfortable."

"Thank you."

He goes. I sit at the desk with the ledger open and the chain laid out in my head like links. Fuck. It's all one chain.

Cassian's mark in the ledger. Ward's payments held in trust. Cray brokered the poison. Cray is funded through the same pipe as Ward. The pipe has a name that isn't a person.

And somewhere at the other end of it, someone asked about Gaspard's wife.

---

Cassian comes in the afternoon.

I haven't seen him in three days. The longest gap since the funeral. He's been at his house — the one I've never visited, the one on a street I've walked past without knowing it was his.

He looks tired. Vampires don't look old like humans do. But there's a settling in him, like a building when the foundation shifts.

"Boy."

"Come in."

He takes his chair. He doesn't pour — that's the first difference. He sits down with his coat still on and his hands on his knees.

"You let the wolf go," he says.

"This morning."

"And?"

"And he'll go home and tell his Alpha what he saw, and I'll deal with whatever that produces."

"What did he see?"

I look at Cassian across the desk. The man has been sitting in this chair for decades. The wood knows the weight of him. Fuck, I know the weight of him.

The question is ordinary. He's been asking me about the investigation most days — what I've found, what I've ruled out, what I need. Every answer I've given him has been true, and every true answer has been a selection from a larger truth, and the selection is the lie.

"He saw my father fight," I say. "He saw wolves die. He saw my father standing when they left."

"And after?"

"After was after. He was through the window."

Cassian nods. His hands stay on his knees.

"You're closer," he says.

"To what?"

"To whatever you're looking for." He says it without asking what it is. "I can see it in the way you sit in that chair. You've stopped leaning back."

He's right. I've stopped leaning back in my father's chair because leaning back is what a man does when he's thinking, and I've stopped thinking and started knowing.

"The poison came through a broker," I say. "A man named Cray, in the Bywater."

"And beyond Cray?"

"I'm working on it."

"Through what channel?"

"Through the financial records." I keep my voice level. "Ward's ledger — the translator. It has a mark in it that matches our internal correspondence seal."

There it is. I've put the evidence in front of him, and only the evidence. The mark in the margin, the connection between the ledger and the house.

Cassian looks at me.

His mouth tightens — a small closing I have to be very careful about reading, because reading it wrong is a door I can't walk back through.

What I see is recognition. He expected it before I said it. He's been waiting for a sentence, and he's just heard the first word of it.

"The house seal," he says.

"Yes."

"Your father's."

"My father used it. So did other people."

A pause. A long one, filled with the sound of a house in the afternoon — a clock somewhere, a footstep above us, the particular quiet of two men in a room who are each carrying a weight they haven't set down.

"So did I," he says.

The words go into the room and sit there.

"I used that mark before your father did," he says. "He took it from me. Or I gave it to him — I've forgotten which, and it was a long time ago, and we were both younger."

He says it openly. Without defense, without explanation, as though it were a fact about the architecture of the house rather than a confession.

"You signed Ward's payments," I say.

"I signed a great many things over the years with that mark." He leans back. "If you're asking whether I knew Daniel Ward, the answer is yes. I commissioned translations from him. Legal work, mostly. The same kind of work any Elder commissions when they'd rather not put their name on a filing."

"That's not what the payments look like."

"What do the payments look like?"

"Large. Regular. Held in trust."

"Then they look like exactly what they are." He meets my eyes. "Private commissions, paid at a premium, held by a careful man who understood that discretion has a price."

It's perfect. Every word of it is plausible, and every word of it fits, and a man who didn't know what I know would take it and go on.

I know what a blade wound looks like. I know it was under the ribs, angled upward, and it didn't close.

"Thank you," I say.

"Is that all?"

"For now."

He stands. He gathers his coat — it was already on, so the gathering is a gesture rather than a function. A man leaving a room in the posture of leaving.

"Lucian."

"Mm."

"When you find what you're looking for," he says, "come to me first. Not the council. Not Marguerite." His hand is on the doorframe, where it always is when he says the last thing. "Come to me, and I'll help you carry it."

He goes.

I sit in the study with his offer in the room, and his honesty about the mark, and his perfect, reasonable explanation.

And I don't believe him.

Not one fucking piece of it. Not the private commissions, not the premiums, not the careful translator who held payments in trust.

I don't believe him because I watched his face when I said the word *mark,* and what I saw wasn't surprise.

It was relief.

He'd been waiting for me to ask. He had the answer ready. And an answer that's ready is an answer that was built, and a built answer is not the same thing as the truth.

The truth doesn't come with furniture.

Fuck his furniture.

I go to the window and look down at the courtyard. His hands on my hands, when I was a boy, showing me where to put the weight.

I take the blade out of my coat and I put it on the desk. Keyed to my father's blood, useless against anyone else. Against Cassian it's an ordinary knife.

He made sure of that. One vial. One blade. One king.

I put the blade back in my coat and I go to find Selene, because she's the only person in this house who can hear the second version of a sentence.

*Ghost.* I've said it so often it's stopped being a name.

It's a location.
