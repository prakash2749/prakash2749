# 35 · Lucian

I take the crown on a Thursday.

That isn't symbolic. Thursday is the day Marguerite had the chamber cleaned, and the day the Roche brothers finished the new door for the dining room, and the day Silas told me the household had been walked and the count was accurate.

It's also the day a man in Barataria takes his boat out. Christ knows why I remember that.

I don't know why that's in my head. It shouldn't be. Rémy Thibodeaux and his shrimp boat have nothing to do with a crown or a chamber or the way a city chooses to notice that a man has stopped being an heir and started being a king.

But it's in my head, and it stays there, because the world goes on being the world while you attend to the parts of it that have your name on them.

---

The coronation is small.

Small, not humble. There's a difference. Humble means you want it noticed that you're not making a fuss. Small means you've decided how much room it takes up.

Marguerite, Silas, three Elders, and the Roche brothers at the door. No public gallery. No continental observers. No Aldric, who was invited and declined, which is a sentence I'm going to fucking remember when the time comes.

The chamber smells like wood polish and new putty. They've re-glazed the gallery windows where the wolves came through, and the new glass is a shade clearer than the old, and nobody has mentioned it.

Marguerite puts the crown on my head.

It's a piece of metal. It's lighter than I expected. Lighter than the blade in my coat, lighter than the glass of wine I had this morning, lighter than anything I've been carrying.

She puts it on and steps back, and she doesn't bow, because Marguerite has never bowed to anything in her life, and I would not ask her to start with me.

"Traditionally," she says, "this is where the King says something."

"Traditionally."

"One sentence will do. The continent doesn't need a speech. They need to know you can open your mouth without starting a war."

I look at the chamber. The table, the chairs, the gallery with its new glass. The floor where my father stood up and the wolves died and a man with a blade walked across the room behind him.

"The door stays open," I say.

Marguerite waits.

"That's it." I look at her. "The door to this chamber stays open. My father kept it open because he believed people should be able to walk in. I'm keeping it open because I believe they should be able to walk out."

Marguerite's mouth thins. She's measuring the sentence against four centuries of coronation speeches and deciding where it falls.

"That's better than your father's," she says.

"What was his?"

"He quoted a poet. Badly." She adjusts a cuff. "Come. There's wine, and three Elders who want to tell you how they voted for your grandfather."

---

Gaspard Thibodeaux comes at dusk.

He stands in the courtyard with two of his men, and he looks like the bayou made him — salt-cured, wide-shouldered, with hands that haven't been clean since before I was born. He's the first wolf to walk into this house since the gallery.

He walks in through the front door. The front, not the service entrance.

Bastard has nerve. I'll give him that.

I meet him in the hall. Silas is at my shoulder. The Roche brothers are at the door, and their presence is noted, and Gaspard notes it, and the noting is the conversation that happens underneath the one we're about to have.

"Your Majesty," he says.

"Gaspard."

"I've come about the boy."

Rémy. His son. The wolf who killed a translator and his wife on their own porch, and spent four years carrying the order.

"He'll testify," I say. "When the time comes."

"He'll testify." Gaspard's jaw works. "And after?"

"After is between him and the woman he wronged."

"The woman doesn't want him dead." He says it without expression. "She had the chance. On my dock. In my territory. She had a blade in her bag and she took her hand off it."

"I know."

"Then what does she want?"

"I don't speak for her."

Gaspard looks at me. A long look — the kind a wolf gives a thing it's deciding about.

"Exile," he says. "That's what I'm offering. He leaves the pack. He leaves Barataria. He goes where I can't see him and he doesn't come back until someone in this city tells him he can."

"That's a father's sentence."

"It's an Alpha's." His face doesn't change. "He took an order from a vampire and killed a human family. That's a violation of every rule we have, and the rules aren't the Code and they don't come with philosophy. They come with the door."

He says it with the weight of an Alpha who has held the pack longer than most wolves have been alive.

"The exile is public?"

"In front of the pack. Tomorrow."

"I'll send Thibault as witness."

"Send the woman." Gaspard looks at the door. "She's earned it."

He turns to leave. Then he stops.

"King of teeth," he says.

I go still.

He's looking at me in a way I've never had from a wolf. It isn't fear, and it isn't deference.

"That's what they called your father," he says. "Not an insult. We meant something else by it. A king with teeth who never used them on us." He looks at the crown on my head. "You used yours. On a man who earned it. The bastard earned it. That's the second meaning."

He walks out through the front door.

---

The next morning, Selene goes to Barataria.

She comes back at noon with the ledger under her arm and her face doing the quiet thing it does when a question has settled in her.

"He didn't look at his father," she says. "He looked at Delphine."

"The sister."

"She walked him to the boundary. The whole pack watched." She puts the ledger on the desk. "She was the only one who walked."

I don't say anything. There's a sentence in what she just told me — about a sister and a boundary and the weight of being the only person who accompanies a man to the edge of everything — and the sentence belongs to a later book.

Tobias is in the study when I come back from the hall.

He's standing at the desk with a document — the coronation certification, in continental form, requiring one witness of administrative rank. He's written it in one pass, in a hand I recognize from the archive tabs. Small, precise, utterly impersonal.

"The certification," he says. He slides it across the desk. "If you'll sign."

I sign. Christ, I sign everything today.

He takes it back. He doesn't sit. He doesn't pour. He folds the document into his coat like he's been folding documents his whole life.

"Will that be all?" he asks.

"That's all."

He goes. He leaves the house before the party, through the side door, and Selene watches him go from the courtyard bench.

"Who is he?" she asks.

"Tobias. Cassian's cousin. Administrative."

"He indexed the archive."

"How do you know?"

"The handwriting on the tabs. Same hand." She watches the door he went through. "He's been organizing the evidence for a month and never once put his name on it."

---

The wine is good. The Elders are old. The evening goes on like evenings do in a house that has decided to act resolved.

Nothing has been resolved. The crown is on my head and the blade is in my coat and his last word is in the fucking room.

*Verity.*

I've been carrying it since the study floor. It sits in me like the blade sits in my pocket. Constant. A weight I can feel and can't put down.

Truth, or a name.

Cassian's last word. Delivered with almost no air, in a voice that was leaving, and I've played it in my head every night since, listening for the emphasis, the inflection, the small difference between a man declaring something and a man identifying someone.

If it's truth — if he was simply saying *I've told you the truth* — then the confession is complete. He acted alone. The plot was his. The grief, the blade, the windows, the wolf sent to kill a translator. All Cassian's. All finished.

If it's a name — if there's a person called Verity somewhere in this city or in the wider world — then the confession is a door, and the door is still open.

I don't know which.

Silas doesn't know either. He's been working on it since the night. Quietly, without orders, like Silas works on everything. He reads the city's registries and the continental records and the correspondence of a hundred years, and he has found nothing, and the nothing is the loudest thing in the house.

"There's no Verity in the rolls," he told me this morning. "Not in the city. Not in the continental registry. Not in any House on record."

"Keep looking."

"I will." He paused. "Sire — it may have been the other thing."

"It may have."

"A man who's dying doesn't always choose his words."

"Cassian always chose his words."

Silas looked at the window. Then he went to keep looking.

---

The crown fits. I expected it to sit wrong, too heavy on one side, too tight, the kind of thing you'd have to get used to wearing. My father wore it like a hat. I saw it on him twice in my life, and both times he looked like a man wearing a hat he'd forgotten to take off.

It fits me. It sits where it sits and it weighs what it weighs and I don't notice it after the first hour.

I notice the chair.

Cassian's chair. The one I turned around, the one he sat in for the last time. I turned it back after. Toward the corner, away from the window. A position that doesn't invite anyone. A chair that says *this seat is taken by nobody.*

Somebody will sit there eventually. The house will produce a person — an adviser, a cousin, a young man with papers — and the chair will go back into rotation, and the groove in the floor will find a new angle.

Not yet.

---

Selene is in the courtyard.

I see her through the study window. She's sitting on the bench near the fountain — the one my father put there because he believed courtyards should have places to sit, even courtyards that nobody sat in.

She's reading the ledger. Still reading it, still going through the pages, still finding things in her father's handwriting that rearrange her.

The crown is on my head and she hasn't seen it yet. The coronation was an hour ago and she wasn't in the chamber.

Marguerite invited her, in the end. Stiffly, and in writing, which from Marguerite is a bouquet.

Selene said no.

*That's their language,* she told me. *Let them speak it. I'll be in the courtyard.*

I go down.

---

She looks up when I cross the stones. The evening light is on her face and her father's ledger is open on her knee and she's wearing the boots she bought on Magazine Street.

She sees the crown.

Her face does a thing I haven't seen before. A woman who has been looking at a man for weeks, and is now seeing what he was always going to become.

"It's smaller than I thought," she says.

"So I'm told, ghost."

"Does it come off?"

"It comes off."

"Good. It's ugly."

I sit down beside her. The bench is stone and cold, and the fountain makes the sound it's been making since my father put it there.

A door opens above us. One of the Elders — Roche, the younger one — steps onto the gallery that overlooks the courtyard. He's leaving the chamber. He looks down.

He sees the King of New Orleans on a stone bench with a woman. Her knee against his. Her father's ledger open between them. The crown on his head and no distance between their bodies.

I look up at him. I don't move. I don't create space.

He holds my eyes for a moment. Then he nods — once, precisely — and goes back through the door.

The claim sits in the air between us. No word. No gesture. The absence of concealment in front of a witness. By morning the whole court will know what that nod means.

She closes the ledger. She holds it on her knee with both hands, carefully, from the edges.

"The pact," she says. "The terms are met. The arrangement—"

"The arrangement ended two weeks ago and we both know it." She looks at me. "I stopped counting when you read my father's ledger and turned the pages from the corner."

"Selene."

"Don't." She holds up a hand. "Don't say something kingly. Don't say *mine* or *stay* or anything with a title in front of it. Say the thing underneath."

I look at her on the bench in the courtyard of a house that is now mine by law and by crown and by the bare fact that I killed the man who sat in the guest chair.

"I don't want to do this without you, ghost," I say.

"Do what?"

"Any of it." I take the crown off. I put it on the bench between us, where it sits like a piece of metal on a piece of stone. That's all it is. "The house. The city. The name that's been in my head since the study floor. The morning, and the one after that."

"That's not a pact."

"No."

She looks at the crown. She picks it up. She turns it in her hands, reading it, looking for the meaning underneath the metal.

Then she puts it back on my head.

"It's still ugly," she says.

"I know."

"But it fits."

"I know."

She leans against me. Her shoulder against my arm, her head tipping toward my shoulder without quite getting there. The ledger is on her knee and the crown is on my head and the evening is coming on.

"I'm not translating this," she says.

"What?"

"Any of it. It's not a pact. It's not an arrangement. It's not a word in wolf or a position in court." She puts her head on my shoulder. "It's just what it is."

"*Verity,*" she says.

"Yes."

"We'll find it."

"Yes."

"Whichever one it is — the truth or the name."

I don't answer. I put my arm around her, on a bench in a courtyard in a house with every door standing open, and the city goes on being the city around us.

It stays.

We'll find it.
