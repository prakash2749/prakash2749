# 2 · Selene

The man at the end of the bar says *condolences* and means *opportunity.*

I know because the word has two shapes. In English it's a hand on a shoulder. In wolf it's the sound a door makes when it unlocks.

He's been here an hour. Good suit, no tie, and a bourbon he ordered by name and hasn't fucking touched. He watches the room like a man working out which chair is about to be empty.

I pour him a refill he didn't ask for because it keeps me close enough to hear.

"Terrible thing," he says to the woman beside him. "A king like that. In his own house."

She nods. She doesn't know what she's nodding at.

"Makes you wonder what's next," he says. "For all of us."

In wolf, *all of us* has a fence around it. It means the pack, the blood, the people who sleep under the same noise. It does not mean the woman on the next stool.

He means his people. A door has come off its hinges somewhere above him, and he's here to see what falls through.

I wipe the bar in front of him and he doesn't see me do it.

Nobody does.

---

At a quarter to nine the street door opens and one corner of the room forgets how to breathe.

The humans keep talking. The band keeps tuning. Antoine keeps losing an argument with the ice machine.

But the man at the end of the bar sets his glass down so carefully it doesn't make a sound.

I look. Holy shit.

He's tall and he's dressed for a funeral, because there's been one. Black coat, dark hair pushed back like he did it with one hand in a moving car. Pale, in a city where everybody's a little sunburned. He stops inside the door and reads the room. Windows first. The kitchen door propped open with a mop bucket. The alley exit behind the stage.

He doesn't look at the people. He looks at how they got in.

And my body does something it has never fucking done in my life.

Wolves are a sound I can almost hear. This is the opposite. The air goes out of one corner of the room and my skin notices before I do. The hair on my forearms lifts. What the hell. My mouth goes dry. Low in my belly, everything I am goes very still and very interested.

My mother used to say there were things in this city that were cold all the way through. You'd know one, she said, because your body would try to make itself small.

My body isn't trying to make itself small.

So what the hell is it doing?

He crosses to the end of the bar without hurrying and stops behind the man in the good suit. Close enough to be felt. He doesn't touch him.

"Sire," the man says, to his bourbon.

English says that's a title. The grammar underneath says it's a man checking whether he's still allowed to be alive.

"You were in the house on Tuesday." The voice is low and doesn't carry, and every word of it lands at my end of the bar anyway. I've spent my whole life listening to rooms. This one voice is easy.

"I was with the Aldrics, Sire. All night. I left before—"

"Say it again."

The man's throat works. "I was with the Aldrics. All night."

"And tonight you're on Frenchmen Street explaining to a tourist what's next." He leans down, barely. "For all of us."

The man goes a color I've only ever seen on things pulled out of the river.

"Go home," the tall one says. "Stay there. If I hear my father's name in your mouth again, I'll take the mouth."

The man leaves cash on the bar. Too much. He leaves the bourbon. He leaves the woman on the next stool, who watches him go and turns back to her phone like the stool emptied itself.

And the dead King's son sits down in the chair he left.

What does a vampire want with a bar on Frenchmen Street?

---

*Sire.* There's exactly one person anybody in this city calls that this week, and he's in my bar, in front of a drink that isn't his.

I do the only thing I know how to do. I take the old glass and pour him a new one.

I set it down. He reaches for it at the same moment, and his fingers close over mine on the glass.

Cold.

Inside-of-the-ice-machine cold. Brass-rail-in-January cold. It goes up my wrist like a wire.

He doesn't move his hand. His fingers stay over mine and neither of us lets go of the glass, and my own pulse is knocking against his fingertips like it wants his attention.

"Thank you," he says.

He doesn't look at me when he says it. He's looking at the street door.

Then he takes the glass and my hand is just a hand again, sitting on the bar with nothing in it.

In English, *thank you* is manners. Underneath, it's the sound a man makes when he's already somewhere else.

He doesn't drink. He gives the glass one slow quarter-turn on the wood and watches the windows, the kitchen, the alley exit, like he's reading a lease for the clause that screws him.

I watch him.

I shouldn't. I've got a two-top waving at me and a ticket printing and Antoine yelling about limes. I watch him anyway. The rain on the shoulders of his coat, beading, not soaking in. The line of his jaw. His mouth, which is some bullshit, frankly.

My thighs are pressed together under the bar and I have no idea when I did that.

Jesus fucking Christ, Selene.

He's a vampire. He's *the* vampire, or will be the minute somebody puts a crown on him. His people own half the buildings on this street. His father just died on a floor, the wolves are getting the blame, and I'm standing here with my pulse in my underwear over a man who looked through me like a window.

He tucks a fifty under the glass and stands.

He never once looks at me.

I'm used to that. I'm not used to minding.

The street door closes behind him. The air comes back into the corner of the room. The woman on the next stool finally puts her phone down.

"Who was *that*?"

"Nobody," I say. "Tourist."

"Honey." She looks at the door, then at me. "That was not a tourist."

"He tipped like one."

It's the first lie I've told in this bar that I wanted to keep.

---

The bar fills up after nine, same as every night.

Frenchmen after dark is a machine that runs on music and liquor and the specific willingness of tourists to believe that whatever happened to them last night was a good time. I've worked it long enough to hear the gears.

There's a grammar to a room like this. Two of them, actually.

The first one is English, what they're all speaking, and in English this is a bar where people come to drink and listen to a band and leave their credit cards open.

The second one is underneath. Nobody in here would call it a language. It's a current. Who is watching whom, and from where, and the small shifts in a room when something with teeth walks in and every human body adjusts without being told.

I hear both. I've always heard both.

My mother gave me that. Or whatever she gave me that I don't have a name for, whatever makes wolves stare at me a beat too long before they decide to be somewhere else.

It isn't a gift. A gift is something you can put down.

---

Around ten there's a woman at the door. Wolf.

I catch it like I always catch it. A sound at a frequency I can almost place. The back of my head sits up and pays attention before I've decided to.

She's young. My age, maybe younger. Good coat, and she holds the door for the man behind her. That's a wolf thing. The one nearest the outside holds the barrier. In English that's courtesy. In pack it's perimeter.

She gets a few steps into the room before she catches whatever I am on the air.

Her nose lifts. A quarter-inch, like somebody catching a bad note in a song.

Then she looks at me.

I've been looked at like that by wolves my whole life. Hatred would be a goddamn improvement. It's the face a person makes when they bite into something that tastes like two things at once and can't work out which one is off.

And here's the part I don't tell anyone.

My body reaches for her. Goddamn it. Every time. Under my skin, the part of me that has reached for wolves since I was old enough to stand in a room with one leans toward her like a plant leans at a window. Nobody asked it to. Leaning is just what it does.

She flinches. The reaching falls short. The gap between us fills up with the ordinary air of a bar on Frenchmen Street.

My whole life of that. My body saying *there, that one, go* and the other body saying *wrong, spoiled, away.* I've been refused in a language I was born speaking, and the refusal never gets quieter. It just gets familiar, like a song you fucking hate.

She turns to the man behind her. "Something died back there," she says, not quietly.

"Yeah," I say. "My will to live. What can I get you?"

She doesn't answer. He shrugs. They find a table near the window, away from the bar.

Away from me.

She's put a room between us. Pack calls that quarantine.

And an hour ago the coldest thing in this city sat at my bar, and my body didn't flinch at all.

It leaned in.

---

After midnight the crowd turns over and I take my break in the alley behind the kitchen.

It smells like grease and rain and the dumpster from the po'boy place, which has opinions. There's a crate out here I've sat on long enough that it has my exact shape in it by now.

Antoine comes out with a cigarette he's not supposed to have and sits on the other crate.

"Your tourist tipped fifty on a drink he didn't drink," he says.

"Maybe he's in recovery."

"Maybe he's a narc." He blows smoke at the rain. "You were looking at him."

"I look at everybody. It's the job."

"You were looking at him like he was the last shrimp on the plate."

"Antoine."

"I'm just saying. Girl, you're allowed."

"I'm allowed to finish my break without a fucking TED talk."

He laughs, and stubs the cigarette out on the wall, and goes back in, and for a minute the alley is mine again.

I take my phone out. No messages, which is every night. I have three numbers that aren't the bar. My landlord. My dentist. A woman in Tremé who sells me the tea my mother used to make, and who has never once asked me why I buy it.

That's the whole list. I'm not feeling sorry for myself. I'm being accurate. It's a shit list, but it's accurate.

My father was a translator. He did contracts. Shipping, some legal work, court business he never brought home. He was good at it. He could hear what a sentence meant underneath what it said, and he taught me to hear it too, sitting at the kitchen table with two versions of the same document and a pencil he never sharpened.

"What did they say?" he'd ask, and tap a line with the pencil.

"That they'll deliver by the fifteenth."

"Now. What did they *mean*?"

"That they won't."

"Why?"

"Because nobody promises a date that hard unless they're scared you'll ask about the month."

He'd laugh like I'd pulled a coin out of his ear. "Two languages, Leenie. Never trust the one they're speaking."

A pencil, a habit, and a bar where I practice it on strangers. The whole inheritance.

And a ledger. His work ledger. Every job, every client, every fee, written in a hand so neat it looks printed. I've had it since the funeral. I've never opened it past the first page, because the first page has his handwriting on it, and his handwriting is the last thing of his I'm not ready for.

What would I even find? A name? A reason?

Shit. Probably nothing.

---

My mother was a wolf.

She was born Marisol Boucher, and she left the Thibodeaux pack to be with my father, which in pack custom is not a departure. It's a contamination.

English says *she left.* Pack has a word for what she did, and it means the limb the body cuts off. Rejected, not removed. The blood stops going there and the flesh makes its own decision.

She stopped being pack the day she chose a human man, and nobody told her, because nobody had to. The blood just stopped.

I got her face. I got whatever makes my nose work sometimes and never when it fucking counts. I got whatever wolves smell when they pass me, which isn't wolf and isn't human. Something unfinished.

I didn't get the shift.

That's the sentence and I'm not going to dress it up. Wolves shift. I don't. Never have. My body reaches for it like a tongue for a missing tooth, and nothing is there, and nothing will ever be there.

My mother could run four-legged through Barataria in the dark. Her daughter can pour a clean Sazerac and hear both sides of a conversation.

That's what came down.

---

They were killed four years ago.

Rémy Thibodeaux came to the house on a Thursday. He was the Alpha's son, twenty-seven, big, and he did it with his hands, and my mother was between him and the door, so he did her first.

I know this because everyone in Barataria knows it. It's the kind of story that moves through a pack like weather. Everybody gets wet and nobody says where the cloud was.

What nobody says, because nobody thinks it's a question, is *why.*

A pack son doesn't kill a woman his own father's age for leaving. That's not how the hell it works. Pack has a long memory and a short arm. They stop calling, they stop looking, and the blood makes its own decision. Killing would be an acknowledgment, and an acknowledgment would open the whole thing back up.

So why.

The official version, the one I've heard in pack grammar from three different wolves in three different bars, is that my father was into something. That he had his hands in business that got people hurt. Bullshit. That Rémy did what was necessary.

In wolf, *necessary* is the same word as *clean.* Both mean: there is no further obligation.

That word has kept me awake ever since. Not because it's wrong.

Because nobody ever says what the business was.

---

Here's what I've built.

A job. A routine. A body that can handle a double shift and a walk home at three in the morning through a city that is not as safe as it has decided to be.

A knife in my bag that used to be my father's. I keep it sharp like other people keep a car washed.

And an intention so clean I can see my own face in it.

I'm going to kill Rémy Thibodeaux. Not in a fantasy. Not as a comfort I take out at night and put back in the fucking morning.

As a plan. With steps. Steps I've been taking for four years, each one small enough to look like a life.

Learn the pack's grammar. Learn their schedules, their customs, the days they come north and the days they stay on the water. Learn which bars they drink at, and who talks too freely, and what a wolf says when a wolf has decided you don't matter enough to be careful around.

I've done all of that. Every bit of it. I'm fluent in a language that does not want me.

What I don't have is a way in.

Barataria is pack ground. A wolf who smells wrong doesn't cross the water without being noticed, and a woman who can't shift is a woman who can't leave the way she came if it goes bad.

I need somebody the wolves are afraid of. I've been waiting a long time for one to walk in.

---

I go back behind the bar and work the rest of the night, and the city does what it does. Tourists leave in twos and threes. The band plays its last set to a room that's stopped listening. A man pays his tab in cash and asks whether I'm from here.

"Born here," I say.

"You don't sound like it."

"I get that a lot."

I sound like both places and neither one. That's the whole of me in a sentence, and he doesn't notice, because he's already signing.

Antoine is closing out the register. He tells me the King is dead, as though I haven't heard it from every person in this room tonight.

"Terrible thing," he says.

"Yeah."

"You know what's gonna happen, right? With the wolves?"

"Something."

"Something *bad.*" He shuts the drawer. "Their boy's been down there all week. The son. People are talking."

I keep my hands on the rag and my face on the glass I'm wiping.

"Which son?"

"Thibodeaux's kid. Rémy." Antoine shrugs. "Somebody saw him at the fuel dock yesterday. Fueling the boat, not going anywhere. Just, there."

"Huh," I say. "Weird."

My voice comes out completely normal. I'd like a goddamn medal.

To a wolf, staying visible is the opposite of hiding. It's a declaration. You put yourself where people can see you because you want it understood that you are not ashamed of the ground you're standing on.

He's in the city this week. He's not running.

He's right across the water.
