# 30 · Lucian

"So the wine came through a supplier on Decatur."

Bastien talks too much, and I have stopped hearing most of it. He's been on the poison money for a week. It's the first real work anybody's given him, and he's taken it as a dog takes a bone.

He has papers. He's brought papers into a hallway. "But the *money* doesn't. The money goes out through a shipping account, then it goes sideways twice, and then there's a broker."

"A name."

"Three names. I checked them all twice, sire, and once more at four in the morning when I couldn't sleep, because I kept thinking I'd got a letter wrong and you'd have my head. One's a company, and one's a dead woman." He turns a page. "And one's Cray, the Marchand woman's broker. He's the only one who's ever picked up a phone."

"Give it to Silas."

"I already did." A pause. "Sorry. I should have said that first."

"Yes."

He grins anyway, as he always grins when I correct him. I've never understood that about him and I've decided not to examine it.

"There's more," he says. "You're going to love this part. Or hate it. Honestly, sire, I can never tell with you. The dead woman's account paid for something else last spring, and I can't read the ledger line, because it's in somebody's private shorthand."

"Whose?"

"I'm working on that bit." He's pleased with himself, and he's earned it, and I'm not going to tell him so. "Sire — can I ask you a thing?"

"You're going to. When have you ever not?"

"When it starts, the war." He gets the papers squared against his chest. "Will you put me where it's happening? Not the gate, not standing outside a door in the Quarter counting wolves."

"You want to be seen."

"I want to be *useful*."

He means both, and he's young enough to think those are the same sentence.

I look at him for a moment. He's ninety years old next spring and he came to this house because my father took him off a dock in a bad decade. He has spent every year since arranging his whole personality around being let into rooms.

Should I tell him he's not ready? It would be true. I could tell him he did good work on the money, also true. It would cost me nothing, and he would carry it around for a month like a coin.

"Later," I say.

"Later when, sire? Next week? Next war?"

"When I say so. Go on, get out of my hallway."

That's what I gave him: *later.*

---

They come in through the kitchen, not the gallery, not the courtyard, not the street doors, not any of the ways my father's house was built to be entered.

The kitchen has a door for deliveries, held by nobody, because the men who use it are human and they come before dawn with fish.

I'm in the west hall when the first one dies. I hear it — not the shout, the shout comes later, but the wet crack of a young vampire being opened by an animal that knows where to open him.

Then the house starts screaming.

Six of them, shifted already, inside the walls. Somebody walked them past two courtyards and a guard post.

Who the fuck opened the kitchen? I'm moving before I've finished the thought, and the thought runs under my feet like a second set of footsteps — *who has a key, who has a key* — and it's the question I've been asking for a month.

It's the wrong question, and it has always been the wrong question. I ask it the whole way down the hall.

Bastien is in the small dining room, and he shouldn't be. He should be behind me.

He's put himself in a doorway with his arms out, because there are two of the household staff behind him — a girl who does the linen, and a man whose name I have never learned, both human.

He's eighty-nine years old, and he has never been in a fight that mattered, and he holds the doorway.

He holds it about as long as it takes me to cross the hall, and when I get there he's on his back with his throat open to the spine.

His hands are still up.

---

Here. This is where it goes.

The wolf over him turns and I take the front of its face off with my hand. I don't strike. I get my fingers into the skull under the jaw and pull.

It comes away wet, and I'm already past it.

The second one comes at my left, and I take the arm off at the shoulder.

Then I use it. I put the loose end through the animal's own chest and out the other side. It's still trying to bite me while I do it.

Noise in the room, somebody screaming in a human register.

The third goes into a wall, not thrown but pushed, with my weight behind it, until the wall stops being a wall. I don't remember choosing the wall.

Fourth. The fourth one is on the floor.

It's on the floor and it has gone back: human, a man, naked, young, with his hands flat on the boards and his head down and blood coming out of his nose in a thin string.

Shifting back is surrender. Everybody in this city knows it — you go human, you put your hands down, and the other side stops.

My father stopped, every time. He'd have the man taken and tried, and I sat in the room while he did it and told him it was a weakness.

"Wait," the man says. "Wait — I'm— please, I'm done, can't you see I'm done?"

*Who has a key.*

I stop asking. Not the killing — the killing is ordinary. What happens is that the question goes out of me as a light goes out. I stop asking who the fuck let them in.

What's underneath it has no question in it at all.

I take his lower jaw off first, because he's talking.

Then the rest of him. It takes a while, and he's conscious for a great deal of it, and I do not hurry.

The fifth is trying to get out through the kitchen, back the way they came, and I catch him in the passage.

I put him through the doorframe, and then I keep putting him through it after there's no reason to.

Sixth is the girl.

She's young, younger than Bastien, and her back is against the stove and she hasn't shifted at all. She never shifted. She came in a coat and a face, and she opened a door for her cousins.

She's crying. "Please," she says. "Please, please—"

I've heard that word tonight already. Somebody said it to me in a hallway a month ago and meant something else by it entirely.

I put my hand around the whole of her head.

---

And then there's nobody left.

That's the part nobody warns you about: there's no ending to it. You don't finish. You run out of material.

I'm standing in my father's small dining room with the raiding party in pieces around me, and I'm looking at the pantry door.

It's shut, and somebody is behind it. There's a heartbeat going in there like a bird in a box, and it's human. I know before I've taken a step that it's the linen girl, the one Bastien died standing in front of.

I'm walking toward the pantry door, and nothing stops me — not conscience, not the Code, not my father's voice, which I have heard in every other room of this house.

I get my hand on the door.

And Silas says, "Sire."

One word, from the hall, with no weight on it at all, and I turn around.

He's in the doorway with his hands loose at his sides and nothing in them, and he's looking at me the way you look at a dog that's just bitten someone, deciding whether it's still yours.

He's been there a while. He watched the last of it and he didn't come in.

"That's a person in there," he says.

And it comes back all at once, like water finding the bottom of a well.

I'm myself again, standing in a room with my hand on a pantry door and a house full of bodies, and I take my hand off it.

---

Afterward, I'm very calm, and the calm is worse. It arrives complete, a guest who was waiting in the next room for its turn.

I go and stand over Bastien. His hands are down now — somebody put them down, Silas probably. There's a decency in that man he has never once admitted to.

The girl comes out of the pantry. She looks at me and her face does the ordinary human thing, the instinct firing underneath thought: *find something else to look at.* She can't, because I'm all there is in the room.

"You were behind him the whole time?" I say.

She nods.

"He didn't move out of the doorway?"

She nods again, shaking hard enough that she has to hold the frame.

"Then you'll tell people that." My voice is level, a courtesy voice, the one I use at council. "Not the rest of it, just that part."

She says something I don't take in.

Then I give the orders — the bodies, the kitchen entrance, the human staff paid for the month and sent out of the city until this is finished.

I do all of it in the tone of a man rescheduling a delivery, and Silas writes it down.

"The name Bastien traced," I say. "Where is he?"

"Cray, still in the Bywater. He hasn't moved."

"Keep him there. Not tonight." I look at the kitchen passage. "Tonight I want the house walked, every door, every way in."

"Yes."

"I want the list short. I want it honest if I'm on it. I mean that, Silas."

"Yes, sire. Including you?"

"I said honest."

"Then I'll put you at the top."

He goes, and I stand in the middle of it and take an account of myself, because there's nobody left in my life who'll do it for me.

I am not sorry about the fourth one. He put his hands down and went back to being a man and said *wait*, and I opened him anyway.

I've looked for the part of me that objects and it isn't there. I'm not sorry about the girl at the stove either.

What I keep arriving at is the pantry door.

I had my hand on it. There was a woman behind it who has never done anything to anyone — she folds linen in a house she has no idea about. She was standing behind Bastien because he put her there.

And I was going to open the door, not in a rage. I'd like to be able to say it was rage.

It was quieter than that: I was going to open the door because it was the next door.

What do you call a man who does that? There's a word and I know it, and it is not one of the ones they use about my father.

---

Marguerite will hear about this before morning.

She'll want it called a defense of the house, which it was, for about the length of the hall, and then she'll want it called a message.

The court will want it called the night the King finally stopped apologizing.

They'll be pleased, and I can see it coming and can't head off the sentence. Some of them have been waiting four centuries for a Croix to do precisely this. They'll put it in a sentence with my father's name in it. The sentence will be about how the son is not the fucking father.

And they'll be right about that, and that's what I can't get around.

I told him mercy was a thing you could afford until the night you couldn't.

This was the night, and I was correct. Being correct shouldn't feel like this.

---

Here's what I won't be saying to Silas, or to Marguerite, or to my father, if he walked in right now and asked what I'd done to his dining room.

I keep going back to the moment Silas said my title.

What I feel about Silas standing in that doorway is that he interrupted me.

Do you know what I wanted? I wanted to keep fucking going.
