# I-1 · Cassian

*Placement: immediately after Ch. 3.*

I could tell him tonight.

He is sitting where his father sat, in the chair his father never once turned toward the window, and he has finally asked the right question: who was in the room. He asked it plainly, as Aurelian asked things, and then he did what Aurelian never learned to do.

He waited.

He is very good at waiting, and I taught him that, in a manner of speaking, by being the only man in his life who never filled a silence for him.

The lamp is lit and the decanter is down a finger and the house has gone quiet around us, as it does after the household eats. A boy and his uncle in a warm room, and there is nothing in this evening that is not exactly what it appears to be, except for me.

I taught those hands to hold a blade. Aurelian was busy being a king, so it was me in the courtyard every evening, through all the years that takes, and nobody has ever thanked me for it and I have never asked.

He has his father's hands and none of his father's certainty. That is the whole difference between them, and it is the reason this city is going to survive what is coming.

"Who was in the room?" he says again.

He makes men repeat themselves. He has done it since he was small, because a man who hears himself say a thing has to live with having said it.

Why don't I hand him the whole of it now?

Across this desk, in this light, before he has spent a single life on the wrong answer.

I would say his father's name, and that the east door has been open to me since before this city had a name, and that a door standing open is not an accident when the same man walks through it for centuries. I would put the rest of it in his hands and let him do what the law requires, and the law requires a great deal, and he would do it. He is his father's son in precisely that one way.

It would take the length of a short conversation, and everything after would be different, and none of it would be mine.

He is waiting for me to be the man he thinks I am.

"How is the Marchand woman?" I ask him.

He blinks, because it is not the question he was braced for.

"Ondine?"

"Her grandmother's chest was bad in the wet years, and I wondered whether it had come down to her."

"She's — well, I think." He is thrown, and being thrown makes him courteous, because courtesy is what he has instead of small talk. "Silas saw her last week. He didn't say otherwise."

"Ask him, when you think of it, not as the King but as yourself." I turn my glass on the wood. "She has held that house neutral through the bad decades and never asked either of us for anything. That is rarer than you know."

"I'll ask."

He will, and he will remember it before the week is out and send someone, and she will be surprised and a little moved, and it will cost him nothing and buy him something real.

I have given him good advice, and I want that to be true because it is true.

"Your father used to say the household was the last place he would look," I tell him, "and the first place he should."

He comes up off the back of the chair.

"He said that?"

"After the business with the Aldric cousin, he said it as a man says a thing he intends to act on and never does." I set the glass down. "He trusted this house, boy, and he built it to be trusted. That was not a weakness in him and I will not have it called one, but a house that cannot be searched is a house that cannot be cleared, and you are going to need it cleared."

"You think it was one of ours?"

"I think you are going to have to be able to say that it was not."

Every word of that is true. He will take it up in the morning and turn it into orders, and the orders will be thorough, and they will find nothing, because there is nothing to find at the end of that particular corridor.

He sits back, and his shoulders let go, a little, because I have given him a direction and the direction is a kindness.

"Thank you," he says.

"How is your mother's house in Toulouse?"

"Standing, but I haven't been."

"Go, when this is finished."

He nods, and he means it. He will not go.

I stand and gather my coat, and he stands because he was raised properly, and we say the things men say at the end of a warm evening in a room where nobody has lied.

He trusts me, so he tells me when he will be at the council and what he intends to say there.

And the door closes without either of us touching it.
