# 16 · Lucian

"Wolf bites," I tell the empty study. "Heavy damage, the worst at the hip."

I have decided to lie to the only person I trust. I am sitting in my father's chair rehearsing it.

The words are simple, and any of those sentences could be true. Most of them were true, once, before Ambroise turned the body over and showed me what was underneath.

What I'm rehearsing is the rest of me, the hands and the eyes, the part of a body that people read when they've stopped listening to the mouth.

Cassian has been reading me since before I could read myself. He taught me to sit still, and he taught me that silence was a room you could put someone in and see what they brought to it.

He's going to walk in here and sit down and ask what Ambroise found, because he's asked every few days since the funeral, because he loved my father more than anyone alive except me.

And I'm going to lie to him, and tell him the wolves did it. It's going to come out of my mouth as plain as a fact, and if I do it right he'll carry it home with him.

---

He comes at the usual hour.

There's no knock, because there has never been a knock. The door is open, as it's been since Tuesday. He comes through it like he comes through every door in this house, as if it was left open for him.

It always had been.

"Boy."

"Come in."

He takes the chair across the desk, the one by the window, my father's guest chair, the one that faces the desk instead of the courtyard. He sat in this chair every evening for longer than I've been alive, and his body knows it like a bed.

Selene is on the window seat, where she's been since I asked her. The ledger is closed on her knee, her hand flat on the cover like it might open on its own. Her body is angled away from the desk as though she's part of the furniture.

She's not part of the furniture, and she's the only person in this room who can hear both layers of what's about to happen.

Cassian glances at her, and the glance is warm. I can read it now: he's deciding what she changes about the room.

He settles without rushing. He has a glass he poured himself on the way in, from the decanter on the shelf. The fact that he's comfortable enough to do that is the whole history of his place in this house.

"Ambroise?" he says.

"I went down yesterday."

"And?"

"Wolf bites." I keep my hands on the desk. "Four of them, heavy damage, the worst at the hip, where one took a piece out."

He nods, and his face settles, as it always does when we talk about the death, water finding the bottom of a well, and it isn't performed.

"And the chest?"

"Ambroise thinks one of the bites went deep enough to catch an artery underneath. The healing would have prioritized the artery over the surface wound, which is why it looked—"

"Like he was still standing."

"Like he was still standing, and he was, while the internal damage was catching up."

Cassian turns his glass on his knee, a slow half-turn, as he turns everything, a man who has outlived the need to hurry.

"So he was fighting," he says. "And winning?"

"And winning, and bleeding underneath it."

"And nobody saw the moment?"

"Nobody in the room was watching him at the end, because they were watching the wolves."

"Yes." He takes a sip. "Nobody looks at anything else, in a room with wolves in it."

He's said that before, the same sentence in the same cadence, and I didn't notice the first time.

I notice now, and the noticing goes into a room in my head where I'm keeping the things I notice about Cassian, and the room is getting furnished.

"How is Ambroise?" he asks.

"Tired. I took him upstairs and made him eat. He cursed me in three languages."

"Only three? He's slowing down." Cassian smiles, and it reaches his eyes, because with him it always does. "Did he tell you about the wife?"

"He mentioned her. Why?"

"Lovely woman, and she died young, young for a human, I mean, which is any reasonable schedule." He shakes his head. "He keeps her portrait in the cold room, between the instruments. I've always found that either very sweet or very troubling."

"Both. It's always both with Ambroise."

"Both, yes." He drinks. "You're being careful with this, aren't you?"

"With what? The weather?"

"The information, Ambroise's findings." His eyes come up to mine. "You haven't brought it to the council?"

"Not yet."

"Why not?"

This is the moment, when the lie has to live in my answer. The answer has to sound like a man being cautious rather than a man being dishonest.

"Because the council will turn wolf bites into a war inside an afternoon," I say, "and I'm not ready for a war."

He holds it like a glass he's going to taste before he commits.

"Your father would have told them on the first night," he says.

"My father would have told them everything, and the council would have drafted a declaration before the wine was finished."

"That's true." A pause. "That's the cost of honesty in a room full of people with agendas."

"I'd rather have the facts before I give them the feeling."

His chin lifts, a movement smaller than approval.

"You're growing into this," he says.

"Don't. For Christ's sake, don't."

"I'm not flattering you, I'm observing." He sets the glass down on the desk. "Your father was a great king and a poor politician. You are going to be the opposite, and the city will be better for it."

I don't answer that, because the answer would be something about the desk I'm sitting at and the man who sat at it before me. The words for that are in a language I speak badly.

"How is your mother's house in Toulouse?" he asks.

"Standing."

"Go, when this is finished."

"You always say that. Do you know that?"

"Because you never go." He stands and gathers his coat off the back of the chair, where he draped it when he came in, and he does up the buttons with the same patient attention he gives to everything. "Lucian."

"Mm."

"You're going to need people you can tell the truth to." His hand is on the doorframe. "Choose them carefully, and choose them soon."

He goes.

---

I spread my hand on the desk where my father's papers were and push down.

The wood creaks, and my fingers are white against the grain.

"He didn't ask," I say. "He didn't even ask."

Selene is still on the window seat, the ledger closed on her knee. She's been watching the door Cassian went through with both registers running. Her face has the expression I'm learning to recognize, the one that means she heard a second sentence under the one everybody else heard.

"I know," she says. "That's worse."

"He should have asked about the blade wound, or whether the bites were survivable. He should have—"

"He should have questioned it." She looks at me. "A man who doesn't question a lie is either trusting or has already seen the body. I know which one you're thinking. I know which one is true."

"Aren't they the same?"

"They're not." She stands. "You're thinking he trusts you, that he took the lie because he loves you and has no reason to doubt you. That's the version on top."

"And underneath?"

"Underneath, a man who already knows what Ambroise found doesn't need to ask about it. He sits in a chair and turns his glass and nods because the performance requires a nod, and the nod is as honest as anything else he's ever done in this room."

The desk creaks again, and I realize I'm still pushing.

I lift my palm off the wood, and there's a mark in the finish, a white crescent from the pressure of my palm.

"He told me to choose people I can tell the truth to," I say. "He said it while standing in the door of a room where I'd just lied to his face."

Fuck.

Selene looks at the mark on the desk, and then at my hand.

"Your hands do that," she says. "When the rest of you is still."

"Do what?"

"Tell the truth."

---

I sit with it.

The lie is in the room now, and the room is the same as it was before. Cassian is walking down my father's hallway with his coat buttoned. He's going home to a house I've never been inside. The glass he left on my desk is still warm from his hand.

He didn't question it.

I keep arriving at it: he asked what Ambroise found. I told him a version, and he turned it over, and he set it down.

He didn't ask about the blade wound, or the entry point, or whether Ambroise had found anything that didn't fit the story of a king savaged by wolves.

Trusting, or already knowing. I can't decide.

I pick up his glass and I put it on the shelf with the decanter. I wipe the ring it left on the desk. I go to the window and look out at the courtyard where he taught me to hold a blade.

Every evening, for years, while my father was busy being a king, Cassian was in the courtyard with a boy and a piece of steel. The boy is sitting at the king's desk now, and the steel is in his coat.

Choose them carefully, he said, from the doorway.

The bastard.

I stand at the window until the courtyard goes dark.

Then I go down to the archive and I pull every letter Cassian has written to this house in the past year. I look at the mark at the bottom of each one.

My father's hook, the little fishhook figure from the household notes. Cassian signs with it too, and he always has. Which of them drew it first? I've seen it a thousand times and never once asked.

I look at it for a long time.

Then I put the letters back, and I go and find the woman in the west wing.

"Ghost."

She looks up from the bed, where she's been waiting.

"Open it," I tell her. "The ledger."

Her hand goes flat on the cover. "Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow. Swear it?"

"I swear it. Damn you, I swear it."

She holds out her other wrist, palm up, across the sheet, without a word.

"You haven't fed since the desk," she says. "Don't tell me you have. I can hear it in your voice."

"Ghost—"

"Take it. And then go and sit in your father's chair and be sick about whatever you found downstairs. In that order."

I kneel on the floor by the bed and put my mouth on her wrist, over the pink line the tape left, and bite.

She sighs, a long, low, shaking sound, and her head goes back against the pillow and her thighs part under the sheet without her permission. I drink slowly. Her pulse beats against my tongue and her other palm comes down on the back of my head and holds me there. I can smell her getting wet with every swallow.

I stop before she wants me to, and I seal the bite with my tongue, and she makes a small furious noise.

"That's it? That's all you're taking?"

"That's it."

"You're a bastard, Your Majesty."

"I'm a bastard with work to do."

"You're a bastard, full stop. Go on. Go be sick about it."

That mark is in my head now.

It sits next to a face I've loved my whole life.

She doesn't ask whose.

I don't tell her.
