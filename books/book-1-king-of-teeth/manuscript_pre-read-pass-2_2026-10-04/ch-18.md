# 18 · Lucian

Thibault has stopped looking at the lamp and started looking at the door.

That means he's decided, because a man who watches a light source is a man waiting, and a man who watches an exit is a man who's made a plan for what comes next, and the plan includes leaving.

I've kept him ten days, fed, watered, allowed to stand and walk the room. Silas brings his meals, and nobody else comes down here.

He's more useful than I expected, a man who saw the last minutes of my father's life from the gallery rail, who has no stake in any version of the answer, and who tells it the same way every time I ask.

I've asked three times and the story hasn't moved. That's either a man with a good memory or a man with a better lie, and I'm betting on the first.

Today I'm asking a different question.

---

"Tell me about Gaspard," I say.

He looks up from the chair, and his face does a small recalculation, because he's been waiting for the next round of questions about the chamber, and this isn't that.

"The Alpha? Hell of a question to start with."

"Your Alpha. Tell me about him."

"What do you want to know? He's an old bastard, like any other."

"Everything you'd tell a man who's never met him. Start with whether he sleeps."

"He doesn't. Not since his wife got sick."

Thibault rubs his jaw, a habit he has when he's sorting through what he knows, trying to decide which piece goes on top.

"He's old," he says. "Not old the way you're old. Old the way a man is old who's done one thing for forty years and the thing has worn a groove in him."

"What's the thing?"

"Keeping us alive," he says simply. "That's the whole job. The pack is — you have to understand, from outside it looks like an army, but it's a town. People with kids and boats and mortgages, and Gaspard's job since his father died is making sure the town stays standing."

"Against us? Only us?"

"Against everything: the water, the city, the humans, you." He counts them on his fingers. "In that order. You're not the biggest thing trying to kill us, and you're not even second."

I sit with that, because it isn't what the court would say. The court would say the wolves are a military force at our border, organized around aggression, bred for it.

Thibault is telling me they're a fishing village with teeth.

"Why did he send you into the chamber?"

"Because somebody told him there was an opening."

"Who? Who told him?"

"I don't know, it was above my head." He shifts in the chair. "The order came down through the captains. We were told the gallery would be open, the windows unlatched, and that the council would be seated, and we were told to go fast and go hard and get out."

"And the target? My father?"

"Chaos, not a kill." He looks at me steadily. "I know what you think: that we came for your father. We didn't. We came for the room."

"And my father was in the room?"

"Your father was the room." His voice shifts down, into a register that sounds like remembering. "You have to understand what he was to us."

"A predator? A monster?"

"A king." Thibault leans forward. "A king with teeth who never once used them on us."

The sentence goes into the room and the room holds it.

*King of teeth.*

The old wolf on the levee said it as an insult: a man who is nothing but the bite, appetite crowned and called a king.

Thibault is saying the opposite with the same words.

A king who had teeth — the worst teeth, the oldest, the ones that could have taken the whole pack and the water it sits on — and who never once put them in a wolf.

"Say that again," I tell him.

"A king with teeth who never used them." Thibault's hands are on his knees. "Forty years of truce. Forty years where the Alpha woke up every morning knowing the man across the water could end it before lunch. And every morning that man got up and went to his table and didn't."

"Isn't that restraint?"

"That's more than restraint. That's a decision, every day."

He looks at me as the old wolf on the levee did, measuring and unafraid, a man trying to understand.

"The wolves at the levee called me that," I say. "King of teeth. They meant the other thing, didn't they?"

"They meant the first thing. The young ones always mean the first thing." Thibault shakes his head. "The old ones know. Your father was the king of teeth because he had them and he kept them in his mouth. That's harder than using them. Anybody can bite."

He's right, damn him.

I've been carrying that phrase since the raid, in my pocket. I liked the weight of it: the insult, the sharp edges, the name for a thing that is nothing but appetite.

I didn't know there were two of them, one that means *monster* and one that means *the man who chose not to be,* and the difference between them is what I've been arguing with my father about since I was old enough to argue.

I told him mercy was weakness.

A wolf in my basement is telling me my father's restraint was the most frightening thing about him.

---

"He was winning," I say.

"He was winning."

"And the wolves knew it. Your captains, your Alpha, everybody who put men through those windows knew they were sending them at a man who would tear them apart?"

"Yes."

"So why go?"

"Because somebody told Gaspard there was an opening." Thibault's voice goes careful again. "Not a chance to win but an opening: a window that was unlatched and a room that was full. The idea wasn't to kill your father but to make it look like we tried."

The lamp flickers.

"Make it look like you tried?" I say.

"So that afterward, when the council voted, the vote would already be made." He meets my eyes. "Wolves in the gallery. Wolves at the windows. A whole court watching the glass come down. That's a war vote, Your Majesty. That's the vote already cast."

I've been asking who killed my father, and asking it about a blade and a wound and a man with a key.

Thibault is telling me a larger story. He's telling me that the wolves were brought into that chamber not to kill a king but to provide a reason — a spectacle, a narrative, a room full of witnesses who would agree that the wolves had declared war.

And the person who arranged the opening — the person who unlatched the gallery windows — would have needed to be inside the chamber.

On our side.

---

"The man who told Gaspard," I say. "Did he have a name?"

"If he did, Gaspard never said it to me. I'd give it to you if I had it."

"What did Gaspard say about him?"

"That he'd been reliable, that the information had been good before, and that the cost was reasonable." Thibault pauses. "And that the man asked about his wife."

The room goes cold.

Both my hands press down on the arms of the chair, and the wood creaks. I can feel the grain finding the line where it would split if I let it.

I don't let it.

*He asked about his wife.*

Cassian asks about wives, about mothers and houses and the health of a grandmother's chest. He does it like other men breathe, constantly, and with a warmth that is entirely real.

"Describe him," I say.

"I never met him. I told you, this was above me."

"Gaspard described him?"

"Gaspard said old, older than he looked. Silver. Good coat." Thibault shifts. "He said the man was sad."

I close my eyes. Christ.

*He was sad.*

I could have a hundred men who fit that, because this city is full of old men in good coats who carry grief like dock workers carry rope, so long that the weight has become the posture.

But it sits on only one of them like that, and I know it.

---

I don't say his name.

A name said in front of a witness stays said. I'm not ready for what comes after it.

What I do is stand up, and push the chair back, and walk to the door with my hands steady.

"Am I done?" Thibault asks.

"Almost."

"Almost means a lot of things in a room with a locked door."

"It means I have something to check." I turn. "You'll be fed tonight, and Silas will come."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow I'll either let you go or I won't. Can you live with that?"

"I've lived with worse."

He nods, because he's been useful, and he knows useful has a shelf life.

"Your Majesty," he says.

I stop. "What now?"

"Your father earned that name. He earned it by having it in him and not using it." Thibault leans back in the chair. "What you do with it is your business. But remember a wolf said it to you and meant the second one."

I go up the stairs.

---

The archive room, late that night, after Silas and whatever couldn't wait, and six letters, all signed with the hook.

I spread them beside the ledger and look at the marks side by side, and they are the same writing. Selene told me that, and I didn't hear her because the hearing would have cost me more than I was ready to pay.

Five of the letters are routine — archive instructions, wine for the council, Ambroise's schedule.

The sixth is about a payment to a translator in the Bywater.

My father didn't write it, because the writing is neater than his, more deliberate, with a particular way of crossing a *t* that I have seen on every note Cassian has left on this desk since I was a child.

Cassian. God damn it. Cassian.

I let the name into the room. It sits where it sits, and the room doesn't change. Nothing in the house breaks, and the lamp goes on humming.

Somewhere above me, in my bed, a woman is asleep with the door open. I can hear her from here, if I stop breathing and let the house go quiet: her pulse, slow and heavy, and the small catch in it when she turns over. I had her against these shelves this afternoon for less than a minute before Silas knocked, and before that I hadn't touched her in three days. I've counted. I think about the car, and her thighs locked around my head in the footwell, and the salt-sweet taste of her, and my cock stirs against my thigh in a room full of my dead father's letters. I let it, because it's the only thing in this building tonight that isn't lying to me.

Tomorrow I sit across from my uncle and ask him about a translator in the Bywater, the way he taught me to ask: around the edges, with warmth, with the question about somebody's family that sounds like care and is.

His own instrument.

Let's see if it still cuts.
