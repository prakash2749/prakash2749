# 4 · Selene

He takes the boat out alone on Thursdays.

I know this because a wolf at a bar in Tremé told me six months ago while trying to impress a woman who wasn't listening. He said it as men talk about other men's routines, as proof the man is predictable, and predictable means safe, and safe means not worth worrying about.

Rémy Thibodeaux goes out at dawn on his boat and comes back at noon with shrimp. The whole dock knows his schedule, and I have been carrying that sentence like a key for half a year.

Today is Thursday.

---

Barataria smells like itself and nothing else.

Mud. Salt. The low green rot of standing water in a place where water never finishes standing. And underneath it, everywhere, the smell I can almost catch and never hold, the one that makes the back of my skull sit up and go very still.

It's pack, and the whole ground is soaked in it. How did my mother ever leave it?

I came in by bus, because my car wouldn't survive the shell road, and I'm walking the last mile in city shoes, which was a stupid fucking mistake. My mother would have known what shoes to wear.

---

The dock is at the bottom of a shell road that goes from pavement to gravel to nothing before you've finished noticing. There's a fuel station with a tin roof and a dog sleeping under it, and beyond that, the water.

I find a sun-cracked bench near the fuel station where I can see the slips.

The knife is in my bag, where it's been since the house. I can feel the shape of it against my hip like a word on the tip of my tongue.

---

I see him at eleven.

He brings the boat in bow-first, which I've been told is wrong for the slip but is how he does it, every time, because — according to the drunk in Tremé — he doesn't like having his back to the water when he ties off.

He's bigger than I expected.

I've known his height and build from a dozen descriptions in a dozen bars. Knowing and seeing are different languages, and the translation is never clean.

He steps off the boat and ties it off without looking at the rope. He's sunburned across the nose and down his forearms. His shirt is dark with sweat, and his right arm hangs wrong.

That arm. A vampire did that, the King, years back, when Rémy crossed a line on vampire ground. Every wolf in Barataria tells that story the same way.

He hauls two crates off the stern and stacks them on the dock, and a man I don't know comes down the slope to help, and they talk.

I can't hear them, because I'm too far and the wind is wrong.

Then it shifts, for a second, and I catch a piece.

"—told her I'd fix the porch Sunday," Rémy says. "She's gonna kill me."

"She's gonna kill you anyway, podna."

"Hell, let her. I'd rather die on a porch than on this shit boat."

The wind turns back, and the rest goes out over the water.

But I can see his face, and his face is not the face of a man who killed my parents. What did I think it would look like?

It's the face of a man unloading shrimp on a Thursday, ordinary and easy in it. He says something and the other man laughs, and Rémy grins. His whole body changes shape when he grins — he gets smaller somehow, younger, the bigness of him pulls in.

I hate that I can see that, and I hate that I'm sitting on a busted bench cataloging how a man smiles.

What the fuck is wrong with me?

He doesn't drink, and the same drunk told me that, unprompted. *Rémy doesn't touch it. Not since the business with the Ward family.*

The knife in my bag is not any heavier than it was this morning.

It feels heavier anyway.

---

I could do it now. Right fucking now.

The dock is empty.

The friend has gone back up the slope with one of the crates. Rémy is alone with the second one, and there's nobody between me and the end of the slip.

It would be fast, because I know where to put it. Fast, and then done, and then the bus back, and the rest of my life would start.

My hand goes to the bag.

And a voice says, "You look like you need water."

---

She's young, my age and small for a wolf. She's holding out a bottle with condensation running down the side of it.

I didn't hear her come up, so either she moves without a sound or I was somewhere else, and both of those are bad.

"I'm fine," I say. "Hot as hell, but fine."

"You've been sitting in the sun for an hour." She puts the bottle on the bench between us. "The bus doesn't come again until two."

Shit. She knows I came by bus, and she knows how long I've been here. I don't know whether she's been watching me or whether everybody on this dock knows everything that happens on it, and both options are the same shape.

"Thank you," I say. "Do you always bring strangers water?"

"Only the ones about to pass out on our bench. You here for somebody?"

"Just the water. Is that allowed?"

"Nobody comes down here for the water, girl. Not even us." She says it as cheerfully as a weather report. "The water's nasty."

"Then I'm a very confused tourist."

That gets half a smile out of her.

"You're not from Barataria, are you?"

"What the hell gave it away?"

"The shoes. Look at them." She looks at them with real pity. "Those are gonna die on the shell road."

"They've had a good life."

She sits down on the other end of the bench, close enough to be friendly and far enough to make no point of it. She's wearing a jacket that's too big for her and her hair is pulled back. She has the kind of face that's going to be something in a few years and hasn't figured that out yet.

Then she breathes in, and her whole expression changes.

There's no flinch, no pull-back, no face like biting into something spoiled.

She tips her head and looks at me as if she's heard me somewhere, a song from a room she can't place.

"You smell like my aunt," she says.

The bench is very solid underneath me, and I need it to be, because my chest has just come apart at a seam I didn't know was there.

Nobody has ever said that to me, not one goddamn wolf.

I have been read as *wrong* my entire life, and not once as *familiar.*

"Your aunt?" I manage.

"She left." The girl shrugs, big, the shrug you use when what you're shrugging about is the size of a room. "A long time ago, when I was little."

In pack, *left* and *contaminated* share a root, and she's used the smaller one, the one that means a person walked out under her own feet.

She's too young to know there's a choice. Christ, she's a baby.

"I'm sorry," I say, and it's the wrong thing. In English it means sympathy, but in wolf it means you're accepting a portion of the loss, and I have no right to that.

She doesn't seem to mind, just picks at the label on the water bottle and looks out at the dock.

"Nobody at home talks about her," she says. "So I don't really know why she left, just that she did, and that we're not supposed to say her name."

"That sounds like every family I've ever poured a drink for."

"You're a bartender?"

"On Frenchmen. You know it?"

"Mama says Frenchmen's where good wolves go to get stupid."

"Your mama's not wrong. I've served a few."

"Huh." She looks at me again, longer. "She liked music. Mama slips, sometimes. What's your name?"

"Selene."

"I'm Delphine." She tears a strip of label off and lets it drop. "You shouldn't be down here, you know."

"I know."

"My dad would — I mean, it's not safe. For someone who—" She stops and recalibrates. "You should go before the boats come back in."

"What happens when the boats come back in?"

"My brother happens. And my brother's friends, and their cousins, and everybody's dogs." She says it fondly, as if it's a joke between us.

Her dad is Gaspard, which makes this girl Gaspard Thibodeaux's daughter and Rémy's sister. I am sitting on a bench a dock's length from the man who murdered my parents, talking about water with his baby sister.

If she knew what I was here for, she'd be standing between me and the dock. Wolves stand between their family and a threat, and it's the first thing they learn.

She's sitting beside me instead, picking at a label, and that's either trust or innocence, and I'm about to betray both.

"My aunt made tea," she says. "The kind you have to get from Tremé, and she used to bring it when she visited."

My mother's tea, the tea I buy from a woman who has never asked me why.

I can't speak, because there's a sound in my throat that won't go up or down. Delphine sits there long enough to see it happen, and she doesn't ask about it.

Then she stands and brushes off her jeans.

"Get the two o'clock," she says. "Okay? And buy some real shoes before you come back. I'm serious. The shell road eats city people."

"Okay. Will you be here next time?"

"I'm always here."

She goes up the slope toward the houses, and I'm alone on the bench with a bottle of water and a knife in my bag and a sentence that has just rearranged a part of me I can't put back.

---

Rémy is still on the dock.

He's hosing the boat down with his back to me, his bad arm doing most of the work and the good one holding the hose. He has the radio on in the wheelhouse, and I can hear it from here.

I could do it, because he's alone, and I'm close, and the girl is gone, and there's nobody.

My hand is on the bag.

My hand stays on the bag. What am I waiting for?

---

The bus comes at two, and I'm on it. The knife is still in the bag where it started, and I cannot say why.

I have lived inside a single intention for four years, and I had a clear path to it today. I left without doing it, and if you asked me in either language why, I would not have an answer.

A girl happened on that bench, and she said I smelled like her aunt.

That isn't a fucking reason.

I'm taking the bus home anyway.

Next time I cross this water, I won't come on a bus. I'll come with something the wolves are afraid of.
