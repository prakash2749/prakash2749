Chapter Five

lucian

The first wolf breaks before I reach him.

His nerve goes first: he comes out of a house on the levee side with a rifle. A rifle. It’s so fucking human it almost earns him the time to use it, and then he sees me, and his whole stance changes.

I’ve seen it in men before, a decision unmaking itself in the middle of a body.

He drops the gun and shifts, and the shift takes him two heartbeats, and by the second one he’s moving, going west, going fast, going away.

I let him go. Why wouldn’t I? He put the gun down.

“Anything behind him?” I ask Silas.

“Nothing armed. You want him followed?”

“What for? He’s running the right way. Leave the rifle where it lies.”

Silas is at my left, and I set the pace and he reads the ground.

The settlement is small and badly lit: shell roads, tin roofs, dogs barking, a generator running somewhere on the far side. It’s a place where people live, and I’m in it with my people and a rule that exists because I said it out loud.

Armed wolves only, and the night is about to test whether I mean it.

The second one is in the road.

He’s shifted already, because he heard us coming, and he’s fucking big, rust-colored and broad across the chest. His ears are flat and his head is low and he’s not running.

He’s between us and a house with a light on in the kitchen. Who’s inside? Children, from the size of the shoes on the step.

In my language that’s a man standing in a doorway. And in his?

I don’t run at him, I walk, unhurried, because I’ve never once in my life needed to rush. Every line of him says charge, and every line of him stays where it is. A man who runs at you has lost control, and a man who walks toward you has already decided what’s going to happen.

He doesn’t charge.

He shifts, and the bones rearrange under his skin and he’s a man, naked, breathing hard, with his back against the wall of the house with the kitchen light on.

I lay my palm on his chest, open, against his sternum, testing a door to see if it’s locked.

I push. Gently.

He goes backward, and the wall goes first: the clapboard buckles and the studs give. He’s standing in the gap of his own house with my palm on his chest and the kitchen light on his face.

“There are people in that house who are not armed,” I say. “I am not going through that door.”

I take my hand off his chest and step back.

He stays in the wall a long time after I’ve gone, and the kitchen light stays on.

“You could have killed him,” Silas says, low.

“Could I?”

“Sire. You know you could.”

“I know what I could have done, and so does he. That’s the point.”

“Is it a good one, sire?”

“It’s the only one I’ve got tonight, Silas. Keep walking.”

My people take that image home, the restraint more than the violence. Nobody in this city understands this about my father’s philosophy. The rules are the proof that there’s an animal here that needs a leash.

Silas moves through the settlement like water through a drain, always downhill, always toward whatever hasn’t been checked.

At a line of four houses near the south end he checks three and skips the fourth without breaking stride. Why that one?

I notice, and I don’t ask. Silas’s answers are always true and never complete, and a man who gives you the truth in pieces has decided which pieces are yours.

They find us at the levee.

Three of them, shifted, coming out of the water where the land goes soft. They’re not strays — they’re organized, moving together, and the one in front is old enough to know what he’s looking at.

The young one doesn’t wait.

He comes out of the water fast, low, mud flying off his haunches. He gets one of mine by the leg, and I hear the cloth tear and the man scream.

Then I smell it: his blood, hot on the mud, and I haven’t fed since before the funeral. For one second the whole levee narrows to that smell. My gums ache, and I swallow, and my teeth want down.

I’m there before the scream finishes.

I take the wolf by the scruff, a soaked sack of fur and muscle heavier than any man, and lift him off my man as you’d lift a dog off a table. He twists in my grip, and his jaws snap at the air beside my throat, close enough that I feel the heat of his breath.

I throw him. He hits the levee slope a long way down and rolls, and by the time he gets his feet under him he’s looking at me from the ground with his ears flat and his belly in the mud.

He doesn’t come back.

The old one in front stops. He puts himself between me and the remaining wolf, and the set of his head is neither attack nor retreat.

He’s reading me. What does he see?

I let him read me.

It takes him about as long as a held breath, and then he looks at the ground behind me — at the road, at the houses, at the lights that are still on in all of them — and his stance changes.

The wolves behind him are waiting for a signal, and he doesn’t give one.

Instead he shifts back.

He’s old, with a face that’s been out in weather for all of it. He’s got a scar down one arm that was stitched badly and healed worse.

He spits on the ground between us.

“King of teeth,” he says.

Is that what they call us down here? I know what it means: a king who rules by appetite, with nothing in him but the bite.

I show him mine. The full set, the ones that come down when the feeding starts, the ones my father kept hidden for three centuries.

The old wolf takes a step back, involuntary, the first honest movement he’s made since the water.

I hold it a breath. Then I close my mouth and the face goes back to being a face. The man in the mud is looking at me as if a room he thought he knew just lost a wall.

I’ve been called worse by people I loved. He can have the name. I’ll keep the teeth.

“You can have the fucking levee,” I tell him. “I’m done.”

He goes back into the water, and the two behind him follow, and the bayou takes them completely.

King of teeth.

Fuck him. I put it somewhere I can find it later.

We come back through a settlement holding its breath, and who can blame it?

“One unaccounted for,” Silas says.

“Where? Tell me it’s not the water again.”

“The dock, the south slip.” He nods toward the water. “Young and armed. He was outside when we came in and he went into the shed.”

“Still there?”

“Still there. You want him dead or talking?”

“Take him,” I say. “Alive, damn it.”

The man in the shed comes out alive.

He’s forty-something, and scared enough to look younger. He’s got his hands up and Silas has him by the collar, knuckles against his throat. The man’s face is the face of somebody who has just found out what his night is going to be about.

“What’s your name?” I say. “And don’t lie to me, because I’ll know, and then this night gets a hell of a lot longer for both of us.”

“Thibault.” He swallows. “I was on the dock. I wasn’t armed. I wasn’t fucking—”

“You were outside with a knife on your belt. Did you forget it was there?”

His mouth opens and closes.

“That’s armed, you fucking idiot,” I say. “In my house, that’s armed. Is it different in yours?”

He doesn’t answer.

“Thibault.” I lean down. “You were in my father’s chamber the night he died. You came in through the gallery with the rest of them.”

His whole face goes slack, mouth open, eyes wide, his throat working, the kind of naked recognition that I can read without any language at all.

“I was there,” he says. “Yes.”

“Then you’re coming with me.”

“Where? To die somewhere drier?”

“To talk. Whether you die afterward is up to what you say.”

Silas puts him in the boat without asking whether there’s a reason, because Silas has never once needed a reason to be supplied out loud. He reads a decision like other men read weather.

We’re on the water heading back when one of my people, the youngest, says it.

“Clean.”

He means the raid: no dead, no burned houses, no women pulled out of beds. He says it close to proud, and the others take it the same way.

“Clean?” I say.

“Nobody dead, sire. Isn’t that the point?”

“Is that what you think that was?”

He doesn’t know how to answer, so he doesn’t, and one of the others mutters, “Hell of a night,” and nobody disagrees.

Clean is the wrong word. The word is chosen — I chose what to leave standing, so I could have chosen otherwise, and every wolf in that settlement is awake right now knowing it.

My father did mercy. What I’ve done tonight is carpentry — a precise amount of violence applied to the joints.

The cost of the message is that the line was mine to draw.

Thibault sits in the bow of the boat with his wrists tied, his knees drawn up and his head down, and he hasn’t said a word since the dock.

I look at the back of his head and think about what’s in it.

He was in the gallery when the windows came down. He was there when my father was still standing, and he was there after. What did he see? Who was standing where?

I could ask him now, but a question asked in the dark gets a dark answer. A man who talks because he’s afraid of the water will say something else when the water is gone.

I want him under a roof with a lamp on, and time to think. What does the truth cost him, next to the alternative?

My father would have asked on the boat. He would have put a palm on the man’s shoulder and spoken low, directly, warmly enough that the man forgot he was answering a king.

I don’t have that, because whatever carried that gift went into the ground with him.

I have a lamp and a locked room and the patience to wait. Isn’t that enough?

Silas brings the boat in and we tie off, and I don’t say a word.

I take the prisoner by the arm and walk him up the dock, and behind us my people go home to whatever they have, and not one of them knows what I want from the man at the end of my hand.

They did their job. I’m about to do mine.
