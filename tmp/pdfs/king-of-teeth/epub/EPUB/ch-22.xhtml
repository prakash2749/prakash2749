Silas has Cray, more or less.

“The money going into Cray’s account,” I say. “Have you traced it?”

The room changes, somewhere under the air and the light.

He goes, and I sit at the desk with the ledger open and the chain laid out in my head link by link, and it’s all one chain.

“The poison came through a broker,” I say. “A man named Cray, in the Bywater.”

“Through the financial records.” I keep my voice level. “Ward’s ledger — the translator. It has a mark in it that matches our internal correspondence seal.”

There it is: I’ve put the evidence in front of him, and only the evidence, the mark in the margin, the connection between the ledger and the house.

Cassian looks at me, and his mouth tightens — a small closing I have to be very careful about reading, because I can’t unread it if I read it wrong.

“The house seal,” he says.

“Your father’s?”

“My father used it. So did other people.”

A pause, a long one, filled with the sound of a house in the afternoon — a clock somewhere, a footstep above us.

“So did I,” he says.

The words go into the room and sit there.

“I used that mark before your father did,” he says. “He took it from me. Or I gave it to him — I’ve forgotten which, and it was a long time ago, and we were both younger.”

He says it openly, without defense, without explanation, as though it were a fact about the architecture of the house rather than a confession.

“You signed Ward’s payments,” I say.

“I signed a great many things over the years with that mark.” He leans back. “If you’re asking whether I knew Daniel Ward, the answer is yes. I commissioned translations from him, legal work, mostly, the same kind of work any Elder commissions when they’d rather not put their name on a filing.”

“That’s not what the payments look like.”

“What do the payments look like?”

“Large, regular, held in trust.”

“Then they look like exactly what they are, for God’s sake.” He meets my eyes. “Private commissions, paid at a premium, held by a careful man who understood that discretion has a price.”

It’s perfect. Every word of it is plausible, and every word of it fits, and a man who didn’t know what I know would take it and go on.

But I know what a blade wound looks like. I know it was under the ribs, angled upward, and it didn’t close.

“Thank you,” I say.

“Is that all? No more questions for the old man?”

“For now.”

He stands and gathers his coat — it was already on, so the gathering is a gesture rather than a function, a man leaving a room in the posture of leaving.

“Lucian.”

“When you find what you’re looking for,” he says, “come to me first, not the council, not Marguerite.” His hand is on the doorframe, where it always is when he says the last thing. “Come to me, and I’ll help you carry it.”

He goes, and I sit in the study with his offer in the room, and his honesty about the mark, and his perfect, reasonable explanation.

And I don’t believe him.

Not one piece of it: not the private commissions, not the premiums, not the careful translator who held payments in trust.

I don’t believe him because I watched his face when I said the word mark, and what I saw wasn’t surprise.

It was relief, God damn him.

He’d been waiting for me to ask, with the answer ready, and a ready answer is a built one.

The truth doesn’t come with furniture.
