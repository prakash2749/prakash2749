Chapter Two

lucian

The door to my father’s study has been unlocked since Tuesday.

I could fix it in an afternoon, but I sit down in my father’s chair instead and look at the door from the inside.

He kept this room open because he wanted people to come in. The people who could walk through it without knocking were few enough to count on my fingers, and every one of them was chosen.

Chosen is what I keep getting caught on. Which one of them did he choose wrong?

They come for me before noon.

Marguerite first, because Marguerite is always fucking first: my father’s sister, twice my age, who has held the council together since Tuesday with nothing but a voice and the correct use of the word traditionally.

“You look terrible,” she says.

“I’ve been traveling. Where do you think I’ve been?”

“You’ve been sitting in that chair.” She crosses to the window and opens the shutter I closed. “Ambroise says you haven’t fed.”

“Ambroise talks. Doesn’t he ever stop?”

“Ambroise is a physician, and you are the last Croix, and you are not starving yourself to death in my brother’s chair, do you hear me? And if you’d like a list of the things I’ve had to say out loud this week to keep that from sounding like a problem, I can provide one.”

She doesn’t sit, and she never sits in here. She stands where she can see the door, and at her age it isn’t a decision anymore.

Then the rest of them arrive behind her, and the room stops being my father’s and becomes a chamber.

Aldric is the first one to speak, because Aldric is always the first one to speak when there isn’t a king in the room.

He’s been occupying chairs and calling it governance since before I was born. This week he finally has the room he’s wanted, one with nobody at the desk.

“The wolves came in through the gallery and the wolves took the King. The court requires an answer.”

“Which court? Yours?”

“The court that buried him, boy.”

The room tightens around boy, from a man who couldn’t hold a fucking door against me if I leaned on it with one shoulder. Every head in this chamber turns toward me instead of him.

I stay seated.

“Say that again.”

He blinks. “Which—”

“The last word.”

The silence in this room is the kind you can feel against your teeth. Marguerite hasn’t moved, and nobody else has either. Which half of my father did I get? They’re waiting to find out, and this is the moment they get to look.

“I misspoke,” Aldric says. “Surely you can see that?”

“You didn’t.” I look at him, just look, with nothing in my face he can use as a reason and nothing he can use as an excuse. “You wanted to see what would happen, didn’t you?”

He doesn’t kneel because I told him to, and that’s the part the room will misremember. Let the bastards. I didn’t say a word about it, didn’t raise my voice, didn’t stand.

He kneels because standing has started to be a decision, and he isn’t ready to make one.

“The wolves are not your concern tonight,” I tell him. “They’re mine.”

Here’s what they want, and none of them say it in one sentence, so I’ll say it for them.

They want a crown on me by the end of the fucking week, and why the rush?

Marguerite makes the case properly: the city has no king, and the continent has noticed. Three Houses that owe House Croix nothing have sent condolences worded as questions.

What she leaves out is that a crowned king can be advised, and an uncrowned heir can only be waited on. Marguerite has been waiting since Tuesday and she does not enjoy it. Who would?

“There’s no contest to your claim,” she says. “You’re not weighing that, because there isn’t anyone to contest it with.”

“I’m aware.”

“Are you? You are what this House has, a born heir, and every one of us has been alive long enough to know how rarely that happens.”

“Say it again.”

She stops. “Which part? I’ve said a great many things this morning, and all of them were true, and you’ve listened to none of them.”

“That there’s nobody to contest it with.”

“There is nobody to contest it with.”

I turn it over while she watches me do it.

Because it cuts the other way, and I don’t think she’s looked at the other edge. If there is nobody who gains the city by my father’s death, then nobody killed him to get the city.

Which means whoever did it wanted something else. What?

Bastien is in the corner with a folder he hasn’t been asked for.

He’s the youngest creature in this room by centuries. He’s been holding that paper against his chest the whole conversation, waiting for a gap.

“Sire,” he says, into the first one.

“No.”

“Sorry. My lord, your—” He gives up on it. “I pulled the door log for the house, who came and went, the week of.”

Marguerite’s head comes around as fast as a gun.

“Nobody asked you to do that,” she says.

“No, ma’am.”

“Then why have you done it?”

And he looks at me instead of at her.

He’s decided, in the last second, who he answers to, and I watch him decide it. I know what it will cost him.

“Because he asked who was in the room,” Bastien says. “He asked it on the first night, and nobody wrote it down.”

The room does a thing I don’t like, and it closes a little.

“Give it to Silas,” I tell him. “Not to me, not to anybody else in this house. Silas.”

“Yes.”

“And Bastien?”

“Sire?”

“You were right to pull it.”

He goes out of the room taller than he came into it.

“He’s a child,” Marguerite says. “Did you see him?”

“He’s a hundred years old, for Christ’s sake.”

“He’s eighty-nine, and he worships you.” She turns from the window. “Lucian. The crown. Please.”

“No.”

“Give me a reason I can carry out of this room. Can you do that much?”

“Grief,” I say. “Use grief. They’ll take it.”

“They will take it.” She doesn’t blink. “It isn’t what it is.”

And there it is, and I’d forgotten she’s my father’s sister and not my father’s courtier.

“No,” I say. “It isn’t.”

So here is the reason, and she will get one sentence of it and no more.

The moment they put that thing on my head, the first business of my reign is a war vote, and it’s already drafted. Somebody in this house has had it drafted since Wednesday. I could name the men who’d move it, and the ones who’d second it before the wax cooled.

And the moment that vote passes, the question of who killed Aurelian Croix is answered forever. Not investigated — answered. The wolves did it. It goes into the record in those words, and every year after that, anybody who reopens it is a man insulting a war.

Uncrowned, I am a son with a grievance and no procedure, and I can ask anything, of anyone.

Crowned, I’m the one thing in this city that isn’t allowed to wonder.

“I’ll take the crown when I have a killer,” I tell her.

“They’ll read it as weakness in about a month, you know that?”

“Yes.”

“Your father would have taken it on the first night,” she says. “And then done as he pleased.”

“My father had four centuries of everybody assuming he was decent. I have a room full of people wondering which half of him I got.”

That lands, and she lets it.

“One month,” Marguerite says. “I can hold them a month. After that I’ll be holding them against their own interest, and I am not going to do that for you.”

“Understood.”

“Can you give me something I can tell a man in confidence to keep him quiet?”

“Tell him to go to hell. No — tell him this.” I look at the door, my father’s door, open since Tuesday. “Every door in this house is open because my father wanted it that way. If someone walks through one and touches what’s mine, I’ll remove the hands, and then I’ll remove the door.”

She says nothing for three seconds, which from Marguerite is a standing ovation.

“That’s not evidence, is it?”

“It’s not meant to be.”

“Then find the evidence.” She goes out. “And eat something.”

Cassian comes in after the room empties, as he always does.

He waits until the noise has gone out of a place, and then he’s in the doorway without knocking, because he has never had to.

“Boy.”

“Come in.”

He looks at the chair I’m in and doesn’t say anything about it. He crosses and takes the seat across the desk. He sits down as if he has nowhere else to be today.

My shoulders let go.

“She’s been at you about the crown, hasn’t she?” he says.

“She’s right, damn her.”

“She’s very nearly right, which is worse.” He settles back. “Go and see Ambroise, and take him upstairs and make him drink something. He’ll refuse twice, and then he’ll tell you more than his report does.”

It’s genuinely fucking useful, and he’s handed it over as easily as a coat. How does he always know?

“Cassian.”

“Mm.”

“Where were you, in the chamber?”

He doesn’t hesitate and he doesn’t perform the thinking. “Behind the west range of columns. With wolves coming through the gallery I went for the platform, and by the time I got there your father was already down.”

“Who was closest to him?”

“I was,” he says evenly. “I was the first one to reach him. I’ve been asking myself the same question all week, and the honest answer is that I don’t know what happened in the last part of it, because I was looking at the wolves and not at him.”

“Nobody was.”

“No.” A pause. “Nobody looks at anything else, in a room with wolves in it.”

He leans forward and rests his fingers on my forearm, briefly, and then takes them back.

“How is your mother’s house in Toulouse?” he asks. “Do you still keep it?”

“Standing, but I haven’t been.”

“Go, when this is finished.”

“I will.”

“You won’t, will you, you stubborn devil?” He stands, and smiles, and it reaches his eyes, because with him it always does. “But you should.”

He goes, and the door doesn’t close behind him, because it has been open since Tuesday and nobody in this house is going to be the one who shuts it.

Wolves came in through the gallery windows, and three hundred people saw the glass come down.

Wolves did not open the east range from the inside. Somebody in this house did, somebody with a key or who never needed one. My father would have walked past him on the way to his own death without putting a hand up.

I’ve asked who twice now, and both times somebody has answered me about the fucking wolves.

I’m not going to be crowned over my father’s fucking body.

I’m going to be crowned over somebody else’s.

So I get up and find Silas in the hall.

“Names,” I tell him. “Every goddamn one who was in that chamber when he died. By dark.”

“And then, sire?”

“And then I start with whichever one is out drinking tonight.”
