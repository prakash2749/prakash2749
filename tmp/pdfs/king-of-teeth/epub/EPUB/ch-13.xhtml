Chapter Thirteen

lucian

Four centuries of records and someone has already pulled them.

That’s what I find when I go down to the archive room, and absence would be obvious and stupid. This is order. Somebody has been through the household ledgers, the personnel files, the correspondence bound by year and sealed with my father’s stamp.

They didn’t take anything. They organized it. Who does that?

The folders are indexed, and every gap in the record has a tab in it, a slip of paper, unsigned, no handwriting I recognize, that says only what’s missing and from which year.

Whoever did this wanted me to see what wasn’t here. Why?

Silas doesn’t know who did it, or says he doesn’t, which with Silas is a distinction I’ve learned to leave alone.

“The archive’s been open since the funeral,” he says. “Staff had access, council had access, and Marguerite was down here the second day.”

“Marguerite doesn’t index. Does she?”

“No. She reads and remembers and tells you what you missed, and then she tells you twice, God help you.” He looks at the shelves. “This is someone who works with their hands.”

I pull a folder at random, household accounts sixteen years old. Every entry is present, every page numbered, and at the back there’s a tab.

Missing: receipts for the east door commission, spring of this year. Three pages.

The handwriting on the tab is small and precise, a hand taught in a schoolroom by someone who believed in consistency.

“Find out who did this,” I say. “And find out tonight, before they tidy anything else.”

“I’ll ask. Who do you want me to start with?”

“Don’t ask. Look. Whoever it was didn’t want to be asked. They wanted it to be here when I came looking.”

Silas nods and goes up the stairs without touching the rail, hands free, weight forward, ready for whatever’s at the top.

I start working.

The investigation has to wait, because it requires knowing what I’m looking at, and I don’t. I’ve spent four centuries leaving the records to the men my father paid to keep them, and those men are either dead or frightened.

So I go back to the beginning, to the household rolls. Who was on the staff the year my father died, and the year before that, and the ten years before that?

I’m not good at it, because I’m good at rooms, at reading who’s in them, who has a key, who walked past what to stand where they’re standing. Paper is a different animal.

But the paper has doors in it too, if you know where to push.

She finds me in the archive around noon. How does she always find me?

I don’t hear her come in, and it’s becoming a pattern. She stands in the doorway for a count I’ll never know the length of, and then she says, “You’re reading those wrong.”

I look up, and she’s in the clothes she arrived in, and there’s coffee on her thumb. She’s looking at the ledger I’m holding as Silas looks at a trail.

“These are household accounts,” I say. “What else would they be?”

“Those are household accounts with annotations in wolf trade pidgin.” She crosses and takes the ledger from me, and her fingers brush mine, and neither of us mentions it. “Here, this column. You’re reading it as French shorthand.”

“It isn’t? Then what is it?”

“It’s a trade cipher that wolf families use for cross-territory business: dock work, shipping, labor contracts. It shifts between French and a Barataria register that nobody outside the bayou writes down.”

She turns the page, her nail tracking a line of annotation I’d been reading as a clerk’s abbreviation for three hours.

“This says the east door commission was subcontracted,” she says. “It’s not missing, it moved. Somebody in this house was sending work to wolf contractors through a translation layer.”

I’ve been staring at this ledger since morning, and she’s been in the room for less than a minute.

“You read this? All of it?”

“I read everything, and that’s the whole problem.” She sits in the chair across from me and pulls another folder toward her. “My father was a translator. I grew up in a house full of documents in three languages, and he taught me to see the seams.”

We work.

It’s slow, and it’s close: she reads the wolf layer and I read the political one. Where they overlap, where the same transaction appears in two registers and the registers don’t agree, we find the gaps.

She reads rooms I didn’t know had walls.

I watch her decode a funding annotation that I would have taken to Silas. Silas would have taken to the Marchand woman, and the Marchand woman would have sent me back with a question. She does it with a coffee cup in one hand and her father’s silence in the other. She doesn’t look up, and I can hear her heartbeat from where I’m sitting, and it’s steady.

She turns a page and stops. What has she found?

“Lucian.”

Her voice has changed, and I look at the page. In the margin, in the wolf cipher, in handwriting she clearly recognizes: Aurelian’s name. My father’s name, written in a language he didn’t speak, in his own household records, by someone who was communicating in both directions.

My hand closes over hers on the page.

I don’t decide to do it. It happens like the hand on her throat happened, the body arriving before the mind. The body’s answer to my father’s name in a place it shouldn’t be is hold what you’re holding and don’t let go.

Her fingers go still under mine, and she looks up.

My eyes are wrong, and I can feel it, because behind them is a version of me that doesn’t sit in rooms and wait. It lasts a second, maybe two.

I let go.

“Sorry,” I say. “Christ. I’m sorry.”

“Don’t be.” She looks at me steadily. “That’s the first honest thing your hands have done since I got here.”

She doesn’t take her hand back.

I lift it, and turn her wrist over on the ledger and bring it up to my mouth, and I don’t bite. I breathe over the pulse and feel it jump against my lips. Her knees shift under the table and her thighs press together. I can smell exactly what that does to her.

“Not here,” she says, her voice gone low and rough.

“Where, then? Name the room.”

“Somewhere with a lock.”

“Nothing in this fucking house locks.”

“Then you’d better be quick about it, Your Majesty.”

“I’m never quick.”

“Oh, for fuck’s sake. I know.”

I put her wrist down on my father’s accounts, very carefully, as if it might go off. Then I pick up the ledger again, because one of us has to, and I read the same line four times.

The household is larger than I thought, page after page of names: the humans who don’t know what they work for, the vampires who’ve been here longer than I’ve been paying attention, and the handful brought in since the truce, by Marguerite or by Cassian or by my father himself.

I pull the record for each of the handful, and most are straightforward: young vampires from good houses, sent here as a courtesy, and Marguerite’s people, placed with her usual precision.

One doesn’t have a record. Why not?

It was never created: a name on the payroll with no entry behind it, no hiring record, no note in anyone’s writing. He’s been on the household roll for years and nobody has ever written down where he came from.

The name is Tobias Vale.

I don’t know him. Has he ever been in a room I was in? Or was he, and I didn’t look?

Vale, Cassian’s name. A cousin, probably, because the Vales are a large house, and most of them are administrative, the kind of vampires who keep courts running and never sit at the table.

I write the name down and move on, because a man with no record is either an oversight or a person somebody decided not to document, and both of those are worth knowing and neither is worth stopping for.

The correspondence takes longer.

My father kept everything. Letters from Houses on the continent, from the Conclave — the continental authority that ratifies kings and has never once bothered to exercise it, as far as I know — from Gaspard Thibodeaux in the years after the truce, written in a hand that suggests the wolf learned to write late and never enjoyed it.

And from Cassian.

They wrote to each other, and I hadn’t known that. They lived in the same city, saw each other most days, and still they wrote — short notes, mostly, about small things. A bottle of something, a book one of them had read, a restaurant recommendation.

The notes are warm.

I stop reading them after the third one because my chest is tightening and I don’t have the time for it.

What I’m looking for isn’t warmth but the household’s private correspondence seal, the mark my father used on internal messages, the informal ones that didn’t carry the House stamp. A little hooked figure, like a fishhook or a question mark, that he drew by hand on anything he sent inside the walls.

I find it on letter after letter in the past year alone, all of them internal, all of them addressed to members of the household or to people with regular access.

I don’t find what I’m looking for, but I know the shape of it now: a mark that somebody outside the house might have seen, might have copied down, might have kept in a ledger without knowing what it meant.

I’ll need her father’s book for that, the one she hasn’t opened.

It’s late when I come up.

The house has settled for the night: lamps on in the halls, the smell of onions from the kitchen, and the particular absence of noise that means everyone who matters has gone to a room with a door on it.

Cassian is in the front hall.

He’s putting on his coat, going home. He came by this evening when I wasn’t there, as he often does. He comes most evenings and sits in the study and drinks a glass and leaves without being asked to stay.

“Boy.”

“Going? Already?”

“It’s late.” He buttons the coat. “You were in the archive?”

“How did you know?”

“You have dust on your collar.” He reaches out and brushes it off, like he did when I was a boy. “Find anything?”

“Paperwork. Mountains of it.”

“Your father loved paperwork. God knows why.”

“My father loved the idea of paperwork, and left the actual work to other people.”

“True.” Cassian smiles. “He always said the archive would be the last place he’d look and the first place he should.”

I stop.

The second time he’s said that: the same sentence, the same rhythm, handed to me like a coat in bad weather. Why twice?

“He said that?”

“After the business with the Aldric cousin, years ago. He meant it, and then he didn’t do it, because that was Aurelian, a man with perfect instincts and no follow-through on the domestic end.”

“You think I should search the household? My own staff?”

“I think your father’s records will tell you what his rooms won’t.” He adjusts his cuffs. “How is the Marchand woman? Ondine. Silas was there this week.”

The change of subject is so smooth I feel it happen and don’t mind it, because Cassian’s warmth is a climate, and you live in it.

“She’s well, I think. Silas would know better. He’s been out there more than he admits.”

“Has he? Good for Silas. God knows he needs somewhere to go that isn’t a doorway. Her grandmother’s chest was bad, and I used to worry.” He opens the front door. “You should order the body examined, if you haven’t.”

“The body? Now?”

“Your father’s,” he says gently. “Ambroise has been waiting, and he won’t do it without your word, and the longer he waits, the less he’ll find.”

He’s right, about this and about the archive and about Ondine Marchand. Every piece of advice he gives me is true and useful and arrives in the exact shape of innocence.

“I’ll go down tomorrow,” I say.

“Take him upstairs first and give him a drink.” He’s in the doorway now, backlit. “He does better when someone remembers he’s a person before he’s a physician.”

“Thank you.”

“For what? For being old and interfering?” He smiles. “How is your mother’s house in Toulouse?”

“Standing, I assume. Why does everyone keep asking me about houses?”

“Go, when this is finished.”

He goes, and the door stays open behind him, because it always does. The night air comes into the hall smelling like the Quarter after rain: wet stone, old wood, jasmine from the courtyard.

I stand there for a while like a fucking fool.

He’s told me to examine the body and to search the records. He’s pointed me at two things that will lead me straight to the truth. He’s done it in the voice of a man who wants me to find it.

Why would anyone point me at the truth and then go home to bed? I don’t understand that.

I go back to the archive and I pull my father’s body order — the one that tells Ambroise what he’s permitted to do and when — and I sign it, and I leave it on the desk for morning.

Then I go upstairs and stand outside the room in the west wing where a woman is sleeping with the door open. I don’t go in, and I don’t close it.

She’s asleep with every door in her life standing open. Who taught her to sleep like that?

“Goodnight, ghost,” I tell the dark, too low to wake her.

I ordered my father’s body examined.

The first honest act of the office.

Somebody in this house is counting on it.
