Chapter Thirty-Six

selene

My apartment smells like dust and a coffee I made weeks ago and never drank.

I came straight here from Barataria, where this morning I stood at the treeline and watched Delphine walk her brother to the edge of pack ground while the whole pack watched. He didn’t look at his father once, and she was the only one who walked with him.

I haven’t been here in weeks, and shit, it shows. The plant on the windowsill is dead. It was dying when I left, and the leaving finished it. Rest in peace, you little bastard.

The deadbolt and the chain go on behind me, two locks Silas had his people put on while I was gone, the week somebody broke a window to get at me.

The knife is in my bag, where it’s been since the shed, blade clean. I set the bag down next to the toaster with the intention of never opening it again.

I take it out. It’s my father’s, a short blade, wood handle, a nick in the bolster where somebody dropped it. I’ve sharpened it more times than I’ve used it. The last time I sharpened it I was sitting on this floor with a king on my linoleum and an intention so clean I could see my own face in it.

The intention is gone, like a word you’ve been using wrong for years, until somebody shows you the real one.

I was going to kill Rémy Thibodeaux, and that was the shape of my life for four years, every day arranged around it — the language learned, the schedules memorized, the bars worked, the bus rides taken — a woman building herself into a weapon aimed at one man on one boat.

And the man on the boat was a fucking instrument, somebody else’s hand, somebody else’s lie, a wolf sent to kill a translator by a vampire who asked about his mother first.

I didn’t kill Rémy. I stood in a shed with a knife in my hand and a man with his hands down saying go ahead, and I lowered it. I haven’t forgiven him. He’d saved my life on a dock days before, and you can’t kill a man you owe. The debt was the only honest thing left in the room.

He’s alive, and my parents are still dead, and that doesn’t fucking change. The man who ordered their deaths is dead too, drained and beheaded in a study with the door standing open. I stood in that doorway and watched every second of it.

Does it even out? It doesn’t.

Revenge promises you a clean floor at the end, and there’s no clean floor.

I put the knife in the junk drawer, next to the batteries and a menu from a restaurant that closed ages ago, something you keep but stop carrying. That’s the whole ceremony.

The bar is the same, and Antoine is behind the register. He looks up when I come in and his face lands somewhere between relief and the plain annoyance of a man who’s been doing two people’s work.

“You’re alive,” he says. “Where the hell have you been? I had to work a Saturday with Tanya. Do you know what that’s like? She sings, Selene. She sings to the limes.”

“I’m alive.”

“You look different. You know that?”

“I’ve been sleeping somewhere else. Mind your own goddamn business.”

“Yeah, I can tell. You’ve got that look. Like a cat that found the good windowsill.” He shuts the register. “You sleeping somewhere better?”

“Somewhere with higher ceilings. And worse coffee, holy shit. It’s like drinking a tire.”

“We’re short tonight,” he says, “because Tanya quit.”

“When?”

“Tuesday. She said the energy was wrong, if you can believe it. Said the bar had a ghost.”

“Did it?”

“Only on the nights you worked, apparently.”

“Jesus.”

He shrugs. “Can you close? Or are you too fancy now?”

“Shut up, Antoine. I can close. Shit, I can always close.”

“And some wolf girl came in asking for you last week, young, jacket about three sizes too big.”

“Did she leave a name? A number?”

“She left a tip.” He looks personally offended. “On a club soda. Five dollars on a club soda, Selene. Who raised these people?”

The bar fills like it always does. At nine o’clock the crowd turns over, and the room becomes the machine I know how to operate. I pour drinks and wipe the bar and listen.

A vampire comes in around ten, young, well-dressed, with the particular confidence of a man who knows what he is and enjoys the knowing. He orders a bourbon and looks at me like they all look at bartenders, briefly, like furniture.

Then he looks again, and his nostrils flare. He’s caught whatever I am on the air, and he’s trying to place it, and he can’t.

“Do I know you?” he says. “Have we met?”

“No.”

“You smell—” He stops. “Sorry. You smell familiar.”

“I get that.”

“From where, though? It’s driving me crazy.”

“No idea,” I say, and slide him his change. “Somewhere you’d remember, probably.”

“I’d remember a bartender who smells like that.”

“Then I guess we’ve never met, sweetheart. Drink your fucking bourbon.”

That’s going to keep happening. How long before every vampire in the Quarter has caught it?

He drinks his bourbon and leaves, and the tip is extraordinary. I put it in the jar without looking at it.

After midnight the crowd thins and I’m alone behind the bar with two regulars and the television set.

Antoine comes out of the kitchen drying his hands and leans on the bar next to me.

“So,” he says. “The tourist.”

“What tourist?”

“Don’t. Fifty-dollar bourbon, and he didn’t drink it.” He watches my face. “That’s where you’ve been sleeping.”

I don’t answer, which is an answer.

“He treat you right? Because if he doesn’t—”

I think about it, actually think about it, standing there with a rag in my hand, and he waits.

“He turns pages from the corner,” I say.

Antoine stares at me. “I don’t know what the fuck that means.”

“It means yes. Happy now?”

“Then why do you look like you’re about to cry in my bar? You never cry in my bar. You cried once in the walk-in, and you told me it was onions.”

“Fuck off, it’s allergies.”

“To what?”

“You, you nosy bastard.”

He shakes his head and goes back to the kitchen, and at the door he stops.

“Girl,” he says, without turning around. “You’re allowed.”

Then he’s gone, and I’m standing behind my own bar with my eyes stinging, like an idiot.

I wipe the bar and I think about what I know. What do I actually know?

My father was a translator, and he did private work for a man who used a hooked mark and paid too much and never gave a name. The man killed him to close the account, and the killing was done by a wolf, and the wolf was done by a lie.

The man is dead, and the wolf is alive. The lie is in a ledger in a house across the Quarter, being held by a king with an ugly crown and clean hands.

And Cray: somebody paid a broker named Cray, and Silas has been in the archive every night since the study, and he won’t say what he’s found. Silas doesn’t say things he can’t carry yet.

A grieving uncle killed a king and was killed in turn. The Code held, and that part of the story is finished.

Tonight I close a bar and count a register and walk home through a city that doesn’t know what the fuck lives in it.

And tomorrow I keep asking. My father left me a ledger with for S, when she asks written on the last page, and I’m not done asking.

I close up, count the register, put the envelope in the safe, and then I sit on my crate in the alley and take my phone out.

Three contacts that aren’t the bar: my landlord, my dentist, and the woman in Tremé who sells me the tea.

I add a fourth, and it isn’t him.

I already have Lucian. He’s been in my phone since the night he texted me about the ledger, saved under a name I’m not going to share with anyone.

The fourth number is Delphine.

She gave it to me at the treeline, the day I came for Rémy. She wrote it on the back of a gas receipt with a pen that was running out, and she said, “In case you need a wolf who’s not my brother.”

Will I call it? I haven’t, and I’m not going to call it tonight.

But the number is in my phone, and the phone is in my pocket, and for the first time in my life I have a wolf’s number that isn’t attached to an intention.

It’s attached to something that started the night I walked into a club on Perdido Street and has been growing ever since, slow and stubborn, in a part of me I didn’t know had soil.

I walk home. A man on a corner is playing a trumpet with his case open. The notes go up into the warm air and stay there.

I pass the block where Lucian pulled over in Silas’s car. I can see the spot — dark, residential, the exact stretch of curb where I climbed into his lap and the horn went off.

I don’t stop, but I smile, and it’s a new one, not the bar smile, not the one I used to practice in a mirror with a crack in the corner. Nobody sees it, and that’s fine.

The apartment is dark when I get home, the plant still dead, the cup still in the sink.

I lock the door, deadbolt and chain, same as every night since the window, and then I unlock it.

I stand there with my hand on the chain and think about Lucian’s house, where nothing closes all the way, and about my mother, who left a pack and never closed the door behind her because it was already closed for her.

I take the chain off and leave the deadbolt, one lock, not two, and I go to bed.

The bed is mine, and the sheets are mine. The window is the one that opens, not the one that’s painted shut. I leave it cracked, because the air outside smells like rain and the Quarter and cut grass.

My phone is on the pillow beside me: my landlord, my dentist, the tea woman, and a wolf named Delphine.

And Lucian, saved under a name I chose, in a city that is afraid and alive and mine.

I close my eyes. The knife is in the drawer, the ledger is in the house, and Cassian’s last word is in the air.

And the last thing I think about before I sleep isn’t the man I came here to kill.

It’s the man I’m going back to in the morning.
