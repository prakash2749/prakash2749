Chapter Thirty-Four

lucian

The door to my father’s study is unlocked, the way it has been since Tuesday.

I could have changed that in an afternoon, with a new lock, a new key, one instruction to Silas. I’ve thought about it most days since and never once given the order, and tonight I understand why.

I left it open because he’s allowed in here, which was always true, and some part of me is still working off the old list.

The trap is a different thing. I built it this afternoon, and it happens to use this room.

Selene is on the bench in the west hall. I haven’t looked, and I haven’t listened. I know it the way I know my own skin is cold — it’s in the blood now, since her wrist, since the cut she put there herself: a low signal, steady as a wire, telling me where the warm thing is.

It’s new, and I haven’t told her about it.

He comes at the hour he always comes, and then he knocks, which he has never once had to do.

“Boy.”

“Come in.”

He looks tired, as he’s looked since the funeral. Nobody would believe that part if I said it out loud.

He crosses to the chair by the window and stops, because I’ve turned it back around to face the desk.

“You moved it?” he says.

“It was facing the wrong way. It has been for a long time.”

“Your father never sat facing the window.”

“No. He didn’t, God rest him.”

Cassian sits down in it anyway, rests his palms on his knees and waits. The silence goes on long enough to be a question, and he doesn’t fill it.

He’s the only man alive who doesn’t fill my silences. Why didn’t that tell me something a long time ago?

“How is Marguerite’s chest?” he asks. “She was rattling at the council.”

“She’s fine. Why do you always ask about her chest?”

“Because nobody else does. She works too hard, and she won’t be told.”

“Cassian.”

“Mm? You’ve got something on your desk, boy. Are you going to show me?”

I put the ledger on the desk between us.

Selene’s father kept it for years: every job, every client, every fee. One client never gave a name, only a mark, a little hooked figure Daniel Ward copied down again and again without ever understanding it.

It’s at the bottom of every private letter Cassian has ever sent me.

He looks at it for a while, then reaches out and turns it a few degrees, square to the edge of the desk, because he’s always straightened things.

“Where did you get this? No — don’t tell me.”

“Does it matter?”

“No.” He sits back. “No, I suppose not.”

“Say it again.”

“It doesn’t matter where you got it.” He almost smiles. “You’ve always done that, since you were small: made a man say it twice so he has to hear himself.”

“Did you kill my father?”

“Yes.”

The word goes into the room and nothing happens to the fucking room.

I had plans for this, a sequence of questions, an order to take them in, a way to walk him into it — and he takes the whole structure apart with one syllable.

Then he sits there and looks at me, very nearly patient.

“Ask me the rest,” he says. “You’ve earned the rest.”

“The wolves? Was that you too?”

“I told a boy on a shrimp boat that a translator was informing on his father.” A pause. “It was easier than it should have been.”

“The windows? The latches?”

“Mine. Somebody had to leave them unlatched,” he says, without weight. “Who looks at the man who arrives afterward?”

So there it is.

Wolves came through the gallery, and this city built a war out of that. And the gallery had a latch, and the latch had fingers on it. They belonged to a man with a key to every room in this building.

I’ve spent a month asking who let them in, and I asked him, twice.

He helped me look. He sat in that chair and went through the household with me, name by name. He was thorough about it, and he never once steered me wrong.

He didn’t have to, because there was nothing at the end of that list.

“You helped me search?” I say.

“You needed to search. Would you have believed anything else?”

“You gave me Thibault’s men, and the gallery roster.”

“I gave you everything that was true,” he says, without defending himself. “You’d have found the hole in it eventually, and you found it.”

“I found it because a dead translator wrote a shape in a book, you bastard.”

Cassian looks at the ledger again, and his jaw loosens, and it isn’t fear.

“Daniel Ward,” he says. “He asked good questions. I remember thinking so at the time.”

“The poison?” I say. “Was that you?”

“I never met the witch. There’s a broker between us.” He glances up. “He has a name and I’d rather you heard it from somebody who isn’t dying. Write it down tonight, because I won’t be able to say it again.”

“Why? Why, God damn you?”

And here it is.

He looks up at me and his face does what it has always done, the warmth coming up in it like milk on a stove. He’s about to be honest with me for the first time in my life.

“Because your father took my daughter’s head off in a courtyard while I asked him not to,” he says. “Because he was right to. Under the law. And that law is a thing we wrote to make ourselves legible to our food. We are the oldest predator that has ever lived, and we drafted a statute that makes us apologize for our mouths. He put the leash on his own throat and called it philosophy. Then he held me to it. I loved him, and I want that understood, and he was the most dangerous idea anyone has ever had about what we are.”

One paragraph, when he’s had sixty years to write it, and he keeps it that short.

Is he wrong? He isn’t, and I can’t put that down.

I made this argument myself, at this desk, with more heat in it and worse phrasing. My father heard me out and passed me the wine. I have no answer to it that isn’t borrowed from a dead man.

“She killed a woman for the pleasure of it, didn’t she?” I say.

“She did. Do you think I didn’t know?”

“You don’t say her name, do you?”

He stops.

It’s the only time tonight I get in front of him. His mouth opens and stays open, and he goes still, and for a moment there is nothing behind his face at all.

“No,” he says. “I don’t.”

“Why not?”

“How is your mother’s house in Toulouse?” He is looking at the window now. “Do you still keep it?”

“Cassian.”

“I ask because she chose the stone herself, and your father wanted brick.” A breath. “You should keep it.”

He won’t walk through that door. He’s had sixty years to brick it up and I’m not the one who gets it open tonight.

I don’t need it open. I need what’s already on the desk.

“Stand up,” I say. “God damn you, stand up.”

He stands.

He knows what this is. He’s known since he saw the chair turned around, and I’d put money on him knowing since Tuesday.

What he does is check his cuffs, one and then the other.

“Will you hear the charge?” I ask.

“Is there one? You’d give an old man a charge?”

“Under the Code: the killing of a king, the killing of a kinsman, a false witness used to start a war.” My voice is steady, which surprises me. “You have no recognized justification. Grief isn’t one, and being right isn’t one either.”

“No,” he agrees. “It isn’t.”

“Then the judgment is mine.”

“You’re not crowned. Does that bother you?”

“No.”

“Then doesn’t the council hold it?”

“The council held it while I looked for you.” I come around the desk. “And I found you.”

He takes that in, and then he nods, once, like a man agreeing to a change in a schedule.

“Do you want to fight me,” I say.

There’s no question mark in my voice and I don’t put one there.

“Would it help you? Tell me honestly.”

“Nothing about tonight fucking helps me.”

“No.”

“Then no.”

He unbuttons his jacket and folds it over the back of the chair. He does it neatly, because he has been neat his whole life.

“You’d have had me standing up, boy, without hurrying.” He smooths the collar flat. “I’ve known that since you were young, and I never said it to you. I’d like you to hear it while it’s still worth something.”

He’s older than me by lifetimes and it has never mattered, because age is the floor. It was never the ceiling.

He was never the threat. He was the threat because he was standing close.

“On your knees,” I say.

He kneels.

Then I open the door.

Silas is outside it with two of the court. They come in and stand where I put them, and nobody says a word.

“One more,” I say, and go out into the hall.

She’s on the bench at the turn, where Marguerite put her, gripping the edge of it.

“Selene.”

She looks up, and she’s already standing.

“The statute wants witnesses,” I tell her. “He killed your father too, and you have the right to be in the room. You don’t have the duty.”

She comes down the hall before I’ve finished the sentence and stops in the study doorway. Cassian turns his head where he kneels and sees her, and for the first time tonight something crosses his face that he didn’t put there himself.

“Miss Ward,” he says. “Have you come to watch?”

“Say his name.”

He knows which name. “Daniel Ward.”

“Again.”

It’s almost a laugh, if there were any air behind it. “You’ve been spending time with the boy.”

“Again.”

“Daniel Ward.” He holds her eyes. “He asked good questions.”

“He taught me to ask them.” She doesn’t come any closer, and she doesn’t need to. “You asked everybody in this city about their mothers. You never once asked me about mine.”

Cassian looks at her for a long moment, and then he lowers his eyes, the only thing I have ever seen him lower.

“Stay, my witness,” I tell her.

“I’m not going anywhere.”

There’s a protocol and the protocol exists for a reason: drained first, then the head, in front of witnesses, so that no one can ever say a king was murdered in a room with the door shut. My father wrote that, and he hated it.

He used it on this man’s daughter and he did it himself, because he wouldn’t hand the ugly part to somebody else.

The Bloodsteel is in my coat, where it’s been for weeks.

I take it out and put it on the desk where he can see it, and it stays there. It’s keyed to my father’s blood, and against Cassian it’s a kitchen knife.

It was only ever going to kill one man, and he saw to that.

I take him by the hair and tip his head back until his throat is bare.

Nearly seven hundred years of pulse under that skin, and I can see it — the vein, the rhythm, the whole long history of a man who kept his collar open because he had nothing to fear from the air.

Then I put my mouth on his throat, and this is not feeding. It is the opposite of feeding. God help me, it is the opposite.

When I feed, the blood comes easily, the body giving it up, a gate opening, a held thing released.

This is a taking, and his body fights me. Every cell of him resists — the blood comes thick and slow, and I have to pull for it. The pulling is the worst thing I have ever done with my mouth. My tongue is flat against the wound, and my throat works around every mouthful.

His blood tastes like my childhood, and I was not fucking prepared for that. It tastes like the courtyard and the blade and the evening light on stone, like the man who taught me to hold a weapon and poured me wine when my father wouldn’t, and it keeps coming. I swallow, and swallow again, and I don’t stop.

I’m shaking.

“Good boy,” he says.

“Don’t. For Christ’s sake, don’t.”

“Good boy.” His voice is going thin, the warmth in it leaving from the edges, like a lamp going out. His hand finds my wrist — the grip from the courtyard, from the funeral, from every evening he sat in this room and steadied me with a touch that was never a lie.

His skin is changing under my mouth. The warmth goes out of it first — he goes cold, and then past cold, into a cold that isn’t temperature anymore. His skin turns gray, not the gray of stone but the gray of paper left in the sun, thin, translucent. I can see the veins emptying under the surface, collapsing like tunnels with nothing left to hold them open.

I drink my uncle until he can’t hold his own head up.

I swallow and swallow. It takes a long time, longer than it takes with anyone, because there is so much of him and none of it wants to leave.

His heartbeat slows under my tongue, the spaces between beats getting longer. Each beat pushes less, and each pull takes more.

A sound comes out of him, a rattle, a chest that has forgotten how to fill, pushing the last of its air through a throat that’s already somewhere else.

By the end he’s on his side on the floor and his eyes are still working.

Draining doesn’t take the mind. My father knew that cruelty and used it anyway, on a girl he’d carried on his shoulders.

His face is a ruin of what it was, the skin pulled tight over the skull, the lips drawn back, the eyes sunken but alive. He looks like a saint’s body found in a wall — a man who has been still for centuries and is only now being discovered.

I kneel down next to him, and my knees go into the wet, and I don’t care.

“Was it only you?” I ask. “Cassian. Was it only you?”

His eyes come around to me, and it costs him something to do it.

“I meant it.” His voice is nothing, a shape made out of air. “Every part of it. Don’t let them tell you I was ill, or mad with grief, or led.”

“Cassian—”

“I chose.” His fingers move. “I want you certain of that.”

“I’m certain. Christ, I’m certain.”

“Good.” His mouth softens, tenderness, maybe, or the last of it. “Good boy.”

“Was it only you?”

The pause goes on so long I think he’s gone.

Then his mouth works. “No.”

“Who? Tell me who, you bastard.”

His fingers come up off the floor and find my wrist.

It’s the grip from the funeral, and from the courtyard before that, when I was a boy: a man steadying someone who stumbled into him.

“Verity,” he says.

The truth.

He’s telling me he’s given me the truth of it. It’s an old man’s word for it, and it’s the last one he has.

What’s the rest of the sentence? I wait for it, and it doesn’t come.

I sit on the floor of my father’s study with my uncle’s blood soaking through my knees, and his grip goes loose around my wrist.

I have been given an answer. What is it? I don’t know, and I stand up.

My knees are wet, and my mouth is wet. I can taste him: age, and dust, and underneath both, the taste of the courtyard, like the blade lessons, like a man saying again until my wrist stopped hurting.

The Bloodsteel is on the desk. It’s been on the desk the whole time — my father’s blade, keyed to my father’s blood, useless for this.

I don’t use it. I take the other blade from my coat, the short one, plain steel, that Silas sharpened this morning without asking why.

Cassian is on the floor with his eyes open, watching me.

The head comes off after the draining. That’s the protocol, and I keep to it.

Silas is behind me, and the Roche brothers are at the wall. Selene is in the doorway, and she hasn’t looked away once, and nobody speaks.

I kneel back down and put one hand on Cassian’s shoulder — the same shoulder I gripped at the funeral, when I was trying to stay upright and he was the only solid thing in the room.

“Close your eyes,” I say.

“No.” His voice is nothing — air pushed through a throat that has no blood left to warm it. “I’d like to see you do it.”

I hold the blade against his neck. The steel is cold, and his skin is colder.

The blade shakes, once.

Then it doesn’t.

The cut is clean, one stroke, through the muscle and the spine. The resistance is less than I expected — his body has so little left in it that the blade goes through almost gently.

His head comes away from his shoulders and his eyes are still open and they are still looking at me when it happens, and the last thing in them is not fear.

It’s pride.

The body settles, and the blood that’s left — not much, a thin dark seep — pools on the floor around my knees.

Silas steps forward and looks at the body, looks at the head, looks at me.

“The hour is nine-fourteen,” he says, as he says everything. Then he takes out a cloth and lays it over Cassian’s face, and the small decency of that nearly takes my legs out.

The word stays in the room after he’s gone out of it.

I sit down on the fucking floor without deciding to.

After a while, Silas clears his throat, which he has never done, not once in two hundred years. The sound is so wrong coming out of that man that I look up.

He’s holding a paper, folded once, with a line written on the front in his small square handwriting.

“When you’re able, sire.”

I hold out my hand, and he puts the paper in it. I don’t unfold it, and I don’t read it. I pick up Cassian’s pen from the desk, still warm from the dead man’s coat, and I sign across the fold.

Silas takes it back and puts it inside his coat, and steps back to the wall.

The doorway is empty, and I didn’t see her go.

I’ll read it tomorrow, though I know already. Silas Roche has asked me for one thing in two hundred years — a week — and whatever he’s spending now, he’s spending on the woman in the house on neutral ground.

I find Isabeau in the front hall.

She’s standing at the bottom of the stairs with a coat she hasn’t put on and a face that knows everything and is choosing what to show me.

How do you tell a woman you’ve killed her father? Yourself: no messenger, no Silas, no note delivered to a house she keeps a few streets from here. I stand in the hall with her father’s blood on my shirt and I say the sentence.

“Thank you,” she says. “For telling me yourself.”

It’s courtesy, not grief. She said goodbye to her father years ago, when he stopped seeing her, which was long before he stopped seeing anyone else.

She puts on the coat and buttons it with fingers that don’t shake.

“He loved you,” she says. “I want you to know that. The love was real, and it was always real.”

“I know.”

“That’s the worst part.”

“Yes.”

She goes out the front door without looking at the study. The coat she chose has a collar that covers the back of her neck. I think she chose it for exactly that reason.

Selene is in the west wing, on the bed, not the floor, sitting upright, with the ledger closed on the table and her fingers laced in her lap, a woman who watched every second of it from a doorway and is deciding what to do with the seeing.

She sees me, and she doesn’t flinch at the blood.

“Marguerite told me to make you eat,” she says.

“I’m not hungry. How could I be?”

“I know. She said you’d say that.”

“What do you need?” she says. “Tell me. Anything.”

“I need you to not leave this room, ghost. For God’s sake, don’t leave.”

“I’m not leaving. Where the hell would I go?”

I stand in the doorway. My father’s study is behind me, down the hall, being cleaned by men who will never speak about tonight, and Cassian’s body is being taken out through the east passage, wrapped in a sheet Silas chose, carried by people who don’t know what they’re carrying.

“I killed the man who raised me tonight,” I say. “I did it correctly, under the law, in the room my father built. I drained him on the floor, and I took his head with a blade Silas sharpened this morning.”

“I know.”

“I did it under the Code, by the protocol, in front of witnesses, with the door open, the way my father did it to his daughter sixty years ago.”

“Lucian—”

“And if he had touched you—” My voice changes. What was in the dining room comes into it, and the pantry door, and I can hear it and I cannot stop it. “If any part of this had pointed at you — if Cassian had sent a wolf to your apartment instead of to a translator’s house — I would have done it in the fucking street with my hands and I would not have used the law and I would not have been sorry and God help anyone who got between me and the door.”

The room is very still, and she doesn’t move.

“That’s the truth of me,” I say. “The law and the animal. I used the law tonight because my father was right, but for you I would have been the thing the law was written to prevent.”

She stands up and crosses the room and puts her palms flat on my face, like she held me on the floor two nights ago, and she looks at me with both registers running.

“I know,” she says. “I’ve always known. That’s why I’m here.”

She doesn’t take me to bed. She takes me to the washstand.

She fills the basin and wets a cloth and washes my hands, one and then the other, as you’d wash something you meant to keep. The water goes pink, then red, and she empties it and fills it again. She doesn’t say anything, and she doesn’t hurry, and she doesn’t look away from what comes off my skin.

When my hands are clean she washes my mouth, the cloth at my lip, her thumb on my tongue, as if she can wipe the taste of him off it.

That’s where it breaks, not in the study but here, with a warm cloth at the corner of my lip and her thumb holding my jaw still. It comes up out of me with no sound at all, and my shoulders go. She pulls my head down against her neck and holds it there while I shake.

“Stay here,” she says, “with me.”

“I’m here.”

“So am I. I’m not going anywhere, you hear me? Not a fucking inch.”

We end up on the floor with our backs against the bed, because neither of us makes it the last three feet. She keeps my hands in her lap, and my fingers are still shaking. She covers them with hers and holds them still.

She says something I don’t catch. It comes out in one language — not wolf, not English, not the court register she’s been working in since she walked through my door — just a word, hers.

I hold onto it, and I hold onto her, and it’s the quietest hour this house has had since Tuesday.

After.

Sometime before dawn we get into the bed, her on my chest and my palm on her back. The window is dark and the house is still and Silas is somewhere in the building writing things down in a script I’ll never be able to read.

“Your father’s Code,” she says.

“What about it? Tonight of all nights?”

“It’s not a philosophy. It’s a fence.” She traces a line on my chest. “You build it because the thing on the other side of it is you.”

“You told me that once before.”

“I’m saying it again.” She lifts her head. “Because that night you heard it about your father. Tonight I need you to hear it about yourself.”

“I hear it. Do you believe I hear it?”

“The fence held tonight, Lucian. You used the law, and you did it correctly. And the man who told you the law was a leash is the man who proved it wasn’t.”

I look at the ceiling, and the house settles around us — the small sounds of a building that has lost a heavy man and is finding its new weight.

“He was proud of me,” I say.

“I know.”

“He taught me the hands I used to kill him and he died proud that I used them well.” I close my eyes. “I’m going to carry that, and I’m not going to put it down.”

She puts her face back on my chest, her finger staying on the line she was tracing.

“Then carry it,” she says. “I’ll be here.”

His last word is still in the room, and tonight I let it stay there.

Tonight the door is open and the chair is turned around and the man who sat in it is gone. His coat is still on the back of it. His coat is smooth, because he was neat his whole life.

I don’t move her, and that’s how I know.
