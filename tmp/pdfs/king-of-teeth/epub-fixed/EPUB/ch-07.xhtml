Chapter Seven

lucian

The wolf has been waiting to be killed for two days, and he’s stopped expecting it.

I watch it happen in his body: the shoulders drop first, then the jaw, and then the eyes start looking at things in the room instead of at the room.

He’s looking at the lamp, and he’s been looking at it for a while.

The east basement is a door, a chair and nothing else, except the lamp Silas brought down on the first night, which I didn’t order removed. A man who can see the room he’s in knows he’s being held by someone who thought of his eyes.

His name is Thibault, a dock worker, and one of the wolves who came through the gallery windows the night my father died.

I’ve left him alone to think about the difference between being killed and being kept.

I come in and shut the door behind me.

He looks up.

“I thought you forgot about me,” he says. “Did you?”

“No.”

“That’s worse, probably.”

“Probably. Are you disappointed?”

“Hell, I don’t know anymore.”

I take the second chair from the corner and sit across from him at a distance that says conversation, not interrogation. He notices, because stupid wolves don’t survive to forty-seven in a pack that shares a city with us.

“You’ve been here a while,” I say. “You’ve been fed, and you have a light.”

“I noticed.”

“Do you know why?”

“Because you want something from me that hitting wouldn’t get. I’m not a genius, but I’m not a fucking fool either.”

There it is. Not stupid at all.

I pick up the lamp, cast iron at the base, heavy, old, the kind of thing a man would need both arms to carry.

I close one hand around it and squeeze, and the iron deforms under my fingers. It folds inward with the sound of a can being crushed, and when I set it back down, the base has the shape of my grip pressed into it.

The lamp stays on, and the light doesn’t even flicker. Did he think it would?

Thibault looks at the lamp, and then at my hand, the hand that just reshaped iron like clay.

“That’s not a threat,” I say. “That’s a fact about the room you’re in. The threat is outside the room, and it wears a council’s face, and it has been asking me to give you to it since the boat came in.”

He swallows.

“Tell me about the chamber,” I say. “Start from the windows.”

He tells it, and stops now and then to check what he saw against what he was told he saw.

“We came through fast, because the whole point was speed: get in, get to the platform, do the damage, get out.”

“Who gave the order?”

“Gaspard. Who the hell else?” No hesitation. “The Alpha. The idea was chaos, enough of us at once that nobody could organize.” His jaw sets. “For about a minute. Then your father stood up.”

He says it like then the weather changed.

“He was at the table when we came in, sitting. I saw him from the gallery rail, and he had a glass in front of him and his hand was on the arm of the chair, and he didn’t move.”

“He didn’t move?”

“Then he stood up, and it was over.”

“What do you mean, over?”

“I mean the ones who were close to him stopped being close to him.” A pause. “The ones who were closest stopped being anything.”

I’ve read Marguerite’s account and Cassian’s and three Elders’, and none of them told it like this. Why not?

“He was beautiful,” Thibault says.

It goes into the room and sits down.

“I’m a wolf. I’m supposed to say he was a monster, or that I was frightened, and I was frightened, but frightened isn’t the right name for it.” He looks at me. “A man that old, moving like that. I’d never seen anything like it. None of us had.”

“Keep going. Don’t you dare stop there.”

“The boys in front were done, and the rest of us went for the windows. Your father was still standing. He’d taken bites, both arms, one at the hip, and I watched a wound on his forearm close up in the time it took me to reach the rail.”

“Then what the hell killed him?”

Thibault stops.

This is the part, and I’ve been waiting for it. He’s been waiting to be asked, and we’ve both known it was coming since the dock.

“He was still standing when we quit,” Thibault says. “He was standing, and he was healing, and the last of our people were going back through the windows. It was over.”

“And?”

“And he turned.”

“Which direction?”

“Away from us.” The wolf’s voice has gone careful. “Toward the east side of the chamber, toward someone behind him.”

“Someone?”

“I couldn’t see — I was on the gallery rail, going out. But I saw his face.”

“What was in it?”

“Surprise,” Thibault says, and his eyes are steady. “He looked at somebody behind him like you look at a man who’s come through a door he’s allowed to come through, and then he looked down, and I went through the window.”

The room has no sound in it but the hum of the lamp.

“You saw him turn toward someone,” I say. “Behind him, on the east side?”

“Yes.”

“Not a wolf?”

“Our people were done, and we were out or we were dead. There was nobody behind him in a wolf’s body.” He stops. “And the smell.”

“What smell?”

“I can’t be sure, because there was blood everywhere and it was hard to sort. But I’ve been in rooms with your kind before, and there’s a specific thing. Old ones have more of it.”

“And you caught that?”

“At the turn, when he turned.” Thibault rubs his jaw. “It was close to him, not across the room. Right there.”

I sit with that for a while. What else can I do with it?

The fight was over. My father turned toward someone he wasn’t afraid of, someone he expected to be there, and that someone put him down.

The scent was wrong for a wolf.

One of ours.

I’ve been asking who was in the room since Tuesday, and I’ve been asking it about the wrong door.

“You’re wondering whether to kill me,” he says.

“Yes.”

“Figured. Hell, I’d wonder too.” He doesn’t look away. “I’d like to make a case for not.”

“Go ahead. Make it a good one.”

“Nobody in your court was looking at your father when it happened, because they were looking at us. And the wolves who got close enough to see are dead.” He holds my eyes. “If you kill me, it goes away. If you don’t, you have a witness with no stake in the answer.”

I could kill him, and nobody would question it.

But a dead wolf tells one story, and a live wolf tells the one I choose.

“We didn’t kill your father,” Thibault says.

The sentence goes through me as clean as a blade drawn across a nerve.

It’s the we. A wolf in a vampire’s basement, using a word that means my people in his language and the enemy in mine.

“We didn’t kill your father,” he says again. “He was still standing when we quit, and he was winning. Whatever happened after that, whatever put him down, it wasn’t us.”

I look at this man for a long time. What do I owe him for that?

Then I stand up, and I pick up the second chair, and I put it back in the corner.

“Silas will bring you food,” I say.

“Am I staying?”

“You’re staying. Is that a problem?”

“Beats the fuck out of the alternative.”

“For how long?”

I don’t answer that. I go to the door and open it, and the hallway is cold and empty and stretches in both directions, and somewhere at the top of this house there’s a room with a desk in it and a question I’ve been asking wrong.

I don’t kill him.

Upstairs, in my father’s desk, is the list Silas brought me the night I came home. Every name that was inside that room.

I’ve been reading it for the wolves.

Tonight I read it for everyone else.
